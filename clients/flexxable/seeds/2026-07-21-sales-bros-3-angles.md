## THE MECHANISM (verbatim from Travis, don't paraphrase away from this)

**The question:** *"How many people really think they're good salespeople? Put 1,000 people in a room — maybe 4, 5, 10 think 'I'm a good salesperson.'"*

**The Old Plan (what everyone else pitches):**
- Build the AI/vibe-code the tool first, before you have a customer for it
- Compete with 5 other AI automation agencies
- Submit proposals, submit bids, go through the negotiation gauntlet, 4 Zoom calls
- MAYBE get the deal
- Then you've got a mountain of s*** to deliver
- Needs 2-3 years of credibility, case studies, testimonials
- Only works if you love selling and are great at it

**The New Plan (the ROYA way):**
- Demo first — "a demo's worth a thousand pictures"
- Propose a risk-free test (Travis's "RFP" — risk-free proposal), commission-only, no upfront
- "I'm not asking you to trust me — let's just run a test"
- When the money floods in, split it
- They ask YOU how else they can work together
- Low delivery hassle, and NOW you've got a customer who'll buy anything else you build

**The forked road (Travis's actual close line — good transition template):**
*"You've got a few choices. One, you keep building AI before you have a customer for it, keep competing with agencies asking five or six figures upfront, wait two or three years to build up credibility... if you love selling and you're great at it, that's a great option for you. Or — you just have a demo. They see the proof up front. The demo and them getting results is what sells them. Then you've got a customer, and once they get results, they'll buy everything else from you too."*

## ANGLE 1 — "Sales Bros Are Dead" (the direct declaration — Dan's own steer)

Entry point: obituary / blunt statement, not a question. Leads with the claim, not the pain.

> **Sales bros are dead.**
>
> Not the people. The whole idea that you need to BE one.
>
> For years the rule for closing AI clients was: sound confident, know your objection-handling, close like a killer.
>
> Turns out… none of that's true anymore.
>
> [TRANSITION] Here's the bit nobody in the AI space wants to admit…

Transition line into mechanism:
> Put 1,000 people in a room and ask who thinks they're a good salesperson.
> Maybe 10 hands go up.
> Everyone else? They're the ones sat at home not starting an agency because they "can't sell."
> So we stopped asking them to sell.

Then run Old Plan / New Plan fork, close on demo + risk-free test + commission split.

**Why this works:** it's Dan's own instinct — declaration hook (no pain, no question), matches "lead with a promise/novelty not pain" rule. Best for cold/unaware traffic that's never heard the mechanism.

## ANGLE 2 — "The 4-in-1,000 Confession" (self-identification, uses Travis's stat verbatim)

Entry point: direct-address question that makes the reader locate themselves in the room Travis described. More personal/confessional than Angle 1.

> Quick one.
>
> If you put 1,000 people in a room and asked "who thinks they're a good salesperson?"…
>
> Maybe 10 hands go up.
>
> Be honest. Would yours be one of them?
>
> If not — good. You're exactly who this is for.
>
> [TRANSITION] Because here's what nobody selling "AI agency" courses will tell you…

Transition line into mechanism:
> The reason 990 of those people never start an agency isn't the tech.
> It's that somewhere they decided "closing clients" = "being a sales bro."
> So they never even try.
> We built the whole thing so you never have to be one.

Then Old Plan / New Plan fork — same as Angle 1, but keep it framed around "you" self-doubt rather than a market-wide declaration. Close with Harry's proof line (approved): *"He didn't have to channel his inner sales bro and become a hardcore closer. He just sent a demo. AI sold itself."*

**Why this works:** uses Travis's own number, which makes the ad "his words" per Dan's brief. Good for the self-doubt / "I'm not a salesperson" objection Travis flagged as the real unspoken block.

## ANGLE 3 — "The Old Plan Is a Trap" (contrarian expose — calls out what every other agency/vibe-coder is doing)

Entry point: pattern-interrupt / contrarian claim about the *industry*, not the reader. Best against warmer traffic that's already looked at AI agency or vibe-coding options and is comparing.

> Every AI agency guru is teaching you the same broken plan.
>
> Build the tool.
> Write the proposal.
> Compete with five other agencies quoting the same client.
> Sit through four Zoom calls.
> MAYBE get the deal.
> Then deliver a mountain of work for it.
>
> That's not an agency. That's a second job you have to win a beauty pageant to get.
>
> [TRANSITION] There's a reason we never do any of that…

Transition line into mechanism:
> We don't pitch. We demo.
> We don't ask for $25k upfront or a $3k/mo retainer.
> We ask for a risk-free test — commission only.
> No sales skills required, because the demo does the selling.
> When it works, THEY ask us how else they can work together.

Then close on the fork: *"You can spend 2-3 years building credibility and duking it out with sales bros for the deal. Or you can demo, let the results sell it, and skip straight to being the one they call first."*

**Why this works:** matches the "contrarian" gap already mapped in [[strategy-map]] (gap 3), and gives the sharpest, most "obvious" version of Travis's point per Dan's brief ("the way he puts it's just so clear and obvious"). Best for anyone who's already priced out another AI agency course/JV and felt the friction Travis described.

## ANGLE 4 — "Sales Bros Are Obsolete" (controversial / us-vs-them — Dan's add, 21 Jul)

Entry point: direct call-out, picks a fight with "sales bros" as a category, not a person. Loudest, most polarising of the four — designed to make sales-bro types comment/defend themselves (engagement) while the target audience (non-salespeople) feels validated.

**On the swearing ask:** skip actual/censored profanity — Meta's ad policy flags it for rejection or limited delivery, and it doesn't buy us anything the existing voice can't. The account's own precedent ([[flexxable-winning-ads]] — "impress the crap out of") shows mild crude language already works without the filter risk. Use "crap," "BS," "screw [the old way]" for the same punch.

> Sales bros, look away.
>
> Because we just made you **obsolete.**
>
> For years, closing AI clients meant being one of you.
> Confident. Slick. Great on the phone. Built for the chase.
>
> Not anymore.
>
> [TRANSITION] Here's the bit that's gonna piss some people off…

Transition line into mechanism:
> The tech guys — the ones who'd never call themselves salespeople — are now closing MORE AI clients than the sales bros.
> Not despite skipping the pitch. BECAUSE they skipped it.
> No script. No "objection handling." No convincey sales calls.
> Just a demo that does the closing for them.

Then run Old Plan / New Plan fork with sharper, more combative framing on the Old Plan side:
> The Old Plan is the sales bro's whole game: sound better than the other guy, out-pitch 5 competing agencies, survive the negotiation gauntlet, THEN spend months delivering a mountain of work.
> We skipped all of it.
> Demo. Risk-free test. Commission only. Let the results talk.
> And now the "tech guys" who can't sell to save their life are outselling the sales bros.

Close options (pick one, don't stack):
> That's not an accident. That's the whole model.
—or—
> Sales bros built their whole identity on being good at something we made irrelevant.

**Why this works:** directly answers Dan's brief — "we ARE better than them" as a claim, not an implication. Best for retargeting/warm audiences and for stirring comments (both from the target segment agreeing, and from actual sales-bro types arguing back — either way, engagement). Riskiest of the 4 on tone; test against a smaller budget slice first and watch comment sentiment before scaling.

**Caution:** this is the only angle of the four making a direct superiority claim ("we ARE better than them") rather than a mechanism claim ("you don't need to be one of them"). Legally/reputationally safer to keep the claim about the OUTCOME (tech guys are closing more) rather than a blanket insult of salespeople as a group — keeps it "we found a better way" not "sales bros are bad people."
