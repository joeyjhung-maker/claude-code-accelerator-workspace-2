# Flexxable Skool Headlines Bank

*Banked 2026-08-20. Source: 37 posts from Dan Wardrope's ROYA Skool group (screenshots in
`~/Downloads/Skool Headlines/`, Feb–Jun 2026, plus one pinned member post from Rob Brown).
Ranked by comments — **structures, not content** — the shape is what gets reused, the exact
wording is always original.*

**Read this before writing any Dan Skool headline. Cross-reference with the headline rules
already banked in [[copy-rubric]] under "Dan Skool posts — round 3" (plain-over-clever, name the
mechanism, hedge the absolute claim).**

## The confounds — read this before trusting the ranking

Joey flagged this correctly: comment count is not a clean headline scorecard. Four mechanics
inflate comments independent of how good the headline is —

1. **Gated/conditional resources.** "...if You PROMISE to Send it" (#1, 629 comments) forces a
   comment to unlock the asset. That's a mechanic bolted onto the post, not proof the headline
   itself is 18x better than everything else.
2. **Direct favor-asks.** "(can you test for me?)" (#6, 201 comments) is a personal request —
   people reply because Dan asked them to, not because the headline pulled them in.
3. **Polls.** Several posts (#12, #13, #22, #29) attach a poll. Poll votes are counted
   separately from comments, and in at least one case (#29, 44 poll votes vs only 7 comments) the
   poll appears to have **absorbed** engagement that might otherwise have been a comment.
4. **Launch/logistics posts** (#17, #28, #30) get replies about access and timing almost
   regardless of the headline — the urgency is structural (a webinar starting in hours), not
   written.

**Read every row below with this in mind.** A high comment count paired with a gate/ask/poll/
launch tag is a mechanic winning, not necessarily the headline. The purest headline-only
comparisons are the posts WITHOUT any of these — that's where the real patterns below are drawn
from primarily (#2, #7, #8, #9, #11, #16, and most of the low performers).

**Likes and comments are different axes, not one score.** Proof and value posts pull likes
without needing comments (Ghosted template #24: 34 likes/8 comments; "waiter mentality" #32: 21
likes/5 comments; Show Up Rate hack #27: 29 likes/8 comments — people save and move on).
Open questions, irony, and asks pull comments even on modest likes (Steal from ManyChat #8: 26
likes/172 comments). **Decide which job this post has before writing the headline** — appreciation
(optimize for likes) or discussion (optimize for comments) — they're different headlines.

## Length

Measured all 37 headlines (character count, whitespace-normal). **Range: 21–80 characters.
Median: 50. Nothing in the dataset has ever run past 80.**

- Top-9 by comments: median **55 chars**, average 58.9, average 10.8 words.
- Bottom-9 by comments: median **43 chars**, average 51, average 9.8 words.
- Middle 19: median 49, average 47.3 — the shortest group of the three, sitting in the middle of
  the results.

**Length itself does not predict engagement inside the tested range** — the top performers are
if anything slightly longer than average, so "shorter wins" is not a supportable claim from this
data. What matters is a hard ceiling: **stay inside roughly 45–65 characters (the top-9 band),
and never go past ~80.** A headline drafted at 100+ characters isn't testing a "longer" hypothesis
against this dataset — it's simply outside anything that's ever been run in this group, proven or
not. Cut to the tested range first, then let content (not padding) fight for the words that remain.

## Winning skeletons

### 1. The Declared Mission
**Best performer by likes (104) AND second-best by comments (538) — no gate, no poll, no ask.**

> I'm gonna [bold verb] "[named problem/mechanism]" in [year] — Here's my [playful modifier] plan

**Evidence:** "I'm gonna crack 'Cold Outreach Automation' in 2026 - Here's my Sexy AF plan" — 104
likes, 538 comments.

**Mechanism:** First-person commitment, not a tip or an announcement — Dan is staking a claim in
public, which reads as stakes-having rather than teaching. The quoted term names the specific
problem in industry jargon (specificity a reader recognizes). The playful modifier ("Sexy AF")
does personality work without becoming a personality-post — it's one word doing double duty.
**Status: use this shape often. It's the single cleanest win in the dataset.**

---

### 2. The Ironic Quoted Flip
**150 comments, no gate/poll/ask.**

> From "[dismissive/negative quote]" to "[surprising positive quote]"

**Evidence (WON):** "From 'Not Interested' to 'What's Your Commission?'" — 32 likes, 150 comments.
**Evidence (DIED):** "From 'reply' to 'coffee date' skillz" — 4 likes, 0 comments.

**Mechanism:** The format only works when the two poles are genuinely ironic or surprising — a
rejection flipping into eager self-interest is a twist. "Reply" to "coffee date" is just
sequential progress, the reader already expects it, no tension. **Rule: this skeleton needs a
REVERSAL, not a step-up. If the second quote is the obvious next stage of the first, don't use
this shape — it'll read as a training-module title and die.**

---

### 3. The Open Contrast (unresolved why)
**46 comments, no gate/poll/ask — a clean mid-performer.**

> Why some [peeps] [win] in [short timeframe]… while others [fail] in [long timeframe]

**Evidence:** "Why some peeps close AI deals in days… while others can't land one in months" — 15
likes, 46 comments.

**Mechanism:** States the gap, refuses to name the cause. Compare directly to #36 below — this
skeleton dies the moment the headline answers its own question.

---

### 4. Steal From a Named Source
**172 comments, no gate/poll/ask.**

> Steal $[X] clients from [named platform/competitor] (and [payoff] doing it)

**Evidence:** "Steal $16K/m clients from ManyChat (and bank a fat retainer doing it)" — 26 likes,
172 comments.

**Mechanism:** "Steal" is an aggressive, slightly transgressive verb — it implies the reader is
getting away with something. Naming a real, recognizable platform (ManyChat) makes the target
concrete instead of abstract ("competitors"). The parenthetical adds the payoff in the same
breath, matching the existing "benefit needs its WHY in the same breath" rubric rule.

---

### 5. Specific $ + Specific Timeframe + Identity
**194 comments, 243 likes — the highest LIKE count in the entire dataset, by a wide margin.**

> £[specific number] per month [outcome] [specific timeframe] after [identity-defining action]

**Evidence:** "£20k per month profit 6 weeks after leaving my 9 to 5…" (Rob Brown, member post,
pinned) — 243 likes, 194 comments.

**Mechanism:** Two specific numbers (not rounded), one identity beat ("leaving my 9 to 5") that
the whole target audience recognizes as their own dream. Note this is a MEMBER'S post, not
Dan's — proof from inside the community reads as less self-promotional and pulls even harder than
Dan's own proof posts. **If B2B Leads Lab gets a strong member result, this is the shape to use for
it, and it should be posted or amplified as the member's own words, not paraphrased by Dan.**

---

### 6. Named-Asset + Status Word
Appears across several mid-to-high performers: "Double Banger" Lead Magnet **LIVE**, **HULK
REPLAY**, Sales Wingman **Lite**, DBR. Naming the mechanism as a proper noun (matches the existing
"Big 4" / "Dream 100" naming-device rule already banked) plus a status word — LIVE, REPLAY, FREE —
signals "this exists right now, not someday." Best paired with an actual gate or genuine urgency
(#1, #5, #6) — as a bare announcement with no urgency (#30, [REPLAY]... WATCH — 5 comments) it's
much weaker.

## Anti-patterns — what reliably underperforms

### The self-resolving question
**1 comment, 16 likes.**

> "[Question]?" - Here's why that's the WRONG question

**Evidence:** "'Is their database big enough?' - Here's why that's the WRONG question" — 16
likes, 1 comment.

**Why it dies:** Directly contradicts skeleton #3 above. The headline answers its own curiosity
gap — it tells the reader the premise is wrong before they've had a chance to wonder. There's
nothing left to discuss; the post has to be read for the answer, not argued about in the comments.
**Rule: never resolve the loop in the headline. State the gap, withhold the cause.**

### The diary update
**3 comments despite 18 likes.**

> Day [N] "[Name]" [product] - We have a new [milestone]!!

**Evidence:** "Day 6 'Amy' VoiceAI - We have a new Buyer!!" — 18 likes, 3 comments.

**Why it dies:** Reads as a personal log entry. Exclamation marks and a milestone don't create an
opening for the reader — there's no question posed, nothing to weigh in on. Good for likes
(people are happy for Dan), useless for discussion.

### The abstract metaphor with no stakes
**3 comments, 11 likes.**

> Is your [thing] a [metaphor] with no [metaphor payoff]?

**Evidence:** "Is your AI bot a Ferrari with no key?" — 11 likes, 3 comments.

**Why it dies:** Clever, but nothing concrete attaches to it — no number, no name, no real
scenario. Compare to the winning skeletons above, all of which anchor to a specific figure,
platform, or quote. A metaphor alone isn't enough load-bearing weight for a headline.

### The big number with no question attached
**5 comments despite a genuinely impressive number.**

> [Big number] [unit] PER DAY Client Closed. IN JUST [short timeframe].

**Evidence:** "300 Leads PER DAY Client Closed. IN JUST 10 DAYS." — 15 likes, 5 comments.

**Why it dies:** Confirms the likes-vs-comments split directly — an impressive number alone reads
as a proof/celebration post. Readers acknowledge it (mild likes) but there's no reason to reply.
**If the goal is comments, the big number needs a hook onto the READER's situation ("what if this
was you"), a question, or a "here's how" open loop — not just a standalone stat.**

### The congratulations post
**10 comments, 22 likes.**

> $[X]/m Retainer - [Mechanism] + [Mechanism]

**Evidence:** "$10K/m Retainer - DBR + Upsells" (body opens "Well done Jeff!!") — 22 likes, 10
comments.

**Why it dies:** Praising a named member is warm but closes the loop immediately — the reader's
only response option is a thumbs up. No question, no gap, no "could this be me."

### Pure logistics
**Consistently under 10 comments across every instance in the dataset** (Monday 3PM webinar
announcement, T-minus 4 hours launch post, [REPLAY]... WATCH). Necessary posts, but don't expect
them to carry discussion weight — they're doing a scheduling job, not a persuasion job.

## Quick-reference checklist for the next Dan Skool headline

1. **Stay inside 45–65 characters, hard ceiling ~80.** Nothing in the tested dataset has ever run
   longer — draft past that and you're outside proven territory, not testing a "long headline."
2. **Decide the job first: likes (appreciation/proof) or comments (discussion)?** Different
   headline, not the same headline dialed up.
2. **If the goal is comments: state the gap, don't resolve it.** Never write "here's why X is
   wrong" — write "why does X happen" and stop.
3. **If using a quoted before/after, the two poles must be ironic, not sequential.** "Not
   interested → what's your commission" works. "Reply → coffee date" doesn't.
4. **A number alone won't carry comments.** Pair it with a question, an ask, or a "could this be
   you" bridge — otherwise it's a proof post, not a discussion post.
5. **Name the mechanism as a proper noun when there's a repeatable asset** — Sales Wingman,
   Dream 100, Big 4, AI Enriched Lumpy Mail. One status word (LIVE, REPLAY, FREE) on top of it.
6. **A real gate, favor-ask, or poll will inflate comments — know that you're doing it.** Fine as
   a deliberate tactic, but don't credit the headline for what the mechanic did.
7. **"Steal" / aggressive verb + a named real competitor** beats a vague "competitors" framing.
8. **Member proof (specific $ + specific timeframe + identity line) outperforms Dan's own proof
   posts on likes** — when B2B Leads Lab gets a strong result from inside the group, let the member's
   own words carry it.
