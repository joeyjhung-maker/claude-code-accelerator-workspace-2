## 1. The offer, in one line

**[Doc/Campaign]** ROYA = **"Rent Out Your Android**s**"** (plural — confirmed by Joey; the offer doc's cover text says "Rent Out Your Android" singular, the book's Part 3 header also says singular "Rent Out Your Sales Android." Plural is the correct/current expansion for the coaching program name — use "Androids" going forward, but don't be surprised seeing the singular in older source material.) A coaching program teaching people to get control of a business's dead lead database + place AI "Sales Androids" inside it, splitting new revenue 50/50 — with zero upfront cost to the business.

## 2. The core positioning

**[Doc]** **Not selling AI. Selling "more customers with ZERO risk."** McDonald's analogy: *"McDonald's isn't in the burger business, it's in real estate"* → *"We're not in the AI business, we're selling more customers with zero risk."* AI is just the tool that does it better/faster/safer. Lets you charge **$20k+ per deal instead of $197/mo**, and insulates the offer from AI-tech commoditization.

**[Book] Tension worth knowing:** the 2024 book's framing is the literal opposite — *"It's not the ones who use AI that will be rich... but the ones who sell the AI shovels."* Great gold-rush narrative color (own the "sell the shovel, not the gold" metaphor), but don't let it leak into the actual pitch — the 2026 doc's "not selling AI" position is the more evolved, defensible one.

**[Book] Certainty is the real product.** *"When they part with their hard-earned cash, they want certainty."* The argument for why a SYSTEM (AI) out-sells a PERSON (agency): systems are consistent, people aren't. Underpins the whole "you don't need sales skills, the demo sells itself" claim.

**[Book] The "2 bleeding-neck pain points."** AI simultaneously cuts costs (replaces/frees staff) AND grows revenue (converts more) — the twin lever that makes the offer a no-brainer in a squeezed economy.

**[Book] "Sell first, build later."** Closing mantra of the book — you don't need the tech built to start pitching.

## 3. The mechanism

**[Doc] The 3-step version (client-facing):**
1. **Make an offer biz owners feel stupid saying no to** — take their old/dead leads, zero upfront, split new sales 50/50.
2. **Fire up the Sales Android** — Sleeping Beauty Android + Prince Charming SMS wakes the database, brings in sales without working a single lead yourself.
3. **Profit** — $10k–$100k+ in sales.

**[Book] DBR = "Database Reactivation."** The formal acronym/term-of-art for the whole mechanism — good for insider-credibility copy ("we run DBR campaigns" reads more expert than "we reactivate leads").

**[Book] The 4-Step Partner-Getting Process (internal/teachable version):** Pitch → Coffee Date → Demo → Agreement. Distinct from the 3-step client pitch above — this is the seller's own funnel.

**[Book] The demo is deliberately lightweight.** It's a raw OpenAI Playground session with a canned prompt, screen-shared live on the Zoom coffee date — NOT a real deployed bot. Useful to know when writing "how it actually works" copy so you don't overpromise on what happens before a contract is signed.

## 4. Client vs. Partner (and other core reframes)

**[Book] "Client" vs. "Partner"** — not just a word swap, a full argument: a "client" is an expense line item, makes you an employee ("10 bosses"); a "partner" is equal standing, shares risk AND upside. Good raw material for objection-handling/positioning copy.

**[Book] Controlling Assets vs. Owning Assets.** Formula: **Cash + Growth = Wealth**. You don't need to OWN a company's database to profit from it — you just need CONTROL. Reframes the whole offer as a "30-year wealth shortcut" — get the cash-flow benefit of an asset without the capital, time, or ownership risk of building/buying one.

**[Book] Retainer pain (why performance-based wins).** The old model = "5 clients felt like 5 bosses," constant chasing for late payments, texting at 10pm, "churn and burn" — you can't scale because taking new clients drops old ones. This is the emotional case for zero-upfront/performance deals, distinct from the logical case (certainty, risk-reversal).

## 8. Named frameworks & selling principles

**[Book] TIMER** (Travis Sago's framework) — scores any offer 1-10 across **T**ime, **I**dentity, **M**oney, **E**nergy, **R**eputation from the buyer's POV. Book's worked example: Copywriting retainer = 18/50, FB Ads agency = 19/50, ROYA = **47/50**. Ready-made comparison-chart device.

**[Book] Anchor Points** — a payment-timing negotiation system. Milestones in a partner's sales funnel, each worth an escalating $ value as a lead nears close; lets you negotiate to get paid at the earliest favorable milestone. Worked example (debt consolidation, 6 anchors, $30 → $350).

**[Book] Named selling principles** (each with its own verbatim title — good as content pillars or a "how we sell" explainer):
1. **Widen The Yes Hole** — remove price as an objection via 100% performance structure (not discounting, which signals low value)
2. **Be Ready To Walk Away** — a discipline/stance, illustrated with the candle-brand nudge script above
3. **We Are The Prize** — "prettiest girl at the bar," know your worth, don't get bullied on splits
4. **It's A HELL YES Or A HELL NO** — the SELLER's own decision filter (distinct from "EASY YES," which is about the buyer)
5. **Show, Don't Tell** — demo-over-pitch philosophy (hot dog description vs. hot dog photo analogy)

**[Book] "Sniper vs. machine gun"** — named contrast for personalized 1:1 outreach vs. mass copy-paste blasts.

**[Book] "No niche" contrarian stance** — explicit pushback on "niche down" advice: because ROYA solves a universal problem, go after every industry, not one. Strong contrarian hook material.

**[Book] Marketing 101 (Dan's own formula):** *"We find a market. We find a pain point. We provide a solution. If that solution solves their problem in a unique, clearly different way using a desirable concept, we have a winner."*

## 19. "The Same Pie" — competitive-resistance logic **[Campaign]**

Developed for the Flexx + Buildy webinar's new business-case section (the "why does DBR get zero sales resistance" beat). Not previously named anywhere in the bank — a genuinely new, ownable argument distinct from the risk-reversal spine (§4/§7):

- **The core insight:** sales resistance isn't really about price or trust in isolation — it's a function of MARKET CROWDING. Every other service a business owner gets pitched (lead gen, SEO, Facebook Ads, copywriting, email marketing) is fighting over the same pool of **NEW leads**, and all of them want paid upfront or via retainer. That crowding is what manufactures the resistance — not something inherent to "being sold to."
- **"Everyone wants a slice of the same pie"** — the coined line for this. Strong, ownable, plain-English way to describe the crowded NEW-lead market. Pairs naturally with the book's existing "money down the drain" line (§7 verbatim scripts — "all they see are flashing red lights: WARNING: MONEY GOING DOWN THE DRAIN") — that's the business owner's felt experience of the crowding; "slice of the same pie" is the mechanism behind why they feel it.
- **The old-leads inversion:** because everyone else assumes dead/old leads are worthless, literally nobody is competing for them. Going after OLD leads instead of NEW ones isn't just lower-risk (already established via the zero-upfront offer) — it's **structurally uncontested**. This is a sharper, more specific version of the book's "trash can assets" framing (book-mining §, "when we switch the focus to 'trash can' assets they don't care about, they're happy to offload them onto us") — same idea, but framed through competitive dynamics rather than just low expectations.
- **Reusable line structure:** "When everyone wants a piece of the NEW lead pie, you get a LOT of sales resistance. But when we go after OLD leads, you rarely get ANY — because no one else wants to do it." Good as a direct hook, voiceover line, or slide beat as-is.
- **Where this plugs into existing material:** strengthens the "we are the prize"/"prettiest girl at the bar" positioning (§8) with an actual *reason* — you're the prize because you're the only one not fighting over the crowded pie, not just because of confidence/frame. Also gives teeth to the Client-vs-Partner reframe (§4): retainer-model agencies are, structurally, all competing for the same finite pool of new-lead budget, which is part of why that model breeds "5 bosses" resentment.

## 17. The Landlord metaphor **[Campaign]**

Surfaced in the Hulk ad campaign: reframe the whole opportunity as **becoming a landlord who rents out Androids**, rather than "running an AI agency." Landlord/property language maps cleanly onto the existing asset philosophy already in §4 (Controlling vs. Owning Assets) — it's the plain-English, relatable version of that idea:

- A landlord doesn't build the house, they just control it and collect rent → you don't build the AI, Flexxable already built it, you just deploy ("rent out") the Androids and collect the split.
- "Almost-passive income" is the promised end-state — echoes "FU money," but landlord framing gives it a familiar, tangible wealth-building analogy most people already understand and aspire to (unlike "AI agency," which requires explaining what that even is).
- Fits the acronym literally: **R**ent **O**ut **Y**our **A**ndroids — the name already IS the landlord metaphor, just not spelled out that way in current copy. Worth testing hooks that make the landlord frame explicit rather than leaving it implicit in the acronym.
- Natural pairing with **Hulk** (§5): if Hulk is the "portfolio manager" that runs and self-optimizes every Android across every client, the landlord metaphor extends naturally — Hulk is what makes it "almost-passive" instead of active management, i.e. Hulk is the property-management layer on top of the landlord's rental portfolio.
- Contrast angle worth testing: real-estate landlord (needs capital, tenants, maintenance, years to build a portfolio) vs. Android landlord (zero capital, "tenant" = already-existing dead leads a business already owns, "maintenance" = Hulk, portfolio built in days not years). This is a stronger, more concrete version of the book's "30-year wealth shortcut" claim (§4) — same argument, sharper analogy.
