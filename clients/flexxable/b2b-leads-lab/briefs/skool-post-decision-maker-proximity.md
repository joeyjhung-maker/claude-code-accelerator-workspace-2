# Skool brief — Decision-maker proximity beats company prestige

Room: B2B Gifts / B2B Leads Lab.

Job: Deliver a useful philosophy NAP that improves targeting decisions.

Tap position: T1. Hook-forward, valuable and complete. No promotion.

Mechanic: Philosophy stance grounded in Matt’s real campaign.

The Ask: None. End on a confident takeaway or instruction rather than bolting on an engagement question.

Avatar: Anyone responsible for landing meetings with high-value B2B prospects. Do not use escape-the-9-to-5 framing.

Proof: Matt sent 14 bottles and booked four coffee dates with businesses doing $20M–$80M annually. Dan’s diagnosis was that $50M–$80M targets often routed the decision through a COO or CMO who then had to resell the idea internally. Dan recommended a $5M–$20M sweet spot where the sender can more often reach the actual decision-maker. Source: `clients/flexxable/primers/lumpy-mail-bottle-coaching-call.md`, sections “Proof numbers” and “What killed Matt’s results.”

Core idea: The biggest logo is not always the best prospect. Decision-maker proximity can matter more than company prestige. A smaller whale where you can speak directly to the owner or CEO may be more valuable than a giant company where your message gets diluted through layers of management.

Format: Skool community post from Dan. Aim for roughly 150–200 words. Headline plus body and Dan sign-off. One sentence per line. Plain text only, with no Markdown bold formatting.

Guidelines:
- Make the distinction vivid and easy to understand.
- Keep Matt’s identity and numbers accurate, but do not turn the post into a video promotion or testimonial recap.
- Do not mention or link to the YouTube interview.
- Do not claim the $5M–$20M range is universally correct for every market; present it as Dan’s recommended sweet spot from this campaign.
- Dan voice: conversational, cheeky, punchy, high personality.

Cross-seed check: No conflict. This post stays inside B2B Gifts and teaches targeting; it does not recruit from or redirect another room.

Seed verbatim: The biggest company is not always the best prospect; decision-maker access at a $5–20M business can beat the prestige of chasing $50–80M firms through layers of gatekeepers.
