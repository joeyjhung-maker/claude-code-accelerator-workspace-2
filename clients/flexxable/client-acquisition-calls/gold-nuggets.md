## 1. The "Doctor's Diagnosis" framework (recurs constantly — this is the core method)

**The core analogy**, Leandro, 2026-08-03 and 2026-08-24 (near-verbatim both
times, so it's clearly a rehearsed teaching line — safe to treat as canon):

> "It's almost like you walk up to a doctor and you say, 'Doc, my heart
> hurts.' And he says, 'Okay, lay down, let's open you up.' And you're like,
> are you crazy? But if he asks you questions — does it hurt when you
> breathe, do you get dizzy when you stand up — and starts learning the
> symptoms... that's the thing, symptoms are really important for us to
> understand and position ourselves towards what we're gonna do."

**Why it lands:** it's a single clean image that explains the entire
"ask before you pitch" sales philosophy. Never open with the AI/solution —
open with questions about their sales process and let them describe their own
pain first.

**Angle:** stand-alone hook/post about why "leading with the demo" kills
deals — this analogy carries the whole post on its own.

## 2. "Smell like a buyer, versus a seller" — positioning as investor, not vendor

Leandro, 2026-08-03 / 2026-08-24:

> "I try to smell like a buyer versus a seller. I'm asking questions, they're
> giving me their... they're telling me about their business... You gotta
> let them tell you a problem, a situation, a symptom, something that's been
> going on in their business before you come in with a solution."

Paired with the recurring **investor framing** — Leandro repeats a version of
this in nearly every call:

> "I don't even know who's the developer who's gonna [build] the thing here
> yet, but we can ask the questions when the time comes... I'm always taking
> away that part of the equation out of the sales conversation, because
> that's not where I want their focus to be."

And Dan's own self-description (secondhand, via Leandro and Nat, multiple
calls): **Dan himself doesn't log into GoHighLevel. He doesn't know how to
build the bots.** He positions as the investor/dealmaker who brings capital
and risk, not the technician. "Some problems are a matter of who, not how."

**Angle:** "Why the founder of [mechanism/method] has never built one of his
own bots" — contrarian authority-through-non-expertise post. Strong pillar
content for the whole Flexxable "you don't need to be technical" pitch.

## 3. The "coffee date" / first-date de-risking language

Leandro, 2026-08-03, explaining why he deliberately uses dating language:

> "One of the things that scares deals is the idea of, like, 'oh this is
> gonna be a lot of work, like I'm marrying Joe.' ...we're taking this very
> small next step, we're testing with a few leads... that positioning helps
> take the weight off the shoulders. Because once you make a decision,
> that's when buyer's remorse kicks in."

**Angle:** post about why "sign a contract" language kills small deals and
"let's just test it" language doesn't — reframing commitment size.

## 6. "Lead Rescue" — a genuinely new mechanism name/angle, not just DBR repackaged

Nat, 2026-08-24, in response to a question about ongoing revenue beyond a
one-time DBR sweep:

> "It's kind of still database reactivation, but it's not — it's essentially
> **lead reactivation**. I like to call it **lead rescue**, because it's not
> dead, it's only 72 hours old, 24 hours old. It's just the salespeople have
> given up on it because they've got hotter leads coming in that, rightly,
> they need to prioritise."

This is distinct from DBR: it targets businesses with **enough salespeople
that they claim AI can't touch their new leads** — the pitch isn't "your
leads are dead," it's "your team is too busy closing hot leads to follow up
the merely-warm ones, and that's real ongoing money, not a one-time sweep."

**Angle:** this is a genuinely under-used mechanism/positioning angle worth
its own brief — "Lead Rescue" as a distinct offer from DBR, aimed specifically
at businesses with active sales teams (the ones who reject DBR pitches
because "our salespeople have got that covered").

## 7. Pricing psychology — "what would make this dreamy for you?"

Leandro's reverse-negotiation line, repeated in 2026-08-03 and 2026-08-10:

> "I'll ask them, 'What would make this dreamy for you?' and then negotiate
> from there. Many times people are gonna do right by you — they're not
> gonna try to lowball."

And the underlying rule (Nat, 2026-08-10, 2026-08-12 — stated almost
identically twice, so treat as canon):

> "Make the size of the retainer equal the size of the pain they're
> currently feeling... charge a small client five grand a month, they'll
> laugh you out the door. Charge a huge client spending 40K a month on ads,
> five grand's tiny — that's a fraction of what one salesperson's salary
> costs them for a quarter."

**Real figures mentioned** (ROYA's own results — needs sign-off before using
as proof, see README caution):
- £18,000/month retainer + £20-per-close performance fee — a large debt-industry client, 48-hour lead-rescue Android (Nat, 2026-08-10 and 2026-08-12)
- $20 (or as low as $10) per saved abandoned cart — large e-commerce client, DBR/abandoned-cart bot (Nat, 2026-08-12)
- $750 setup fee → $1,497 total onboarding, stepping down retainer over 3-6 months, then pure pay-per-appointment after the lead list runs dry (Brian's own pricing model, 2026-08-24, described in detail — this is a student's real structure, good as a "here's how one operator prices it" teaching example)

**Angle:** "charge the pain, not a rate card" post, maybe paired with the
found-money/leaky-bucket concepts as a 3-part pricing mini-series.

## 9. The Facebook "Lurker Method" — fishing questions in comments, not pitches

Nat, 2026-08-10, describing how to prospect inside Facebook groups without
getting booted for pitching:

> "Hi guys, I'm working with a few life insurance agents right now, and
> they're saying it's costing them more and more to get good quality new
> leads. Is anyone else finding this?"

Anyone who replies "yes" has just self-identified as a live prospect.
Alternative version — commenting on someone else's rant post instead of
starting your own:

> "You mentioned you've got one salesperson but you're really struggling —
> what would happen if we took some of the low-value work off their plate?"

**Angle:** this is a genuinely reusable prospecting tactic, not just a story —
could become a workflow note as well as a content post. Dan calls it "the
Lurker Method" internally.

## 14. Small local businesses vs. big spenders need different pain angles

Nat, 2026-08-12, a clean split worth its own post:

> "Local business owners, if they're smaller, don't necessarily feel the
> pain of turning old leads into sales, because they're not spending 10K a
> week to only get half those leads picked up. What resonates with them is
> more like: are you sick of answering the phone on a Saturday night at
> dinner because all your staff have finished for the weekend?"

Bigger spenders respond to the money-left-on-the-table math (DBR/speed to
lead); smaller mom-and-pop respond to the personal-burden angle (voice AI
answering out-of-hours calls, chasing quotes nobody follows up on).

**Angle:** segment-specific hook pairing — same mechanism, two very different
emotional entry points depending on business size.

## 21. Skip the signed contract for the test — send a data-processing agreement instead

Nat, 2026-03-16, responding to a student whose prospects were "digging their
heels in" every time he sent a formal agreement mid-conversation:

> "They're not so much bothered about the quote-unquote contract of what
> everyone's going to be paid for a test... What I would say you send... is
> just how I approach our data, and the security measures... you might find
> a lot less friction, but you've also overcome that objection before it's
> even come into the head."

The story behind it: Rob Brown (a ROYA member who later sold a stake in his
company for millions) got started this way — no agreement for the test, just
a data-processing document, commercials discussed after results were proven.
Leandro adds a variant from 2026-07-13: for a more legal-minded prospect (a
newspaper owner, ex-lawyer, who wanted a real agreement), he took ROYA's
standard partnership agreement, fed it to Claude, and had it rewritten in two
or three iterations into a **test-only** version — scoped to the pilot, not
the whole relationship.

**Angle:** "don't let the contract kill the coffee date" post — the DPA swap
is a genuinely reusable, low-friction move for anyone hitting signature
friction on a small test.

## 24. Travis Sago's "Second Helping Ladder"

Leandro, 2026-05-04, attributing this to Dan's own mentor:

> "Travis Sago... has this concept of the second helping ladder. He got a
> lot of partners just by, like, if you buy my book, and I have Roya at the
> end, I would offer you that campaign: if you send a physical letter to
> your customers who bought the book, thanking them for buying and offering
> the next thing, do you think that would help? ... People just bought the
> book, they're kind of harder to buy the next level thing right now, and
> no one's willing to send a physical letter anymore."

This is distinct from DBR — it's about the **next** offer after someone's
already bought something, not reactivating dead leads. Leandro reuses the
same underlying idea later with Gabriel (2026-05-25) for a medical-device
client's affiliate buyers, and with JSpeck (2026-05-25) for car dealership
customers.

**Angle:** "the second helping ladder" as its own named framework — a good
explainer post about post-purchase reactivation being a distinct, underused
lever from cold DBR.

## 30. Anchor a referral fee as a fixed cap, not a percentage — and remember it's not your best long-term client

Nat, 2026-03-30, on structuring a super-connector referral deal:

> "Dan usually does a fixed number — I'm gonna pay you up to 10K. That's a

Nat adds an important caveat later, 2026-06-24, using Rob Brown (the ROYA

**Angle:** "anchor high, not percentage" as a standalone pricing-psychology
post, with the honest addendum that your first referral deal is a foot in
the door, not the deal you build your business on.

## 31. Reverse-engineer the price from what they already pay per call or appointment

Leandro, 2026-05-04, describing how he actually lands on a number instead of
guessing:

> "I reverse engineer what it's gonna look like. How many calls are we
> looking at, and how much are they willing to pay for a call? ... This
> looks like $1,000 a week. I'm okay with that."

He frames the risk to himself the same way, with a coin-flip: "if it's
heads, I lose $100 [worth of my time]... so I just need to make sure the
coin's flippable at first, that's it." Nat separately mentions the old
industry precedent for this kind of milestone-based pricing — Dan's
debt-consolidation days used a "pack-back" model, paying 3-4x more once a
full document set was collected versus a flat fee per raw lead.

**Angle:** "don't guess your price, reverse-engineer it" — a practical,
numbers-led post for anyone unsure how to quote a first deal.

## 33. "Free cheapens it" — never call the test a free trial

Dan Wardrope, 2026-05-18, correcting a student's pitch on the spot:

> "There's a very big difference between free and low risk. Free almost
> cheapens it. We remove the risk — I'm prepared to invest in a test.
> That's a very different stance to free, where someone might think, meh,
> okay, free, I don't have to value this. A lot of people don't value free,
> do they?"

**Angle:** a sharp, contrarian-sounding correction to a common instinct

## 36. Wade Collins' free-lead-funnel reciprocity play

Wade Collins, 2026-05-18, describing a distribution hack he's used for
years: he builds a generic SEO lead-gen site for a home-services niche
(e.g. "hot water heaters, Atlanta"), lets leads flow in for free from
HomeAdvisor/Angie's List/Thumbtack, then hand-picks the best-reviewed local
company and gives them the leads **for free**, unprompted:

> "I don't want anything for this, I just want to find someone who can take
> care of these people... I get a good sense of who the guy is, because I
> want to work with quality people." Once trust is built: "Hi, this is
> Wade — can I talk to the owner? I don't have any problem getting ahold of
> the owner... I'm looking for a plumber who can handle the leads."

He compares it to the free-sample counter at a food court: "how many people
actually eat there after they had a sample? That's all I'm doing — giving
him a sample."

**Angle:** a full story-led post on its own — reciprocity-driven,
zero-cost-to-you lead brokering as a foot-in-the-door before ever
mentioning AI or DBR. One of the strongest standalone stories in this batch.

## 37. Harvey Brody's NEER — Nearly Existing Economic Relationship

## 39. Reframe a flat "no" as agreement, then ask an assumptive question

Nat, 2026-06-01, coaching a student (a recruiter pivoting into DBR) who kept
getting "we don't work with recruitment agencies" as a knee-jerk objection:

> "That's fantastic news, because that's not what I'm offering you." Then,
> instead of an open question that invites the prospect to list every
> reason they distrust the category: "I'm guessing the reason you don't
> work with recruiters is because of XYZ — am I on the right track there?"

Nat's reasoning: an open "why don't you work with them?" question can make
a defensive prospect brace for a sales pitch; an assumptive, already-correct
guess does the opposite — it shows you already get it.

**Angle:** "turn a categorical no into a yes" objection-handling post —
useful any time your offer gets pattern-matched to a bad category it
doesn't actually belong to.

## 42. Verify a performance deal with campaign-level access, not full CRM access

Nat, 2026-07-01, on the trust problem in a back-end percentage deal:

> "On the back end, we ask for a weekly report... but also, we want a
> view-only seat in your CRM." When a client worries that means seeing
> everything: "you can give us campaign-level access — when you send the
> leads to them, they'll have a tag of a certain campaign, they can just
> give you access, view-only, to those leads."

She also flags the built-in sanity check: cross-reference the client's own
stated close rate against what your bot actually booked — "if you've done a
slight bit of qualification and you've converted zero, that's when you
might start thinking, huh."

**Angle:** a practical "how to protect yourself on a performance deal
without asking for the keys to the kingdom" post — solves a real, common
anxiety for anyone new to percentage-based pricing.

## 43. Stacking bots without coordinating ownership can actively backfire
