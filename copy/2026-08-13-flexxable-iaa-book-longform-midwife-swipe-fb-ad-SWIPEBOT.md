I've been building lead gen agencies for 10 years.

I've worked with over a thousand agency owners. SEO shops, paid ads teams, SMMA, local lead gen, the lot.

And I can tell you something that most people in this industry won't.

When an agency owner comes to me stuck, working sixty-hour weeks and still earning the same flat income month after month, I can usually tell within the first conversation whether they're going to break out or stay stuck for years.

It has nothing to do with their skill.

It has nothing to do with their niche.

And it has very little to do with how hard they work or how good their ads are.

It comes down to one thing.

And once I explain it, you'll probably look at your own agency completely differently.

---

When I first started, I believed what everyone in the industry believed. That the way to grow was more clients, better results, tighter funnels, a bigger team, higher rates.

Some agencies were just earlier on. Some needed better systems. Some just needed more time.

The advice I gave, and the advice I followed myself, was: work harder, get better, scale the team, and push through.

And for the first few years, that's exactly what I did. Because that's what I believed.

But somewhere around year three or four, I started noticing something that didn't fit.

I'd speak to ten agency owners in a week. All with similar skills. All running real agencies with real clients. All billing flat fees for some combination of ads, funnels and fulfilment.

And without fail, they'd split into two groups.

Group one would break out within a few months. Revenue would climb. Hours would drop. The owner would start talking about the business like it was something they ran, not something that ran them.

Group two wouldn't break out. Month after month. Year after year. The same flat income. The same exhaustion. The same person looking at me on a Zoom call with dark circles asking "when does this get easier?"

I kept thinking it was talent. Some owners were just better operators. Some agencies had a longer runway before things clicked.

But it wasn't consistent. I'd see an average operator with nothing special about his funnel break out beautifully within months. And I'd see a genuinely brilliant marketer, someone whose campaigns I admired, still grinding at the same flat income two and three years later.

It didn't track with skill. It didn't track with niche. It didn't track with anything I'd been taught about how agencies grow.

But it did track with one thing.

Whether they were getting paid flat, or getting paid a cut.

---

It took me years to see it because I wasn't looking for it.

But once I saw it, I couldn't unsee it.

The ones who broke out were almost always taking a cut of what they generated, and you could see it in what their week actually looked like.

One of them had stopped sending invoices altogether, he just sent a monthly statement showing what he'd generated and what his cut of it was. Another had his biggest month ever from a client he'd never run a single ad for. Another got paid twice in one week without opening an ad account, because the AI had booked calls out of two different client databases while he was on holiday.

The ones who stayed stuck were billing flat. Flat fee for running the ads. Flat fee for managing the funnel. Flat fee for delivering the leads. The only way to earn more was to take on more clients, which meant more hours, more stress, more of themselves poured into the machine. Flat income, flat months, flat no matter what they did.

And I started to feel uncomfortable. Because the pattern was clear.

I brought it up with another agency owner once. He said "well yeah, obviously. If you're paid flat for your effort, then more effort is the only lever you've got. And there's a hard ceiling on effort."

And I remember thinking, that's it? That's the whole thing?

Because if that's the whole thing, then why is the entire industry teaching people to work harder, hire more, take on more clients and optimise more campaigns, all while billing flat? We're telling people to push harder against the one thing holding them down.

---

There was an agency owner I worked with last year. I'll call him Mark.

Mark ran a paid ads agency. He'd been at it about two and a half years. Decent clients. Good results. He knew what he was doing.

He was doing everything right. And I mean everything.

Running the campaigns himself because he didn't trust anyone else with the spend. On client calls three, four times a week. Writing the copy, building the landing pages, pulling the reports. Taking WhatsApps at 9pm because one client's cost per lead had crept up and they wanted answers now. He'd done free work for two clients that quarter just to stop them leaving, and undercut his own pricing on the last three pitches just to win the business.

And some of it was working. In the moment. While he was doing it.

The campaigns performed, while he watched them. The clients stayed, while he kept them sweet. The flat fees landed, while he kept grinding.

But the second he stepped back, even for a weekend, everything he'd spent the week holding together would start to slip. A campaign would overspend. A client would get anxious. A lead would go cold because nobody followed up fast enough.

Every single month. The same cycle. Pour himself in, things hold. Step back, things slide.

He'd get on our calls looking worse. More tired. More flat. By our third conversation he'd stopped pretending things were fine. He'd just say "I don't know how much longer I can do this."

I could see what was happening. He had become the only thing holding the agency together. His hands were on every campaign. His voice was on every call. His phone was the only safety net between a client staying and a client leaving.

He was the entire machine. And he was breaking.

---

Around this time I started looking properly at what was actually different about the ones who'd got off flat billing.

Not the surface stuff. The economics underneath.

Every business, every single one, is sitting on a database of old leads. People who filled out a form, clicked an ad, maybe had a convo, weeks or months or even years ago, and never bought. They're in the CRM. Hundreds of them, sometimes thousands, gathering dust.

And the business owner already paid real money to acquire every one of them. That cost is spent. It's gone.

This is why every business owner you talk to says the same thing about their old leads. "Yeah, we should probably do something with those." And they never do. Because in their head those leads are dead and the money's already written off.

That write-off is exactly what creates the opening. If you turn even a fraction of those dead leads into sales, the owner is getting revenue out of something he'd already binned, without spending another penny to generate it. Which is why, when you say "I'll take a cut, 30 to 50 percent, of any sale that comes out of your old leads," there's almost no resistance. You're not asking him to spend anything. You're not asking him to risk anything.

I stopped guessing about whether AI could really do the work when I saw what happened with a legal client of ours. Stamp duty refunds. They were about to cancel their campaign because the solicitors had to check every lead against hundreds of different case laws by hand, and they couldn't keep up. So we trained the AI on the entire case law corpus instead. It hit around 90% qualification accuracy, and it saved the campaign.

And that's when I knew. If it can qualify a lead against hundreds of case laws better than a room full of solicitors, it can hold a convo with a dead lead in a CRM and work out who's ready to buy.

So here's what was happening with every agency owner in group two.

They'd spend all week generating new leads, managing campaigns, writing copy, optimising funnels, doing all the work. And they'd get paid flat for that effort. The same fee whether the client made ten sales or a hundred.

Then they'd try to grow by doing more of the same. More clients. More campaigns. More hours. And the income would stay flat, because flat was baked into the deal.

They weren't failing. Flat billing was capping everything they did.

---

I couldn't tell Mark to just work harder. He was already working harder than almost anyone I knew, and that isn't a strategy, it's a countdown.

But I also couldn't keep telling him to optimise his way out of it. Because optimising inside a flat fee just means hitting the same ceiling more efficiently.

I needed something that did what Mark was doing, pulling revenue out of clients, without Mark being the one doing it every hour of every day.

That's when we built what we now call an "AI employee."

I'd been sceptical about the whole AI space. I'd seen agency owners try chatbots, automations, all sorts of tools, and most of them were either clunky, ignored by the leads, or both.

But this was different. It wasn't just an automation. It had three things working together. It reaches out to the client's old dead leads by SMS, not email, so the messages actually get read. It holds a real back-and-forth convo, qualifies the prospect and handles objections, instead of blasting one message and hoping someone replies. And it books the sales call straight into the calendar, whether you're at the gym, out for lunch, or picking your kids up from school.

And the agency takes a cut, 30 to 50 percent of every sale it generates, without writing a line of copy, managing a campaign, producing a creative, or spending a penny of the client's ad budget.

I walked Mark through the mechanics and why I thought taking a cut would change things for him.

He looked at me and said "I'll try anything at this point."

The following month we spoke again.

Mark got on the call and he looked different. Not transformed. Not buzzing. Just less hollowed out. Like someone who'd had a weekend off for the first time in a year and hadn't spent it panicking.

He said "I didn't touch my laptop last Saturday. First time I can remember."

I asked about the install. He'd started with one client, a database of a few thousand old enquiries, some over a year old and a few nearly two. The install took him an afternoon. The AI started sending SMS the next morning. Three days later the first call was booked, and by the end of the week there were appointments sitting on the client's calendar from leads he'd completely forgotten about.

I asked about the cut. He said 40 percent of every sale that came out of those old leads. No invoice, no retainer negotiation, just a cut of what the AI generated.

I asked about the client. He said the dynamic had shifted. He wasn't walking into calls apologising for results or justifying a fee anymore.

I asked how he was doing. He went quiet for a second.

Not upset. Just the kind of pause where someone realises something has actually changed and hasn't had time to process it yet.

He said "I feel like I'm running a business instead of being trapped inside one."

By the end of the first month he'd installed it into three clients, and the cut was landing alongside his retainer work without the hours, the stress or the 9pm WhatsApps.

Mark said "I don't know why I spent two years billing flat."

Now, this works best stacked on top of what you're already doing. If you're serving clients already, you've got the relationships and you've got the trust, so you're not starting from zero.

But what surprised me with Mark, and with almost every owner since, is that the install changes more than the income. It changes your position with the client.

The moment a call gets booked off a lead the owner had written off, you stop being a cost on his P&L and become a source of revenue. He stops questioning your invoice, because there isn't one. He starts asking what else you could do. The clients who used to haggle over your flat fee start introducing you to people.

That's the part nobody expects. It isn't just that you earn more, it's that the whole relationship flips, and you go from defending a retainer to being the person they can't afford to lose.

Mark was one of the first agency owners I saw this with. But he wasn't the last.
