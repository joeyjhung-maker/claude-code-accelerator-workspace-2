HL: Japan understands what cold outreach completely ignores

Dan here…

Japan has a long-standing tradition called ochūgen.

It happens around the middle of the year… and there’s another gift-giving season at the end of the year called oseibo.

The short version is that people send gifts to show thanks to those who’ve helped them.

Family, friends, business acquaintances, superiors… anyone they feel gratitude towards.

And here’s what makes this interesting to us.

The Japan Foundation explains that the gift isn’t simply about handing someone an object.

It gives shape to your thoughts and gratitude… with real attention paid to what you choose, how it’s presented and the act of giving itself.

In other words… it’s about the RELATIONSHIP.

The gift says “I thought about you” before anybody asks for anything.

Now compare that with most cold outreach.

Give me your time.

Give me your attention.

Give me 15 minutes on your calendar.

Give me your money.

ME ME ME ME ME.

Every message is a withdrawal from someone who never agreed to open an account with ya.

This is why lumpy mail works so well… but also why some peeps think about it completely wrong.

The box on their desk isn’t a bribe.

It’s not buying access to their calendar.

It’s opening the relationship with consideration BEFORE you make your request.

When they open the package and read the letter, the first feeling should be:

“This person actually thought about me.”

Not…

“This person wants something from me.”

That’s the difference between another cold stranger demanding a meeting… and a real human being who gave before they asked.

The coffee date becomes easier because the relationship started in a completely different place.

So before your next send, look at the package through the recipient’s eyes.

Does it feel considered?

Or does it feel like a clever tactic that could’ve been sent to anyone with a pulse?

The gift and letter should make them feel considered… not targeted.

Dan
