# Everything is made of only seven building blocks

One last thing before we start zooming in, and it is honestly the thing that makes this whole monster doable in the first place.

No matter how big that blueprint gets, no matter how many nodes you stack onto it, the entire thing is built out of just seven kinds of parts. Seven.

The moment you can look at any node and go "oh, that is just a bot," or "that is just a connection," all the fear drains right out of it, because you already know how to build that kind of thing. You are not building a hundred different things. You are building seven of them, over and over, in different combinations.

## The one trick: sort by what it does, not what it is made of

Before we name them, here is the rule that keeps the seven straight, and it trips everyone up at first.

You do not sort these by what they are made of. You sort them by what they do.

A lot of these are just markdown files under the hood. A primer is a markdown file. A skill is a markdown file. But they do completely different jobs, so they count as different parts. Function first, always. Once you hold that, the rest is easy.

[image.png]

## The seven

**Code** is the part that does the same thing every single time. It is a script, usually Python. It scrapes your reviews, it scores your ads, it pulls your report, it formats the final doc, it glues the other parts together in the right order. If you can describe it as a mechanical recipe that does not need any taste, it is Code.

**A Bot** is the part that thinks. It is a prompt, a skill, an instruction you hand to the model so it can write a hook, edit a line, classify an ad, or generate an idea. It takes something in and makes something new come out, and it does it using judgment instead of a fixed recipe. When somebody says "skill file," they mean a Bot. The Bot is the worker.

**Knowledge** is the part the Bot reads to get good. Your primers, your swipe banks, your frameworks, your notes. Knowledge does not do anything on its own. It just sits there being true, and you feed it into a Bot so the Bot knows what good actually looks like. The cleanest way to feel the difference: a Bot acts, Knowledge informs. A primer never wrote an ad in its life. It just makes the thing that writes the ad better.

**A Database** is where you keep the stuff you need to sort and search later. Your hypotheses, your seed bank, your coverage map of which segments you have hit, your library of saved edits. The difference between this and Knowledge is how you reach for it. Knowledge you read whole, top to bottom. A Database you query, as in "show me every ad I have for women who are unaware." You can put a little retrieval layer on top later if you want to chat with it, but you do not have to start there.

**A Connection** is a wire to the outside world. It is how your system talks to Meta, to your scraper, to your image generator, to Google Docs. Under the hood it is an API key, or one of those MCP links, but they are the same kind of part: a socket and a key that lets your system reach something it does not own. Every time the system has to touch the real world, that is a Connection.

**A Surface** is a screen where you, the human, actually see things and move them around with your hands. The segment map you click through. The canvas where you edit. A little dashboard. You do not strictly need surfaces, because a lot of the system can run with no screen at all, but when you want to see the thing and play with it directly, you build a Surface. And the good news is a Surface can be as simple as a single web page.

**The Human** is you. Your judgment, your taste, the moment where you step in and choose. On the map it shows up as a gate, and almost every gate is optional, because you can always let the system run straight through if you trust it. But the Human is where the one of one lives, so most of the time you want a hand on the wheel somewhere.

## The same seven, at a glance

| The part | What makes it this part (the test) | Examples in the system |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Code** | runs the same every time, no taste | classifier, ad scorer, the doc formatter, the orchestrator |
| **Bot** | takes input and makes something new with judgment | hook bot, writing bot, editing bot, ideation engine |
| **Knowledge** | gets read or injected, never acts on its own | primers, swipe bank, your frameworks |
| **Database** | you query it for rows, you do not read it whole | hypotheses, seed bank, coverage map, saved edits |
| **Connection** | a wire and a key to an outside service | Meta API, your scraper, the image generator |
| **Surface** | a screen a human sees and acts inside | the segment map, the editing canvas |
| **Human** | judgment, the optional gate | you, choosing at the gates |

## The boundary rules (for when two of them feel the same)

A few pairs blur together until you have a clean line between them. Here are the lines:

- **Bot vs Knowledge.** Both are markdown. A Bot produces output, Knowledge gets read into a Bot. A skill file is a Bot. A primer is Knowledge.
- **Knowledge vs Database.** Knowledge you read whole or inject. A Database you query for specific rows. A primer is Knowledge. A tagged table of your ad coverage is a Database.
- **Surface vs Human.** The Surface is the screen. The Human is the judgment. You can have one without the other (a voice note gate has no screen, a read only dashboard has no gate).
- **Connection vs Code.** The Connection is the wire and the key. The Code is the logic that uses it. A Python script pulling your Meta report is Code using a Connection.

And the question everyone asks, so let us just settle it here: are an MCP connection and an API key two different things? No. They are the same type. Both are just a wire to an outside service. The MCP is the socket and the API key is the key that fits it. So we call them both a Connection and we move on.

That is the whole vocabulary. Code, Bot, Knowledge, Database, Connection, Surface, Human. Learn to spot those seven and you can look at any node on the map and immediately know what it would take to build it.

## The two games

Here is the frame underneath everything. You are playing two games at once.

- **The psychology game.** Making ads that actually move a human. Hooks, pain points, copy blocks, avatars, mechanism, proof. This is winning the game of persuasion, and it is old as dirt and it never really changes.

## Move 1: The Map (creative diversity)

A healthy ad account is not one winner cloned fifty times. It is the right message, to all the right people, in different formats, at different stages of their journey.

[image.png]

That whole spread is creative diversity, and the simplest way to hold it is this:

> **Creative Diversity = Messaging × Creative**

And one layer down:

> **Messaging = Segments × Awareness × Concept/Angle**, and then all of that times **Creative** (the visual)

Of everything in there, segments and creative carry the most weight, so those are the two you watch. When you multiply it all together, segments by awareness by angle by visual, you get hundreds of thousands of possible ads. It is combinatorially explosive, and that explosion is exactly the point, because it means you always have somewhere new to go, and it is also the whole reason the real job is selection.

### Segments: the biggest unlock

A segment is just a slice of a market. That is it. And finding a slice nobody else is talking to is the single biggest win in this entire game, because when you are first into a segment your copy does not even have to be great. There is no competition there yet.

[image.png]

Now, the thing that trips everyone up: people mix two totally different things together when they talk about segments. Keep them in separate boxes and it all gets clean.

**Box one: the dimensions.** These are the axes you slice on, and there are three of them.

| Dimension | The question it answers | What it holds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Outcome** (the vertical spine) | what they want, or what hurts | pains and desires; narrower outcomes nest under broader ones (joint, then knee, then hip) |
| **Demographic** (horizontal, crosses the outcomes) | who they observably are, or say they are | age, gender, location, identity (Christian, athlete) |
| **Qualifiers** (filters that thin a slice) | what narrows them further | Beliefs and Conditions |

That third one, Qualifiers, has two kinds inside it, and you have to keep them straight:

- **Beliefs**: what they think or prefer. The mechanism they credit for the problem (cortisol, inflammation), plus their soft values (organic only, no needle, natural). Soft, chosen.
- **Conditions**: a fact that limits fit (vegetarian, pregnant, diabetic, on a GLP-1). Hard, factual.

The reason Beliefs and Conditions cannot be one bucket: a belief is something you think, a condition is something you are. A pregnant woman is not believing she is pregnant. Soft versus hard. Do not collapse them.

> Quick note on language. I call that facet "Belief," not "mechanism," on purpose, because "mechanism" needs to stay reserved for your product's own unique mechanism, the curiosity block in the copy. Two different things, so do not let the one word do double duty.

**Box two: the segment types.** These are the named sizes of slice. This is your vocabulary, and the one rule that fixes all the fuzziness is that none of these are dimensions. They are just how big a slice you happened to grab. A persona is not a dimension. TAM is not a dimension. Dimensions are axes, sizes are sizes, and you never put them in the same list.

[image.png]

| Term | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **TAM** | the whole market, everyone, the entire spine |
| **Outcome segment** | one outcome, a vertical slice, the chunkiest and most natural cut |
| **Super segment** | spans multiple outcomes, usually because one belief bundles them (cortisol pulls joint, heart, and hormones together) |
| **Sub segment** | underneath one outcome, narrowed by a facet (a line inside a column) |
| **Cross segment** | one facet held constant across the whole spine ("just women," or "everyone who is vegetarian"), horizontal |
| **Persona** | one thin slice, fully dressed up as a real named person |

A persona, by the way, is just an identity you attach to a slice. They are useful, but do not let a list of six personas fool you into thinking that is the whole market. There are thousands of slices under there.

[image.png]

Here is the subtle rule that keeps Cross and Super straight, because they feel similar: does the filter pick its own outcomes, or is it blind to them?

- A **condition** like "vegetarian" is outcome blind. You can be vegetarian with any pain on the board, so it sweeps the whole spine. That is a **cross segment**.
- A **belief** like "cortisol causes it" is not blind. It implies the outcomes cortisol actually drives (joint, heart, hormones) and excludes the ones it does not (eyesight), so it naturally bundles a subset. That is a **super segment**.

So belief filters bundle, and condition and demographic filters sweep. That is the clean line.

And there are exactly two ways to add a brand new segment: find a new outcome and grow the spine (a new column), or get narrower on one you already have with a new qualifier (a new line inside a column). New territory, or a finer slice. That is it.

### Awareness: sits on top of every segment

Every segment travels through stages of awareness, and you can reach the same person at any of them:

[image.png]

- Unaware
- Problem aware
- Solution aware
- Product aware
- Most aware

You do not have to treat all five as separate every time. Collapse them into three buckets: unaware and problem together, solution and product together, and most aware on its own.

The big thing to hold: awareness is a layer that rides on top of a segment, not another dimension inside it. Keep it out of the cube or you start double counting.

### Concept and Angle: keep it loose

This is the actual messaging, the different angles you take. And here I want you to do the exact opposite of what you did with segments: do not try to map it.

Mapping the entire space of possible angles is a fool's errand, because there is always one more out there you did not think of. So we keep this axis loose on purpose and we generate it instead, by pulling from real live sources. That is what STORMING is for, downstream in Ideation. For strategy, just know that it is a lever, and know that it gets fed.

### Creative: the visual

Two tracks.

[image.png]

- **Statics** come in three types: native (looks organic, top of funnel), templated (proven layouts you reuse), and branded (your hero product, bottom of funnel).
- **Video** has near infinite variety, and we break it up cleanly later with the CAST system (Conceit, Actor, Style, Terrain).

### One thing to settle about the visual

You will hear me say "segments are the biggest unlock," and you will also hear that Meta weighs the visual the heaviest. Both are true, and they are not in conflict, because they are true on different axes.

The way Meta works right now, the creative itself tells the algorithm who the ad is for, and the visual is the heaviest signal in that decision. Change the picture and you get treated as a genuinely new ad with a fresh shot at a new audience. The message and the segment still count, they are just secondary to the visual in that specific call.

But the segment is still the highest leverage move you can make, because it is the one decision that pays into both games at once. Change who you are talking to and the ad naturally looks different (you win the algorithm) and it speaks to a real person's real situation (you win the human). The visual is what the algorithm counts. The segment is what causes the right visual and converts the human. That is why we lead with segments even though the camera is what Meta sees first.

---

## Move 2: The Gaps

Now you have got the map. Here is the payoff: lay your actual ads on top of it. Wherever there is a cell with no ad, that is a gap.

[image.png]

The key gaps to hunt for:

- **New segments**: both kinds (a new outcome, or a finer sub slice), plus a process to actually find them.
- **New awareness levels**: almost nobody makes truly unaware ads, and almost nobody makes creative most aware ads. Everyone is jammed in the middle.
- **Diverse creative styles**: formats nobody in your category is running.
- **Totally new messages**: angles nobody is taking.
- **Or some combination** of all of the above.

Finding a gap that is still relevant is what blows an account up. That is the whole reason this matters so much. It is blue ocean. There are entire sections of the market, with real people and real money, where nobody is making ads that speak to them. You put a stall in the empty wing of the market and you might be the only option standing there.

So identifying gaps is a really, really big deal. Most of your biggest winners come from right here.

---

## Move 3: The Bet (explore vs exploit)

Now you decide where to point your effort, and there are only two directions:

[image.png]

- **Explore**: make net new ads to fill the gaps. Genuinely new territory.
- **Exploit**: squeeze your winners. Variations and scaling.

That is the same thing as your strategic allocation, which is just how you weight net new versus variations versus scaling. Net new is explore. Variations and scaling are both exploit. One axis, and the split is your call. Some people live almost entirely on net new, some spin a ton of variations. You figure out your own flow.

On the exploit side, the way you actually squeeze a winner is the CASHED system: Concept, Angle, Style, Hook, Editing, Demographic. Those are the levers you pull to top off, revive, or optimize a winning ad, and we will cover it in full later. One thing to flag now, though: not all of those levers are equal. Some of them (style, demographic, format) make the algorithm see a genuinely new ad and go find you a new audience. Others (a new hook on the same visual, an edit) help the human convert but get you zero new reach. Worth knowing which lever does which.

And the real answer is you do both at once. Only exploit and you plateau, because winners always fatigue eventually. Only explore and you have got no momentum. The brands that compound are always opening new territory while milking what already works.

---

## Move 4: The Decider (judgment + taste)

So now the actual strategic question: out of all those gaps and all those winners, what do you do next? You use two things.

[image.png]

### Judgment: chad logic

This is you looking at the whole map and making a few big, chunky, confident calls. You are not grinding a spreadsheet cell by cell, because there are too many cells and you would never finish. You weigh the things that actually matter:

- **Urgency** of the pain or desire (usually pain).
- **TAM**: how big is the slice.
- **Gap size**: both inside your own account and out in the competitor landscape.
- **Is it genuinely new**: because brand new wins even when the copy is mediocre.
- **Relevance**: can the creative actually reach them.
- **Zeitgeist**: what is trending right now that you are genuinely obsessive about.

It is impossible to be too scientific here, and you should not try. Big chunky changes work. You will get winners and sometimes you will not even know exactly why. And here is a bonus: chad logic gives you a clean story, for yourself and for your clients, which is honestly half of staying sane in this job.

### Taste: intuition

This is just knowing an ad is going to work and not always being able to say why. This is Malcolm Gladwell's Blink. And it is real, it is not woo, and it is worth understanding why, because a skeptic will poke the pop version.

Taste is not a gift. It is compressed experience: pattern recognition running so fast that it feels like a feeling. Three pillars hold it up:

- **Two systems of thinking** (Kahneman). System 1 is fast, automatic, intuitive. System 2 is slow and deliberate. Your taste is a trained System 1, your chad logic is System 2, and the whole point is to train System 1 well enough that you do not have to run System 2 on every single call.
- **Experts recognize, they do not compare** (Klein). Studying firefighters and ER nurses, Gary Klein found that experts do not weigh options. They recognize the situation as a pattern they have seen before, and the right move comes pre attached to the pattern. That is literally "I do not know why, but I want to test this." The pattern fired before the reasoning caught up.
- **Chunking** (the chess research). Masters do not calculate more moves than amateurs. They have stored tens of thousands of board patterns from years of saturation, so they just see the move. That is exactly why "live in the ad accounts" works. You are building chunks.

Now the part that actually matters for your system, and it is a gift, because it turns the dual flywheel from a metaphor into a mechanism. Kahneman and Klein argued for years and then co wrote a reconciliation, and their joint conclusion was that intuition is trustworthy under exactly two conditions:

1. A high validity environment, meaning there are real, learnable regularities that actually predict outcomes.
2. Enough repetition with feedback to learn them.

Where those fail (low validity, no feedback), "intuition" is just confident noise. Their own example was long range stock pickers and pundits, who feel certain and are wrong.

Here is the punchline: the dual flywheel is the machine that manufactures both conditions. Ad performance is a reasonably high validity environment, because hooks and novelty and segment fit do predict response. And the Analysis stage, where you saturate on winners and losers, is the feedback loop that intuition requires. So your taste here is not woo. It is expert pattern recognition, and it is valid specifically because the loop gives it the two things it needs: real regularities and feedback. Skip Analysis and your "taste" is just bias, confident and wrong. That is the deep reason you cannot skip the front of the wheel.

One honest boundary, and stating it makes the claim stronger, not weaker: intuition is compressed past experience, so it fails on regime change, when the algorithm shifts or a segment saturates faster than you can re saturate. That is exactly why you keep the explicit judgment lane running right next to taste. You trust the gut in a stable game, and you fall back to explicit criteria when the game changes underneath you.

Put judgment and taste together, feed them back every cycle, and they compound into instinct, the one of one thing nobody can copy off you, not even an AI on the same model.

---

## The circuit (and the loops that keep it alive)

Here is strategy as one clean circuit:

[image.png]

The thing strategy produces is not this week's orders. It is the **Strategy Map**: the living picture of every segment, what you have covered, and where the openings are. Strategy governs from the top, it does not turn with the wheel. It draws the terrain. Turning that terrain into this week's actual batch is Analysis's job, not strategy's.

And here is a clean way to hold the difference between the two: Strategy is the slow, governing map, and Analysis is the fast, loop closing stage that keeps redrawing it. Strategy points. Analysis turns and reports back. Strategy barely changes week to week, because you do not redraw the whole map every Monday. You let the loop edit it for you.

Which brings up the most important thing about the map: it is not a one time drawing. It is alive, and a few loops keep it that way.

- **The map grows.** Reading comments and reviews surfaces new outcomes you did not have, and those add whole new columns to the spine. The territory itself gets bigger as you work it. Most people only ever update coverage. The good ones grow the terrain.
- **Gaps re open.** A cell you covered and won fatigues, competitors copy it, and it decays right back into a gap. So exploring is never "done." It is permanent maintenance.
- **The competitor picture refreshes.** A blue ocean gap is defined by what competitors are not doing, and they move, so you refresh against the market, not just against your own ads.
- **Your judgment calibrates.** When a gap you ranked high flops, or a low one pops, that should correct the weights you put on your own criteria. So your logic sharpens, not just your taste.

That is the dual flywheel showing up right here in Strategy. The **system lane** is the Strategy Map itself, a Foundations artifact the loop keeps current. The **human lane** is the segment model in your head and your sharpening ranking criteria, the instinct that lets you rank gaps in seconds instead of grinding the grid.

---

## Pulling strategy together

Strategy is the map of what you could make, and the goal is dead simple: a healthy, diverse account full of winning ads that scale.

You hold it in four moves: map, gaps, bet, decider. Segments are the biggest lever, and a segment is just an outcome spine with facets hanging off it, sized into clean named slices, with awareness riding on top. You overlay your real ads to find the gaps, you decide explore versus exploit, and you pick with chad logic and taste, which compound into instinct over time.

And remember, this is my system, not the system. It is idealized on purpose, and you should want to take it apart and rebuild it your way. What strategy hands forward is the Strategy Map, and the moment you sit down to turn that map into this week's actual ads, you have stepped into Analysis. Which is exactly where we go next.

# Stage 2 · Analysis: the full map, beginning to end

## Where analysis sits

Strategy drew the terrain. Analysis is where the wheel actually starts turning.

This is the start of the process. You sit down, you read what your account actually did, and you let that read plus your strategy feed back and forth until you know what you are going to make next, decided by your judgment (chad logic) and your taste (instinct). You are taking a pile of ads and turning it into two things at once: a sharper gut, and a set of real hypotheses about what is working.

But here is the thing to hold onto, because it is the whole reason this stage matters: you are not just reading numbers. You are saturating your brain. Analysis is doing two jobs at the exact same time. It is training you, so your gut gets sharper, and it is filling your system, so your hypotheses pile up for the AI to use later. Same ads, two jobs. That is the dual flywheel showing up right here at the front.

So the one line to keep: read your account until you can feel what to do.

## This stage bends to fit you

Before anything else, know that Analysis is not one fixed procedure. It bends to you: your strategy, your KPIs, how you run your account. People do this differently and that is correct, not sloppy. So the things that flex:

[image.png]

- **Your KPI**: what does "winning" even mean to you? Spend? ROAS (return on ad spend, how much revenue you get back per dollar in)? Some specific mix? Everything downstream sorts against this, so it has to be set first.
- **Your data**: pulled straight from the Meta API, dropped in as a reporting export, or fed in as the reports plus the copy of the ads themselves. However it arrives, the point is the same: now all your data is in one place.
- **How you bracket**: more on this in a second.
- **Your mix**: how heavy you go on net new versus variations versus scaling.

None of that is assumed for you. It is yours to set.

## Bracketing: a lens for your brain, not a science

The first move is to bracket your ads into buckets so the patterns jump out. There are a bunch of ways to do it:

[image.png]

- winners / almost winners / losers
- all time winners / winners / ads with traction / losers
- breakthrough / KPI winner / spend winner / loser

And here is the important part: do not agonize over which taxonomy is "right." The bracket is a human artifact. It exists for exactly one reason, which is to make the patterns legible to your brain so your taste can grab them. An AI does not need brackets at all. It can regress on the raw metrics and find the patterns in the numbers directly. You are the one who needs them.

So the bracket is a free variable. Tune it to your KPIs and your strategy, and do not try to make it perfect or universal. The only rule is that it has to sort by something your KPI actually cares about, so the patterns you "feel" are real signal and not noise. It is the same move you made with Concept and Angle back in Strategy: loose on purpose.

## Saturating: the part that trains your gut

Now you look at your winners and your losers inside those buckets. And you do not stop at your own account. You widen out and take in everything: organic, competitors, the zeitgeist, the comments, your ads, your metrics.

This alone, before you have written a single hypothesis, is training your intuition. This is the saturation. This is you living in the accounts until the patterns are in your bones.

And remember why this actually works, from the taste science back in Strategy: intuition only becomes real when there are genuine patterns to learn and you get feedback on whether you were right. This stage is where the feedback lives. Skip it and your "taste" is just confident guessing.

## Two reads happen here, and you keep them separate

[image.png]

This is the part people muddy, so I want to be sharp about it. There are two different reads going on in Analysis, and they are not the same thing. Jamming them into one word is what makes this stage feel slippery.

**Read one: Hypotheses (backward, your account).** Why did this ad win? Why did this one lose? Why is this thing not even here? These are explanatory, they are about your own ads and your own results, and "hypotheses" is exactly the right word for them.

This is the natural place to drop a bot. You let it work through each ad and develop a specific hypothesis, and you store those hypotheses in a database you can come back to, maybe with a RAG or chat layer on top so you can question it and figure things out together. That hypotheses bank is a Foundations piece, one of the stores every later stage gets to lean on.

One honest note: this is not a science, and it should not be. The platform and the market are always shifting underneath you, you cannot isolate every variable, and perfect attribution is a fantasy. What you want is chunky, reliable enough claims you can actually test, like "the cortisol mechanism is carrying the joint ads," or "unaware long form beats short form for menopause." Big chunky ideas and the overall trend, the things you can actually feel and act on.

**Read two: Openings (forward, everywhere).** Where do I aim next? This one is wider, and a lot of it is external: what competitors just opened up, what is trending organically, what the comments are screaming for, plus your structural gaps coming down from the Strategy Map. These are not "hypotheses about why." They are bets about where.

Here is the thing that makes it click: the external stuff does not feed your hypotheses. People try to cram competitor analysis into "hypotheses" and it feels wrong because it is wrong. The external read runs in parallel and meets your hypotheses one layer downstream. Three streams converge into one place:

- **Gaps** are structural and fairly static, the things you do not have on the map.
- **Hypotheses** are backward and yours, why your stuff moved.
- **External signals** are live and time sensitive, what is hot right now.

All three land in one ranked list of **openings**, your actual this week bets. A hypothesis generates an opening ("cortisol carries the joint ads, so test cortisol on heart and hormones, that is a super segment opening"). External signals add openings that never touched your win and loss data at all. Same list.

And you save both banks into the AI: your hypotheses to explain things and write from later, your openings to prioritize from.

## How the plan actually settles: it is an oscillation

Now, the clean version of the story says Analysis spits out a Production Plan, hands it to Ideation, and we are done. That is a lie, and you can already feel it. Analysis and Ideation ping pong back and forth until the briefs settle.

[image.png]

Two directions the loop runs, and every brief is born one of these two ways:

- **Gap led (the plan pulls).** "I need three menopause unaware ads," so you go hunting in Ideation to fill that order. Plan first, ideas serve it.
- **Idea led (the idea pushes).** You go out, run the STORMING process, stumble on a banger, and you just let it rip. The idea pushes back up and amends the plan. Idea first, plan bends to it.

Both are completely legitimate. Sometimes you walk in knowing you need an angle to support a specific bucket. Sometimes you just want to go look at ideas and let what you find define what you make. So you bounce: strategy told you what you are up to, analysis primed your brain on what is working and what is not, you have got hypotheses and a sense of the gaps, and now you are cross referencing all of that against fresh ideas, back and forth, until something settles.

Two things keep that bouncing from going forever:

1. **Your inventory is the clock.** "I need N ads this week, roughly this allocation." Once your plan plus the seeds you have found cover N, you stop and write briefs. The number is what ends the ping pong. That is what makes this decisions under constraints instead of endless wandering.
2. **The plan serves the goal, never the reverse.** The allocation mix guides you, it never gets stamped onto a seed. Because at the end of the day you just want winning ads, so if a great idea breaks the plan, the plan yields.

## What comes out

Two things, and name them cleanly so they never blur:

- **The Production Plan**: this week's concrete batch. "I am making this many ads, of these types, at these awareness levels, to fill these specific gaps." Your inventory for the week, and the thing that flows into Ideation and shapes how you look at every source there.
- **The Allocation Mix**: how that batch splits across **net new** (the priority, a genuinely new ad from a raw idea), **variations** (off your winners), and **scaling** (really a media buying lever). How heavy you go on each is your call.

One honest note before it ships forward: the Production Plan goes forward into making ads. It does not loop back into the platform. The results of those ads come back to Analysis later, through the big loop. So Analysis is a snapshot with open doors, not a closed circle.

## The circuit

Here is the whole stage as one circuit:

[image.png]

Under the hood, the wiring is simple: ad data flows in from your reports or the platform, a classifier scores it by performance and spend, that feeds the board where you bracket it, a bot reads each ad and writes a hypothesis, and the hypotheses land in the database with its chat layer. You sit next to that database with a two way connection, feeding it and questioning it, and that is where instinct forms.

Two things to notice. The first is the **⇄ between Analysis and Ideation**, which is the oscillation, not a clean handoff. The second is the node's real signature: Analysis has **one clean output going forward** (the plan to Ideation) but **four wires looping back or up** (results coming in from shipped ads, coverage going up to Strategy, the oscillation with Ideation, and the hypotheses bank feeding itself next cycle). That ratio is the tell. This node's real job is not to produce. It is to close the loop and aim the next turn. It is the one place in the whole system where the big wheel both ends and restarts.

And look at the two things this node feeds at once: hypotheses going into the database, and taste going into your gut. That is the dual flywheel rendered as plain wiring. Every cycle through here, the system gets richer and the human gets sharper, from the same ads, at the same time. Which is exactly why Analysis is the one stage you can never skip.

## Pulling analysis together

Analysis is the start, the moment the wheel turns.

You bracket your account into a lens that fits you, you saturate your brain on winners and losers and everything around them, and you run two reads: hypotheses (why your stuff won or lost, backward, yours) and openings (where to aim next, forward, pulled from your hypotheses plus what competitors and the market and your gaps are telling you). Then you and Ideation bounce back and forth, gap led and idea led, until your inventory target converges it all into a settled Production Plan with its Allocation Mix.

That plan is what Ideation picks up to build the briefs. And the whole time, you are getting sharper while the system is getting richer.

That is analysis. Next is Ideation, where those openings turn into seeds, and seeds turn into briefs.

# Stage 3 · Ideation: the full map, beginning to end

## Where ideation sits

Analysis handed you a Production Plan. Ideation is where you go find the ideas to actually fill it.

This is where creative diversity gets generated, so it is a big one. And the one thing to hold onto is this: you do not invent in a vacuum. You pull from everywhere, and that pulling is what gives you variety almost for free.

Pull wide, save everything, brief the best.

## The two modes: pointed and open

When you sit down to hunt for ideas, you are always in one of two modes.

Sometimes you walk in **pointed**. You need an ad for a specific gap, say three menopause unaware ads, so you go get them. The plan is steering you.

Sometimes you walk in **open**. You are just fishing for bangers, and a great one you did not see coming bends your whole plan toward it. You found something too good to ignore, so you let it rip.

Most sessions are a mix of both. You spear fish for what you need, and you cast a net for what you did not know you wanted. And either way, pointed or open, you are seeing all of it through your own taste and through what your analysis just taught you. That is why the same swipe lands differently for you than it does for the next person. Your eye is doing the filtering the whole time, whether you notice it or not.

## Why you pull from sources instead of mapping it all

Here is the temptation. You want variety in your concepts and angles, so you try to build a big map: a paradoxical question, a problem, a unique mechanism, and you make sure you hit them all. We have a lot of those maps and templates and they genuinely work.

But mapping the whole space of possible creative is a fool's errand. Any map you draw is already incomplete, because there is always one more thing out there you did not think of. So you engineer diversity a different way. Instead of mapping the space, you pull from live sources and let the variety come along for the ride.

That is what STORMING is.

## STORMING: the eight sources

STORMING is the master concept, one letter per source, easy to hold in your head:

[image.png]

- **S: Swipes.** Other people's winning ads, borrowed. Tools: ScrapeCreators (paste an ad link and it pulls the text and media back out), the Meta Ad Library, Hookd, Get Hooked. A swipe bot can Mad Lib it for you.
- **T: Templates.** Your internal map of proven concepts, structures, and beats. The template first side. Tool: the template bots we provide.
- **O: Organic.** Ideas from organic feeds, where the incentives are different and things move fast. Curate who you follow, screenshot and paste posts directly, or scrape the comments through the Meta API.
- **R: Research.** Everything you can read: your own ads, reviews, comments. Tool: a scraper like Apify, plus your own ad reports.
- **M: Matrix.** Your chad logic pointed straight at the identified gaps, pouring ideas into them. Fed by the Strategy Map.
- **I: Internal vectors.** Patterns pulled from your own past winners. Tool: a bot that analyzes them, and we have some of these built for Genesis already.
- **N: New styles.** Fresh visual styles you spot and save as you go. Feeds your visual styles database.
- **G: Gambits.** Wildcard plays, the longer shots. Fed by your hypotheses and your comments.

A few of those look inward (Templates, Matrix, and Internal vectors all run off stuff you already own), and the rest reach outward into the world. The trick is not to make these perfectly clean and separate. They overlap on purpose. You just pull from all of them, and the diversity falls out. Many different wells means many different flavors of water.

## STORMING also sharpens your taste

Here is the part people miss. STORMING does not just produce ideas. While you are doing it, looking at swipes, reading organic, digging through comments, it is training your taste. That makes Ideation the second place in the whole system where you saturate your brain, right alongside Analysis.

[image.png]

And the two saturations are different on purpose:

- **Analysis saturates you on performance.** Your own account, graded by real results. It answers what worked.
- **STORMING saturates you on possibility.** The whole world, raw and ungraded. It answers what could we try.

So your taste gets fed from two directions: the truth of what is already winning, and the wide open space of what is out there. Both flywheels turn here, same as Analysis. The seeds and the swipe banks fill the system, the looking sharpens the human. Two jobs, one activity.

## The engine and the seeds

Then comes the engine. All your sources, plus your own instinct, feed an ideation engine, which is just a Bot, a skill file you hand the pulled material and your Production Plan, and it spits out raw concepts. Runs right inside Claude Code.

[image.png]

Those concepts become seeds, and every single seed gets saved into a seed bank. The seed bank is a Database, another piece of your Foundations, so start it as the simplest thing that works:

- One markdown file is plenty on day one.
- Step it up to an Airtable or a spreadsheet when it gets clunky, so you can see and tag them.
- Only move to a real backend like Convex or Supabase if a screen ever needs to read them live.

You save everything so you can run operations across all your past ideas later, like asking the bank to resurface every seed you ever had for a given segment.

One discipline that matters here: a seed is small. It is the verbatim hook or swipe plus a one line description of the idea, and nothing else. You are not copywriting at this stage. The moment you start writing the ad inside the seed, you have jumped the gun. Generate a big pile of cheap seeds first, because you can always come up with ideas, then go pick the best.

[image.png]

## The brief: the big inflection point

This is the moment the whole stage turns.

[image.png]

Everything up to now has been divergent: pull wide, save everything, build a big pile. The brief is where it goes convergent. You stop gathering and you commit, and a cloud of seeds collapses into one ad with fixed DNA. That is why the brief is the inflection point, and it is really two moves.

First you **SELECT.** Out of all those seeds, which ones survive? This is judgment and taste again, the exact same decision you made in Strategy when you ranked gaps, just at a finer grain. Strategy picked which gaps. The brief picks which actual ad. Same engine, sharper resolution.

Then you **SPECIFY.** For each seed you are promoting, you lock its DNA. Before any ad gets written, you decide:

1. **Segment**: who it is for.
2. **Awareness level**: where they are in the journey. (This one basically picks which primer you will load too, though that part lands down in writing.)
3. **Mechanism**: the mechanism of action you want it to lean on.
4. **CTA**: what you are driving them to do, and where.
5. **Guidelines and steering**: how you want it handled. Your direction.

And one more thing the brief carries that is easy to forget but is honestly the most important field: the **source**, plus the reference links. Source matters most because it routes the work:

- If it is a **swipe**, you Mad Lib it. Keep it nearly the same, near the same length, just swap the specifics, and send it to the swipe bot.
- If it is **organic**, you keep the exact hook and write the rest fresh from there.
- **Everything else** runs the regular path.

So the full brief is the seed, plus those five decisions, plus the source and links. The five decisions are what to say and to whom. The source is how to handle it. A brief builder Bot assembles it all into a clean, consistent format, every brief gets saved to a brief store (another Database, same simple to fancy ladder as the seed bank), and the finished briefs flow out to Copy.

Why slow down on all this? Because the brief is the last cheap decision. Once you are in Copy you are writing, and changing the segment or the mechanism now means rewriting from scratch. Everything downstream inherits the brief, and after this point changes cost money. So this is where you take your time.

## It is an oscillation, not a clean handoff

One honest thing about this whole stage: Ideation and Analysis do not run in a tidy line. They bounce.

Pointed sessions pull from the plan. Open sessions push a banger back up and amend the plan. You cross reference your seeds against your analysis, back and forth, until something settles.

Two things keep that from going forever:

- **Your inventory is the clock.** Once your plan plus the seeds you have found cover the number of ads you need, you stop and write briefs.
- **The plan serves the goal, never the reverse.** The allocation mix guides you, it never gets stamped onto a seed. Because at the end of the day you just want winning ads, so if a great idea breaks the plan, the plan yields.

## Two branches worth noticing

Not everything from Ideation flows straight to Copy:

- **The visual styles database** keeps filling up from the organic and swipes you look at, and that one branches forward to Creative, not Copy. You are stocking the visual shelves while you hunt for copy ideas.
- **New outcomes loop back up to Strategy.** When STORMING surfaces a brand new outcome or segment nobody was serving, that grows the map, the same way Analysis does. Operating the system keeps expanding the territory.

## The circuit

[image.png]

Two things to see. The **⇄ with Analysis**, which is the oscillation, not a one way arrow. And the **funnel shape**: wide intake on the left, a big seed pile in the middle, necking down to a single committed brief on the right. The brief is the throat, the place the widest node in the whole system necks down to one decision. That is the structural reason it is the inflection point.

## Pulling ideation together

Ideation is where you go find the ideas to fill your plan, and you do it by pulling from everywhere instead of trying to map the whole space, because mapping it all is a fool's errand.

You hunt in two modes, pointed when you need a specific thing, open when you are fishing for bangers, and either way your taste and your fresh analysis are filtering the whole time. STORMING gives you the sources, and working it sharpens your taste while it fills your banks. You generate a big pile of small seeds, save every one, and then comes the big moment: the brief, where you select what survives and specify exactly who it is for, how it works, and where it is driving. The last cheap decision before the writing starts.

That is ideation. Next is Copy, where the brief finally becomes words.

# Stage 4 · Copy: the full map, beginning to end

## Where copy sits, and why it is the easy one

The brief comes in. Copy is where it finally becomes words.

People think this is the whole job. It is actually the easiest stage in the system, and it is worth knowing why: it is the same move you have already done three times. Strategy selects gaps, Ideation selects seeds, the brief selects an ad, and Copy is that exact same generate wide then select move, just at the finest grain. Sentences instead of segments. There is nothing new to learn here, you already know the shape.

## The unit: brief + primer + writing bot

The whole stage runs on one repeatable unit:

[image.png]

> a brief, a primer, and a writing bot = ads.

You feed the machine an idea, you push it a few times, you pick the best. That is it. And the magic is that you can take any source of an idea and turn it into ads, because the unit does not care where the idea came from.

## Why the primer and the bot work together

Here is the thing people get stuck on: why does putting a primer in front of a bot work so much better than just asking it to write? You do not have to take it on faith, because there is a real reason, and it is the reason every primer in the whole system works.

[image.png]

A primer shows instead of tells. If you describe good copy to the bot, like "write a punchy, emotional hook," it averages that into mush, because those words mean a thousand things. But if you show it five hooks that already won, it imitates the actual pattern, including all the tacit stuff you could not put into words if you tried. Models are far better at copying a concrete example than following an abstract instruction.

That is exactly why you feed it winning ads and not a buyer doc or a pile of product facts. The information already worked, so it is the least you need and the most that helps. It is even worth writing some of these by hand, because those will be your most human versions. Spend real time here. And every single time you get a winner, you add it, so the primer tightens over time.

The deeper point: the primer is your taste, written down. Your gut is compressed experience held in your head, and the primer is compressed experience held in markdown. Same thing, different storage. That is literally the system lane of the dual flywheel, and it is why the primer is the heart of your whole Foundations layer.

[image.png]

Your primer library ends up looking something like this:

- A long form primer for unaware and problem aware ads (the 500 to 1500 word, story driven ones).
- A long form primer for solution and product aware ads (built on comparison and offer).
- A short form one for punchy text ads.
- One for video ad copy meant to be performed on camera.
- One for full video scripts, and one for AI animation scripts.
- One for story based ads.
- One for most aware ads with direct offers and urgency.
- Plus a hook primer and a headline primer.

You lean heaviest on the two long form ones. And in tool terms none of this is fancy: a primer is just Knowledge, a markdown file. A bot is just a skill file. Both sit in your folder, both run in Claude Code.

## One quick fork: what shape is this

When the brief lands, there is one small decision before you write, and it comes down entirely to which primer you load.

For most things (statics, standard direct response ads) it is just long form copy, so you grab your long form payload primer and go. Your awareness level, which you already locked back in the brief, basically picks that payload primer for you.

The one place it gets different is video. Some video ideas have a specific conceit, like a customer call, a cue card ad, or a thumb fight, and the conceit changes how the script is written. So for those you load a conceit primer on top, because pouring the message into that shape is a different animal than writing a long form advertorial. Statics usually do not need this, because they are long form copy that becomes an image later. Video conceits do.

So the fork is small but real: standard copy uses a payload primer, and a conceit driven video uses a conceit primer too. The primer is the format choice, and it is the one real decision left inside an otherwise mechanical stage. (The visual format, the actual look and actor and render, gets decided later in Creative. Here you are only choosing the shape of the words.)

## The principle: generate and select, do not one shot

This is the most important habit in the stage, and it runs through everything: you do not write the ad. You run a generator and you select.

[image.png]

The bot is cheap, stochastic variation. Push it twice and you get two different outputs. Your judgment is the selector. Speed comes from generating wide and picking the best, not from trying to nail it in one shot.

It feels backwards, but one shotting is actually slower. You burn time wrestling a single output toward "right" when you could have generated five and recognized the winner in seconds. Recognition is faster than construction. So you run things a few times, sometimes in parallel with two different bots, and you let your taste choose. And every time you choose, that is another rep training your eye, so this is not just about quality. It is the human flywheel turning at the smallest grain.

## The fixed order: hooks, then body, then headlines

There is an order, and you always follow it, because each piece constrains the next. You lock the hardest, most determining thing first and let it cascade down.

[image.png]

- **Hooks first.** Hook bot, plus hook primer, plus the brief. Then push it two or three times, because no matter how good the first batch looks, you have to push it, since good hooks are genuinely hard and they take iterations. Then you pick. This is the most important gate in the whole stage, because the hook is the most important part of the ad, and once it is locked you can trust the machine through the rest if you want.
- **Body second.** Take your chosen hook plus the brief into a writing bot running the body primer, something like the Mario bot or one of your own. The body follows directly from that specific hook, which is the whole reason hooks went first. Write a great body and then go hunting for a hook and you are working backwards. Run it in parallel, usually two bots at once, two to four times each, because one is just going to come out better. Then you pick.
- **Headlines last.** Feed your finished bodies into a headline bot with the headline primer, and pick the body and headline pair that go together.

Put it together and you have got your selections document, and that heads to Editing.

## The flywheel: the part that compounds

Here is what makes Copy more than a one time machine. Every time you get a winner in a category, it goes back into that primer as a fresh example plus updated dos and don'ts. So the next ad you write of that type starts from a better place than the last one. And the next. And the next.

That is the system lane compounding, right inside your primers. The machine gets better and better and better, on its own, just from you feeding it your winners.

One thing to notice, though: that loop is a long one. The winner that sharpens your hook primer had to go all the way around the wheel first, written here, edited, turned into creative, shipped, and flagged as a winner back in Analysis, before it loops home to the primer. So the primer flywheel is not local to Copy. It is the whole system feeding this one stage's foundation.

## The circuit

[image.png]

The shape to notice: three identical stages, primer then bot then gate, chained so each one's pick feeds the next. It is the most repetitive shape in the whole system, which is exactly why it is the easiest to stand up. It needs no Connections and no Surfaces, just primers, bots, and your gates. And the one wire underneath it all is winners climbing back into the primers.

## Pulling copy together

Copy is brief to hooks to body to headlines, and the unit under all of it is a brief plus a primer plus a writing bot.

The primer works because it shows winning examples instead of describing them, which is your taste written down. You pick the primer to set the shape (long form for most things, a conceit primer for video), then you generate wide and let your taste select instead of one shotting, because recognition is faster than construction. You write hooks first and push them hard, body in parallel off the chosen hook, headlines off the body. And every winner you ever ship climbs back into the primer, so the machine gets better and better and better.

Out comes a clean selections document, headed for Editing.

# Stage 5 · Editing: the full map, beginning to end

## Where editing sits

Copy handed you a selections document. Editing is where you push that ad until it is right, and then finish it clean.

[image.png]

First, the honest part: a lot of the time you skip this entirely. The ad one shots out of Copy and it is good. This stage is for when you want control, when you want a hand on the final thing instead of trusting the machine all the way through. Some people always edit, some almost never do, and both are completely fine.

## The canvas

The setup is simple. You have a canvas, which is really just one of your favorite writing bots, something like the Mario bot, and you drop the ad you selected into it. Now you edit from there. That is the whole apparatus: a good writing bot, your ad inside it, and your primers loaded.

The canvas can live wherever you like, inside Poe (an app that lets you chat with a bunch of different bots in one place), inside Claude Code if it calls the bot, or on the Genesis server. Any solid writing bot works.

And the primers earn their keep here too. A lot of what I do is literally drop the ad in and say "make it more like my primer." Same trick as Copy. The primer is your winning pattern, your taste written down, so "pull it toward the primer" is really "pull it toward what already works."

## Three ways to steer

Once the ad is in the canvas, you push it until it is right, three ways, and you stack them however you like:

[image.png]

- **By hand.** You just edit it yourself.
- **By voice.** You talk your edits in with a voice tool like Wispr Flow, which types out whatever you say. You fire off what you want fixed, rapid fire, and then polish.
- **By button.** You drop in a steer prompt, a little saved instruction for the things that come up constantly: make it more natural when it is too AI sounding, add more bounce, shorten it and cut the fluff, simplify the reading level, make it better, fix the transitions, fix the logic, fix the CTA, find and replace. Each one is just a tiny markdown file you keep on hand and pop in when you need it.

And here is the thing that ties voice and buttons together: a steer prompt is just a voice note you have banked. You say "make it less AI" out loud enough times, you stop saying it and save it as a button. So the buttons are not a separate tool, they are your repeated voice notes, crystallized.

The stack I have personally found most effective is exactly this: take an ad I wrote, drop it into the Mario bot with my primer loaded, then run voice notes through Wispr Flow plus steer prompts to dial it in. That is the fast way to really optimize a piece.

## The two loops: this is the whole point

Now the part that makes editing more than a chore. Every edit you make does not just fix this one ad. You take the taste you just applied and you bake it back into your foundations, in two directions:

[image.png]

- **Your edits become new steer prompts.** The thing you kept doing by voice becomes a button. So editing gets cheaper every time.
- **Your feedback becomes new primer rules.** Whatever you told the bot gets written back into the primer as a fresh do or don't. So the next draft comes out better, and there is less to fix in the first place.

Those two loops attack the problem from opposite ends. One drives down the cost of editing. The other drives down the need for it. Which means this stage is quietly trying to put itself out of a job: the better your primers and steer prompts get, the more your ads just one shot and skip editing entirely. That is the deep reason editing is both optional and worth doing. Every edit you make is buying down future editing.

And you do not have to do this banking by hand. You set up an after session skill, a bot that runs when you are done, takes all the voice notes and feedback from that session, and writes them into both places automatically: new buttons into your steer prompts, new rules into your primers. So your editing literally upgrades your own toolkit while you work, and a saved edit rules database sits underneath holding it all.

## Finish

When the editing is done, you finish the ad. Make new hooks from the edited version using your hook primer, pick one. Make new headlines with your headline primer, pick the pair. Format the whole thing into a clean document. Then it goes to Creative.

## The circuit

[image.png]

Two wires to notice, and they are the soul of the stage: the edits looping back into the steer prompts and into the primers. Everything else is just a bot with your ad in it.

And here is the twist worth naming. The edits-to-steer-prompts loop is a self loop, the stage feeding its own buttons. But the edits-to-primers loop feeds upstream, into Copy, which makes Editing the one node whose feedback flows backward. It improves the stage before it, so the next ad arrives needing less editing. And both of these loops are fast, because you bank the rule the moment the session ends. Contrast that with Copy's primer flywheel, which is the slowest loop in the system, since a winner has to lap the whole wheel first. Same primer, two speeds: Editing sharpens it tonight, Copy's winners sharpen it a full lap later.

So Editing is the optional polish node whose real, durable output is not the ad. It is the smarter foundation. It is the one node designed to shrink itself toward zero.

## Pulling editing together

Editing is pushing the ad until it is right, by hand, by voice, or by button, inside a canvas that is just your favorite writing bot with the primer loaded.

Most of the time you do not even need it, because the ad one shots. But when you do, you steer it, and remember that a steer prompt is only a voice note you have banked. The magic is the two loops: your edits become new buttons so editing gets cheaper, and new primer rules so the drafts get better, and an after session skill bakes both back in for you. Then you finish with fresh hooks and headlines, format the doc, and send it to Creative.

# Stage 6 · Creative: the full map, beginning to end

## Where creative sits

Editing handed you a finished ad. Creative is where those words become statics and video, and then ship.

The big idea, and it is the one to hold: you take one message and you pour it into many shapes. Some are statics, some are videos. The message underneath stays the same, and the shape keeps changing.

[image.png]

And here is why that matters: the format itself is the new mechanism. A shape nobody has seen resets the clock twice over, once for the human brain, which novelty grabs, and once for the platform, which cannot slot your new thing next to your old winners, so it goes and finds a fresh audience for you. That is the novelty machine, manufacturing new shapes faster than the market can copy them.

## Why visual diversity actually works: the fingerprint

Before the tracks, the mechanism underneath, because it governs everything here.

Every time you upload an ad, the platform scans what it looks like and gives it a fingerprint, an entity ID. If a new ad looks too much like one you are already running, it gets grouped with it: same fingerprint, no new reach. If it looks genuinely different, it gets its own fingerprint, and its own clean shot at its own audience.

[image.png]

So creative diversity is not a vibe. It is literally how many distinct fingerprints you have. Fifty ads that look alike are five ads wearing different tablecloths. Five that look genuinely different reach five different pockets of people.

And here is the kicker: the system reads the visual, not the words. So you can put the same proven copy on five different looking shapes and get five different fingerprints, each finding its own audience. That is the whole reason this stage exists. Every new shape is another shot at another audience, and the job here is to manufacture genuinely different looking shapes for a message that is already proven.

[image.png]

## Two tracks, same shape

Statics and video both run the same three beats:

1. You already have the message.
2. You choose its shape.
3. You render it.

The only thing that differs between the two tracks is the shape engine. Let's take them one at a time.

## The statics track

Statics come in three types, and the real axis is not awareness, it is how much the ad announces itself as an ad:

[image.png]

- **Native**: hides that it is an ad at all. Looks like a real post in the feed.
- **Templated**: a familiar ad shape, a proven layout you reuse.
- **Branded**: openly a product ad, your hero shots.

That is a disguise spectrum, and it loosely tracks the funnel (native up top, branded at the bottom), but that mapping is a best guess, not a law. A native ad can be solution aware, a branded ad can land on someone unaware. So do not lock the type to the awareness level.

### Native: an idea engine

Native is the interesting one, and it is an idea engine, the same generate and select move you already know. You pull from a bunch of sources, and for each one you spray out a big rough pile and keep only the ones that stop your thumb.

[image.png]

It is approximate on purpose. Figure roughly a 20 percent hit rate, so you generate 80 to 200 rough concepts and keep the 15 or 20 that actually stop you. Spread your shots, do not cluster them. And push each source two or three passes, because the first pass is boring and passes two and three are where it gets real.

The actual mechanic of making one is simple and worth saying plainly: a reference image plus a hyper realistic prompt is what gives you your best images. The reference is the single biggest quality lever. You go find a real, native looking image (usually Google Images, "reddit" plus your topic) and feed it in alongside the prompt. The prompt itself is built to look real: ultra realistic, with a named camera ("shot on an iPhone front camera," "point and shoot with direct flash"), because the camera spec is what sells "a real person took this." Render it native to the feed. Sometimes you do not even need a reference and the prompt alone is enough. The reference is just the strongest lever when you want it.

[image.png]

Those sources are SCRAWLS, and notice this is the same move as STORMING back in Ideation, just one layer down. STORMING pulls message sources, SCRAWLS pulls visual sources. Same pull wide and let diversity fall out logic, so do not agonize over making the seven perfectly separate. The overlap is the point.

- **S: Swipe.** Find swipes (Meta Ad Library, ScrapeCreators, Hookd, Get Hooked), then a prompt reverse engineers one, uses it as a reference, and ships a new version. Renders through an image generator like KIE (which runs GPT-Image and Nano Banana) or Higgsfield.
- **C: Copy derived.** A prompt analyzes the important features of your own ad, the copy blocks and objects, and turns those into a static.
- **R: Reptile triggers.** A prompt keyed on primal, reptile brain triggers, the raw stuff that grabs attention before thinking kicks in: faces, food, danger, novelty.
- **A: Audience derived.** You take the comments (pulled through the Meta API or Apify) and derive a static straight from real audience language.
- **W: Wildcard.** You scrape what is out there in the wild (something like Apify) and riff on it.
- **L: Loopback.** You keep a vector of all your winning ads (a Database) and run operations on it to spin out new variants.
- **S: Source.** You start from a raw source asset, a real photo, a product shot, a screenshot, a piece of customer footage, and build the static around that real material.

### Templated and branded

These two are simpler.

[image.png]

**Templated** is a fill the slots prompt. You build the layout once (comparison, before and after, infographic, testimonial), then fill the slots and re render it forever, pulling from your visual and template database.

**Branded** is the same engine pointed at your product, and this is the one you cannot fake. You need the real product info and assets first: the founder, the brand colors, the logo, real product shots, and the right aspect ratio.

## The video track

Video runs the same three beats, message then shape then render, but the shape decision is its own thing.

You already have the message, and for video it lives as a script. Then comes the one real choice: the conceit. A conceit is the social shape the ad mimics, like a customer call, a street interview, cue cards, a thumb fight. The thing to understand is that the conceit IS the script's shape, not a separate step. Writing "in a conceit" just means writing the script that way: a customer call script has two voices, a cue card script is one beat per card.

And it is optional. Sometimes you just take a straight talking head or VSL script and produce it, no special conceit. Sometimes you pour the message into a distinctive shape, and that is the novelty move. It can come from either direction: you reshape your message into a conceit, or you saw a thumb fight ad and swiped the shape first. A creativity bot can invent new conceits for you. You feed it what is working in organic and in ads, it abstracts the shape, and each new conceit it finds becomes its own primer in your Foundations.

### The four axes: CAST

[image.png]

The conceit is the most generative piece, but it is one of four axes, and the full framework is called CAST:

- **C: Conceit.** The social shape the ad mimics. This is where novelty actually lives.
- **A: Actor.** Who is on screen, and whose word you are trusting.
- **S: Style.** The visual substrate, like live action or AI animation.
- **T: Terrain.** Where it is set.

The old way of cutting video (human first versus voiceover versus animation) was off, because each of those was really keying off a different axis. CAST gives each its own axis, so you can decode any video into four coordinates, and you can generate brand new ones by picking a combination nobody has run.

### How CAST actually stacks: the examples

People throw these at you like a to do list: do podcast ads, do on stage ads, do Pixar ads, do skits, do street interviews. And it feels like a pile of unrelated trends you are somehow supposed to keep up with. The trick is that every one of those is just a point in the same four axis space. Once you see that, the overwhelm goes away. You are not chasing ten formats, you are turning four knobs.

[image.png]

First, decode the buzzwords. Every "type" people name is really just its CAST coordinates:

| "Just do ___ ads" | Conceit | Actor | Style | Terrain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Podcast ad | podcast | two hosts | live action | studio |
| On stage ad | keynote | founder / authority | live action | stage |
| Pixar ad | story / VSL | character | AI 3D | animated |
| Skit ad | skit | creator / peer | live action | native |
| Street interview | street interview | peers | live action | street |
| Customer call | phone call | peer + founder | live + screen cap | abstract |
| Thumb fight | thumb fight | hands / creator | live action | native |
| Talking head | talking head | founder / creator | live action | office |

That is the answer to "how does this all fit together." It all fits because it is all the same four dials. The formats people obsess over are just the popular coordinates.

Now the fun part: you can hold three dials and turn one, and watch it become a different ad every time. Start with a plain podcast ad:

> Podcast ad = podcast · two hosts · live action · studio.

- Swap the **Actor** and drop the founder in as the guest. Now it is a founder podcast interview.
- Swap the Actor again to a celebrity guest. Now it is a celebrity podcast ad.
- Swap the **Style** to claymation. Now it is a claymation podcast with the founder in it, same shape, same person, totally new look.
- Swap the **Terrain** and put it on a stage. Now it is a live podcast recorded on stage.
- Swap the **Conceit**, keep the founder and the claymation, change podcast to skit. Now it is a claymation skit starring the founder.

You just generated five distinct ads by turning one knob at a time, and stacking two swaps (claymation plus founder) invented "claymation podcast with the founder," a shape probably nobody has run. That is the novelty machine, and you can watch it work instead of guessing.

### Novelty vs scale: which knob you turned

The dials also tell you which kind of move you just made:

- Swapping **Conceit** or **Style** gives you a shape the market has not seen. That is **novelty**: a new fingerprint AND a reset sophistication clock.
- Swapping **Actor** or **Terrain** keeps the shape and gives you a fresh variant. That is **scale**: a new fingerprint of a familiar shape.

All four earn a new fingerprint, so all four buy new audience. But conceit and style do the extra thing, resetting the clock, because they are shapes nobody has seen. So in "claymation podcast with the founder," the founder is your scale knob and the claymation is your novelty knob, and you know exactly what each one is buying you.

### Producing it

Once the shape is set, the rest is production direction (Actor, Style, Terrain), and you apply your primers in sequence: the payload primer says what to say, the conceit primer says what shape to pour it into, and then Style, Actor, and Terrain are your production directions.

Then you produce. Avatar tools like HeyGen or Hedra turn a script into a talking person, and video generators like Runway, Kling, or Veo (Higgsfield when you want it stylized) make the footage and animation behind it.

## Ship

Both tracks meet at an optional creative gate, where a human steps in or waves it through. This is the natural place to stress test, to sanity check that a shape actually makes sense before you spend on it. Then you ship through the Meta API.

[image.png]

And the loop closes. Four wires go home, all feeding your Foundations:

- **Results** back to Analysis.
- **Winning ads** back to your primers.
- **New visual styles** back to your visual database.
- **New conceits** back to your conceit primers.

The whole system gets richer every single time you ship.

(Squeezing more out of a winner, re hooking it, swapping actor or terrain, re cutting it for a different awareness level, is its own thing. That is the Iterations engine, and it gets its own section coming up.)

## The circuit

[image.png]

Two things to see. Both tracks are the same beat, message then shape then render. And the four open wires going home: this is the node where the whole wheel closes back onto Analysis. It is the mirror image of Ideation. Ideation necked a whole wide world down to one brief, and Creative explodes one message back out into many shapes. It is also the most Connection heavy node in the system, because the render tools all live outside, and the only node with no forward handoff. It does not pass work to a next stage. It ships, and it returns.

## Pulling creative together

Creative is two tracks, statics and video, that both ship, and both run the same beat: you already have the message, you choose its shape, you render it.

It works because each genuinely different looking shape earns its own fingerprint and its own fresh audience. Statics come native, templated, or branded along a disguise spectrum, with native built as an idea engine: pull from SCRAWLS, feed a reference image plus a hyper realistic prompt, generate wide and keep the stoppers. Video runs a script whose shape is the conceit, optional and where the novelty lives, then Actor, Style, and Terrain to produce it. You push conceit and style to manufacture novelty, and you swap actor and terrain to scale.

Then an optional gate, ship through Meta, and four wires carry results, winners, styles, and conceits all the way home into your Foundations.

# The iteration engine: turning knobs on a winner

This one is not a turning stage like the six. It is the exploit half of the Bet from Strategy, the engine you run alongside the wheel whenever you have a winner worth squeezing.

Once you have a winner, you exploit it, and exploiting means turning knobs. (Net new is the explore half, and that lives up front in Ideation. This is the other half.) Every variation runs on one model: CASHED.

## CASHED: the knobs

[image.png]

One variation equals freeze every letter, turn exactly one:

- **C: Concept / Claim.** What you assert: the core idea plus its facts, proof, and mechanism.
- **A: Angle.** The psychological frame you wrap the claim in.
- **S: Style.** The visual: medium, look, who delivers it. (For video, this knob is the full CAST panel: Conceit, Actor, Style, Terrain.)
- **H: Hook.** The opening.
- **E: Edit.** Tone and format: length, intensity, register, pacing, CTAs.
- **D: Demographic / Who.** The whole segment. The biggest swing, and it borders net new.

Two disciplines make this work:

- **Decode before you turn.** Read the winner back into its CASHED settings first: what is the claim, the angle, the who, the look. You cannot freeze five and turn one if you do not know what the five are.
- **Find the smallest thing that moved.** Work big to small and stop at the first real change. New who is D, same who but new claim or mechanism is C, same claim but new frame is A, same claim and frame but new look is S, same everything but a new opening is H, and only tone or length is E. A small turn is cosmetic, a full turn becomes net new.

## Which knobs buy new audience

Remember the fingerprint from Creative. The platform reads the visual, so the six knobs do two different jobs:

- **Reach knobs (the algorithm game): D and S.** A new who forces a new looking ad, and a new look is new by definition. These earn fresh delivery, a new fingerprint, a new audience.
- **Conversion knobs (the psychology game): C, A, H, E.** They make the same audience convert harder, but on the same visual they do not earn new reach.

So CASHED splits right down the two games. The one catch: H and a visually loaded C tip into reach only if the visual opening actually changes (the first three seconds, the static's whole composition). A word only hook swap is the same fingerprint. That is also why E never rides alone, so fold it inside a bigger turn. The strongest variation turns a reach knob and a conversion knob together.

## The moves

Priority key: ◆ workhorse (your bread and butter turns) · ◇ situational (when the ad calls for it).

**Turn D, the who** (the highest judgment turn):

- **Restage ◆:** move the winner up or down the awareness ladder, mostly a hook plus problem depth change.
- **Blame Shift ◆:** point the same promise at people who pin the problem on a different cause (cortisol → inflammation → hormones).
- **Avatar Port ◇:** keep the winning script, re voice it for a different person (age, season of life, situation).

**Turn C, the claim** (new substance, same who):

- **New Why ◆:** explain the mechanism a fresh way, or swap in a different cause and effect story.
- **Proof Trade ◆:** swap the kind of evidence (lived experience ↔︎ data ↔︎ crowd ↔︎ expert).
- **Doubt Killer ◆:** take a real objection from the comments and dissolve it inside the story, woven in, never a list.
- **Pain Stretch ◇:** sit longer in the problem before you turn to the solution.
- **Fresh Facts ◇:** new specifics, numbers, or findings under the same claim.

**Turn A, the frame** (same claim, new doorway, the multiplication engine):

- **Frame Swap ◆:** hold the claim exactly, change the psychological doorway you walk them through.
- **Reverse Sell ◇:** pitch against your own interest ("skip this if…") to drop their guard.

**Turn S, the look** (this is where CAST lives):

- **Recast ◆:** a different person delivers the same script (Actor); vary age, gender, energy.
- **Relocate ◆:** same ad, new setting (Terrain).
- **Reshape ◆:** pour the winner into a new social shape or substrate (Conceit or Style), the novelty turn.
- **Medium Jump ◆:** move it across formats, video to static, static to video. Always a new fingerprint.
- **The Long Pull ◆:** stretch it into a longer VSL, go deeper on the why, the proof, the teaching.

**Turn H, the opening:**

- **Hook Flood ◆:** keep the proven body, generate 5 to 10 new openings; the winner is your new bar.
- **Hook Transplant ◆:** rebuild the ad around an opener that already won somewhere in the account.

**Turn E, tone and format** (inside a bigger turn, never alone):

- **Tone Shift ◆:** move the emotional register (calm ↔︎ urgent, warm ↔︎ fear, mild ↔︎ cranked).
- **Plain Talk ◇:** strip to natural spoken language, filler and pauses, almost too simple.
- **Length Dial ◇:** trim or extend. **Pacing Tweak ◇:** adjust rhythm, add soft CTAs.

## How much to vary vs make new

The split between net new and variations is the Allocation Mix call back in Analysis. Bias it with two reads:

- **The gap map.** Big open gaps lean you toward net new. Well covered with fresh winners leans you toward variations.
- **Account maturity.** A young account is mostly net new. A mature account does more variations, but never zero, because winners always fatigue.

## The patterns that repeat (this is the real point)

Here is what makes it one system instead of six stages: the same handful of moves show up at every single layer. Once you see them, you are not learning six things, you are learning five, applied over and over.

[image.png]

1. **Generate wide, then select with taste.** Strategy generates gaps, Ideation generates seeds, the brief picks the ad, Copy generates hooks, native statics generate concepts. Every stage diverges, then a human picks. You never one shot, you spray and choose, because recognition is faster than construction.
2. **Decode, then recombine.** You overlay your real ads to find gaps, you decode a winner into its CASHED settings before you turn a knob, you decode any video into its CAST coordinates. Read the world backward into coordinates, then generate forward by recombining. That is how "do podcast ads, do Pixar ads, do skits" stops being a to do list and becomes four dials.
3. **The two games, at every layer.** You win the algorithm (the visual: diversity, fingerprints, reach) and you win the human (the message: psychology, conversion). It is in the creative diversity formula, in the entity ID model, in the way the CASHED knobs split into reach knobs and conversion knobs. Same split, all the way down.
4. **Pull from many sources instead of mapping the whole space.** STORMING for ideas, SCRAWLS for visuals. You do not draw the complete map of what is possible, because that is a fool's errand. You pull from live sources and let the diversity fall out.
5. **Feedback bakes back in.** Winners climb into primers, edits become steer prompts, new outcomes grow the map, new conceits become primers. Nothing good is ever lost, and every turn leaves the system a little smarter than it was.

And underneath all five, the dual flywheel: every turn sharpens your taste and enriches your banks at the same time. Twice over, in fact, at the two saturation nodes, where Analysis trains you on what worked and Ideation trains you on what is possible.
