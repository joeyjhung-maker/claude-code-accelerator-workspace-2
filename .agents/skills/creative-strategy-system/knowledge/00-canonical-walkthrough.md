## The two lanes, or why the wheel is secretly a dual flywheel

Here is the beautiful part, and it is the thing I most want you to take away from this whole section. Every single time you go around that loop, two completely different things get stronger at the same time. The wheel looks like one wheel, but it is secretly optimizing two lanes at once.

The first lane is the human lane. This is your taste, your gut, your judgment, your instinct for what is actually going to work. And it gets sharper on its own, just from spinning the wheel, because you are constantly looking at what wins and choosing between options.

The second lane is the system lane. This is everything the machine carries: your primers, your segment map, your seed bank, your style library, your hypotheses. And it gets richer on its own too, because every time you find a winner you save it and feed it back in.

Now here is where I want to get really specific, because "your taste gets better and your system gets better" is an easy thing to say and an easy thing to wave past. Let me show you the actual wires, the exact places where each lane compounds, because these are the real flywheels and you should be able to point straight at them.

Here is where the human gets better, node by node:

- In Analysis, you saturate your brain on winners and losers until you can feel the patterns, and that is where raw instinct forms.
- In Strategy, the judgment you apply picking which gap to chase builds a mental model of your segments that nobody can ever hand you.
- In Ideation, just looking through your swipes and your organic primes your eye and feeds your attention.
- In Copy, in Editing, and in Creative, every time you sit at a gate and pick the better option, you are training your taste on real examples.

And here is where the system gets better, node by node:

- Every winning ad flows back into your primers, in Copy, in Editing, and in Creative, so the writing machine and the editing machine get tighter every single time.
- Your segment map and your coverage update from the ads you actually run, so Strategy always knows what you have and what you are missing.
- Your hypotheses database grows in Analysis, so you can sit down and ask it questions later.
- Your seed bank grows in Ideation, so you never lose an idea once you have had it.
- Your visual styles library grows from the organic and the swipes you see, so Creative always has more to pull from.
- Your repeated edits become saved steer prompts, and your new formats become new conceit primers, so the system keeps inventing reusable parts out of your own work.

That is the dual flywheel. One wheel, two lanes, and the more you spin it the easier and better it gets on both fronts at the exact same time. That is the part that compounds, and it is the whole reason this is worth building.

## The cycle, stage by stage

Now let me name the parts so the loop has real labels on it. The workflow has six stages, and once Strategy has set the goal up top, they run in the same order every time.

- Strategy is where you decide what is worth making. It is the goal that governs everything below it.
- Analysis is where you read your account and prime your judgment.

## The architecture, and the seven kinds of parts

When you stop looking at it as a story and start looking at it as a circuit, each stage becomes a box with clear inputs and outputs, and the boxes pass work down the line. Strategy hands a plan to Ideation. Ideation hands briefs to Copy. Copy hands selected writing to Editing. Editing hands a finished ad to Creative. Creative ships it. And the results travel all the way back to Analysis, which closes the loop.

### Everything is made of only seven kinds of parts

Here is the thing that makes this whole system buildable instead of overwhelming. No matter how big the map gets, no matter how many nodes you stack onto it, the entire thing is made out of only seven kinds of parts. That is it. Seven. Once you can look at any node and go, oh, that is just a bot, or that is just a connection, the fear goes away, because you already know how to build that kind of thing. You are not building a hundred different things. You are building seven things, over and over, in different combinations.

So before we talk about where any of it lives, let us get the seven straight. And the trick to keeping them straight is this. You do not sort them by what they are made of, you sort them by what they do. A lot of these are just markdown files under the hood. A primer is a markdown file. A skill is a markdown file. But they do completely different jobs, so they are different parts. Function first, always.

[IMAGE SLOT: the seven node types, the legend, laid out simply.]

**Code** is the part that does the same thing every time. It is a script, usually Python. It scrapes your reviews, it scores your ads, it pulls your report, it formats the final doc, it glues the other parts together in order. If you can describe it as a mechanical recipe that does not need taste, it is Code.

**A Bot** is the part that thinks. It is a prompt, a skill, an instruction you hand to the model so it can write a hook, edit a line, classify an ad, generate an idea. It takes something in and it makes something new come out, and it does it using AI judgment, not a fixed recipe. When people say skill file, they mean a Bot. The Bot is the worker.

**Knowledge** is the part the Bot reads to get good. Your primers, your swipe banks, your frameworks, your notes. Knowledge does not do anything on its own. It just sits there being true, and you feed it into a Bot so the Bot knows what good looks like. The simplest way to feel the difference is this. A Bot acts, Knowledge informs. A primer never wrote an ad in its life. It just makes the thing that writes the ad better.

**A Database** is where you keep the stuff you need to sort and search later. Your hypotheses, your seed bank, your coverage map of which segments you have covered, your library of saved edits. The difference between this and Knowledge is how you reach for it. Knowledge you read whole, top to bottom. A Database you query, show me every ad I have for women who are unaware. If you want to ask it questions in plain language later, you can put a little retrieval layer on top, but you do not have to start there.

**A Connection** is a wire to the outside world. It is how your system talks to Meta, to your scraper, to your image generator, to Google Docs. Under the hood it is an API key, or one of those MCP links, but they are the same kind of part, a socket and a key that lets your system reach something it does not own. Every time the system has to touch the real world, that is a Connection.

**A Surface** is a screen where you, the human, actually see and move things around. The segment map you click through. The canvas where you edit. A little dashboard. You do not strictly need surfaces, a lot of the system can run with no screen at all, but when you want to see the thing and put the pieces together with your hands, you build a Surface. And the good news is a Surface can be as simple as a single web page.

**The Human** is you. Your judgment, your taste, the moment where you step in and choose. On the map it is the gate, and almost every gate is optional, because you can let the system run straight through if you trust it. But the Human is where the one of one lives, so most of the time you want a hand on the wheel somewhere.

That is the whole vocabulary. Code, Bot, Knowledge, Database, Connection, Surface, Human. Learn to spot those seven and you can read any node on the map and immediately know what it would take to build it.

One more thing people always ask, so let us just settle it here. They ask whether an MCP connection and an API key are different things. They are the same type. Both are just a wire to an outside service. The MCP is the socket and the API key is the key that fits it. So we call them both a Connection and we move on.

# Part two: Strategy

Strategy is the big overall layer. It is the map of everything you could possibly make, and it is the thing that points all of your making in the right direction. This is the part that matters more than any single ad, because if your aim is off up here, it does not matter how good your copy gets later.

A quick orientation before we start. We are teaching Strategy first because it is the map that everything else hangs on. But notice that Strategy is not a stop on the turning loop the way the other stages are. It sits on top and governs, and the wheel itself actually starts turning at Analysis. So think of this part as drawing the terrain, and the next part, Analysis, as the moment you sit down and decide what to make on it.

There is one phrase I want you to hold above everything else in this part. Segments are the biggest unlock. We will come back to that more than once.

## Level one: the idea

Here is the simple version. Strategy is not about optimizing one ad. It is about keeping your whole account healthy. And a healthy account is full of many different winning types of ads, not one winner cloned fifty times. The way you get that variety is called creative diversity.

The biggest lever on creative diversity is your segments. A segment is just a slice of your market. And the single biggest wins almost always come from finding a slice that nobody else is talking to. When you are first into a new segment, the copy does not even have to be perfect, because there is no competition there yet.

So at the simplest level, Strategy is three moves:

- Know your map.
- Find the slices nobody is serving.
- Decide where to plant your flag.

[IMAGE SLOT: the segments are the biggest unlock view, the grid with gaps circled.]

## Level two: the flow

Now let us name the parts. Creative diversity comes from multiplying a few things together, and the cleanest way to hold it is as two big sides.

- Messaging is what you say.
- Creative is how it looks.

Your messaging is itself made of three things multiplied together: a specific segment, times an awareness level, times a concept or angle. Your creative is the visual side. And here is the weighting that matters: the segment is the heaviest factor inside messaging, and the creative is the heaviest factor overall. Those two carry the most weight, so those are the two you watch.

When you multiply all of this together, segments by awareness by angle by visual, you get hundreds of thousands of possible ads. It is combinatorially explosive. That explosion is exactly the point, because it means you always have somewhere new to go, and it is also why the real job is selection.

### What a segment actually is

Let us define a segment properly, because this is the heart of it.

A segment is any slice of a market. A persona is just an identity that you attach to that slice. So a persona is a specific segment dressed up as a real person. Personas are useful, but do not let a list of six personas trick you into thinking that is the whole market. There are thousands of possible slices under there.

The way I like to think about segments is in three or four dimensions, and we may give you some segment builders to help you do this.

- Outcomes are the vertical slices. These are usually pain points, sometimes benefits. The thing the person wants, or the pain they feel. You can add as many new outcomes as you want, and you can keep going narrower underneath them. Inside joint pain there is knee pain, hip pain, and so on. Those narrower outcomes are still vertical slices.
- Demographics cross underneath. Gender, age, identity, and a bunch of other things like that. This is the dimension that crosses your outcomes.
- The third dimension is the qualifiers. Things like the mechanism (what they believe is causing it, like cortisol or inflammation), the condition or filter (a fact that limits them, like vegetarian or pregnant), and the value (a soft preference, like organic only). You take these and you spin up different personas, and you spot different gaps.

[IMAGE SLOT: the segment model, outcomes as vertical slices crossed by demographics and qualifiers.]

That gives you two clean ways to add a brand new segment:

- Find a new outcome or pain point and add it.
- Or get more narrow on one you already have, by finding a specific condition or a smaller sub segment inside it.

### Awareness sits on top of every segment

Once you have a segment or a persona, it does not live at just one stage. Every single segment travels through the stages of awareness, and the same segment can be reached at any of them. The stages are:

- Unaware
- Problem aware
- Solution aware
- Product aware
- Most aware

You do not have to treat all five as separate every time. You can group them into three buckets: unaware and problem together, solution and product together, and most aware on its own.

And of course you have different visuals on top of all of this, which is huge, because each one carries a different kind of messaging. That is how you manufacture a lot of different strategic shots out of the same map.

The angle itself, the actual messaging, comes from pulling ideas from a lot of internal and external sources. We have a whole process for that in the Ideation stage, so here we just point at it.

## Level three: the architecture

[IMAGE SLOT: the Strategy architecture, the clean circuit. Research in, segment map built, ads overlaid, gaps ranked, judgment, map out.]

Here is Strategy as a clean circuit. Trace it left to right. Research comes in, things like reviews, comments, and competitor swipes, and you extract the real outcomes and desires people are actually talking about. That builds your segment map. Then you overlay the ads you actually have on top of that map, and wherever a cell has no ads, that is a gap. You rank those gaps by how big and how reachable they are, and a human applies judgment and taste to decide which ones matter most.

The thing this circuit produces is not this week's orders. It is the terrain. We call it the Strategy Map: the living picture of every segment, what you have covered, and where the openings are. The Strategy Map is the artifact Strategy owns, and it is one of the core pieces sitting in your Foundations layer, the segment map every other stage leans on.

So the handoffs are simple. The winning ads come back from Analysis already sorted into types, and they keep the map current. The map itself, with its ranked gaps, feeds forward into Analysis, which is where you actually sit down and decide what to make. And your personas, along with your copy blocks, the building blocks of the message, feed across into Copy.

## Level four: the blueprint

[IMAGE SLOT: the full Strategy blueprint, every wire. The cube of all possible slices, the coverage layer, the gap finder, and the judgment step.]

At full depth you can see the whole board. The segment map and the cube of all possible slices. The coverage layer that shades in what you already have. The gap finder that ranks the openings. This is the version you eventually write out as markdown and hand to Claude. And then the part that really matters, the judgment step.

### Judgment, or what I call chad logic

Here is the thing about all those slices. You cannot work through them procedurally. There are simply too many, and going cell by cell is too explosive and too slow. So you have to use judgment.

And judgment has a specific meaning here. Judgment is you understanding the model well enough to make some chunky decisions. You are not grinding through a spreadsheet. You are looking at the whole map and making a few big confident calls. I like to call this chad logic, because it is not nerdy logic, it is using your brain to weigh the things that actually matter:

- The urgency of the pain point.
- The size of the market, the TAM, which just means how many people are in that slice.
- How big the gap is, both in the competitor landscape and in your own account.
- Whether it is genuinely new, because something completely new is helpful to bring in.
- What competitors are already doing, and what is working in the zeitgeist right now.

Sitting right next to judgment is your instinct, which is a mixture of taste and gut for where to focus. And remember the dual flywheel from part one. This whole system is built to make that instinct better over time. Your judgment combined with your taste compounds into instinct, and that instinct is the one of one thing that nobody can copy off you. That is the human lane compounding, right here in Strategy.

### From the map to what you actually make

Now, the vocabulary, because this is the exact spot people get confused. Strategy is the map. It is not the list of ads you are making this week. When you sit down to actually make things, that happens in Analysis, and it produces two things worth naming clearly now so they never blur together:

- The Production Plan is this week's batch. It is the concrete sit down decision: I am making this many ads, of these types, at these awareness levels, to fill these specific gaps. That is what flows into Ideation.
- The Allocation Mix is how that Production Plan splits across the three kinds of work: net new, variations, and scaling.

You will build both of those in the next part. Here, just hold the line: Strategy draws the map, Analysis turns the map into the Production Plan and the Allocation Mix.

One quick word on net new, because it is the priority inside that mix. Net new means an original ad that starts from a raw idea or input. It is not a variation of your own winner, and it is not just a straight copy of someone else. It is the genuinely new thing. The platform rewards genuinely new creative, so you weight your effort toward it. Variations spin off your winners, and scaling is really a media buying lever.

## Pulling Strategy together

Strategy is the map of what you could make, and the goal is a healthy, diverse account. Segments are the biggest lever, and a segment is a spine of outcomes with facets hanging off it: demographics across the bottom, and qualifiers like mechanism, condition, and value on top. A persona is just an identity you attach to one of those slices. Awareness sits above every segment. You overlay your real ads to find the gaps, and you pick the best gaps with judgment and taste, your chad logic. What Strategy hands forward is the Strategy Map, the living terrain with its ranked gaps. Turning that map into this week's Production Plan, and deciding the Allocation Mix inside it, is the job of Analysis, which is exactly where we go next.

# Part three: Analysis

Analysis is really the start of everything. This is the moment the wheel starts turning. Strategy drew the map in the last part, and now you sit down, read what your account actually did, and turn a pile of ads into two things at once: a sharper gut, and a set of real hypotheses about what is working. The way you analyze is shaped by your overall strategy, because people run their accounts differently. Different media buying strategies, different philosophies on net new versus variations, different ways to allocate. So this stage bends to fit your strategy, not the other way around.

One thing to hold onto here. You are not just reading numbers. You are saturating your brain on winners until you can feel what to do next.

## Level one: the idea

The simple version is this. All your ads come in. You bracket them into types, into winners and losers and the in between, so you can actually see the patterns. As you do that, you start to feel it, you form hunches and hypotheses. And from that you decide what to make next.

That is the whole thing. Read your account until you can feel what to do.

[IMAGE SLOT: the analysis intuition view, ads coming in and a gut feeling plus a plan coming out.]

## Level two: the flow

### Getting your data in

The data comes in one of a few ways. You can pull straight from the Meta API. You can drop in your reporting data. Or you can feed in the actual reports plus the copy of the ads themselves. Whichever you do, the point is the same: now you have all of your data in one place.

The first thing your Claude Code needs to understand is your KPIs. Is it spend. Is it ROAS, which just means return on ad spend, how much revenue you get back per dollar in. Is it some specific set of metrics. This matters, because the whole sort that comes next depends on what winning actually means to you.

### Sorting into ad types

Then you sort that data into types of ads. And you get to pick your own buckets here. You might do something detailed like breakthrough, KPI winner, spend winner, and loser. You might do something simpler like winners, traction, and losers. Some people just do winners and forget the rest. Make your own types if you want. The key intuition is that you bracket your ads so you can digest the learnings more easily. The brackets are the whole point.

### Asking why, and building hypotheses

Then, for each ad, you ask why. Why did this end up in this category. Why did it win, why did it lose. This is a great place to drop a bot, because the bot can work through each ad and develop a specific hypothesis. And it is a great place to store those hypotheses, in a database you can come back to. You can put RAG or some other layer on top so you can check them and chat with them and figure them out together. That hypotheses database is a Foundations piece, one of the stores every later stage gets to lean on.

Here is the nice part, and it is the dual flywheel showing up again. Going through this process, with you or with your team, is also where your human instinct forms. So the same ads are doing two jobs at once. They are training your gut, the human lane, and they are filling your hypotheses database, the system lane. The raw intuition and the formal hypotheses grow at the same time, from the same ads, and they feed each other.

One honest note. This is not a science. It is genuinely hard to isolate every variable, because the platform and the market are always changing underneath you. You are not after perfect attribution. You are after the big chunky ideas and the overall trend, the things you can actually feel and act on.

### Two levels of hypotheses

There is a second, wider read happening on top of the per ad one. You also develop hypotheses about the whole account.

This is where you look outward and look for gaps:

- What are competitors doing that has opened up new possibilities.
- What is trending organically that you could be adding.
- What new segments, sub segments, or cross segments are missing.
- What awareness levels are we not covering.
- What visual styles are we missing.

That account level read is what turns a pile of per ad hypotheses into a real plan for what to make next. It is also where you are reading against the Strategy Map from the last part, checking your live results against the terrain and its ranked gaps.

## Level three: the architecture

[IMAGE SLOT: the Analysis architecture, the clean circuit. Data in, classifier, bracketing board, hypothesis bot, hypotheses database, human, plan out.]

Here is Analysis as a clean circuit. The ad data flows in from your reports or the platform connection. A classifier scores the ads by performance and spend. That feeds a board where you bracket the ads into types. Then a bot analyzes why each one won or lost and writes a hypothesis, and those hypotheses go into a database with a chat layer on top. You sit next to that database with a two way connection, feeding it and questioning it, and that is where instinct forms. What comes out the far side is your Production Plan, this week's batch, which heads off to Ideation.

One correction worth stating plainly, because it is a common mistake. The Production Plan does not loop back into the platform. It goes forward into making ads. The results of those ads come back to Analysis later, through the big loop. So Analysis is a snapshot with open doors, not a closed circle.

## Level four: the blueprint

[IMAGE SLOT: the full Analysis blueprint, every wire. Input connections, metrics store, bracketing board, analysis bot, hypotheses database with chat, human, and the plan it emits.]

At full depth you see it all. The input connections. The metrics store the classifier reads from. The board with your bracketed types and your own custom types. The analysis bot writing hypotheses. The hypotheses database with its chat layer. The human, with raw intuition and mental models of segments forming. This is the version you write out as markdown and hand to Claude. And the output, which is the thing everything has been building toward.

### The Production Plan and the Allocation Mix

This is the artifact you came here to make, so let us name its two parts cleanly, because they are easy to blur.

The Production Plan is your sit down decision for the week. It is the simple, concrete version of, after my analysis I am making these ads: this many, of these types, at these awareness levels, to fill these specific gaps. You can also think of it as your inventory for the week, the list of what you are actually going to produce. This is the thing that flows into Ideation and constrains how you look at every source there.

The Allocation Mix is the split inside that plan, across the three kinds of work:

- Net new, the priority, the genuinely new ad from a raw idea.
- Variations, which spin off your winners.
- Scaling, which is really a media buying lever.

How heavy you go on each is your own call, because people run their accounts differently. Some lean almost entirely on net new. Some spin a lot of variations. You get to figure out your own flow.

The way it all comes together is pretty natural. You have your set of hypotheses. You have read what competitors are doing. You have your gaps from the Strategy Map. You have a sense of your inventory. And then you prioritize. Sometimes you walk in with constraints already, a specific type of idea you are hunting. Sometimes you just know you need a set number of fresh ideas to try. Either way, you can stress test it and drop your own ideas in wherever they make sense. The learnings here are a blend of the human and the machine, guided by your metrics and your own feel for how to turn ads into winners.

## Pulling Analysis together

Long story short, Analysis is the start, the moment the wheel turns. You take in your reports and your account, and you feed it all in to do two things: make yourself better, and fill a database of hypotheses the AI can use to make the work better too. Your ad report goes into a database, your hypotheses go in alongside it, the two cross reference, and your intuition keeps sharpening on top. You read all of it against the Strategy Map, and what comes out the other side is your Production Plan for the week, with its Allocation Mix across net new, variations, and scaling. That plan is exactly what Ideation picks up next.

# Part four: Ideation

Ideation is where you take that Production Plan and turn it into actual briefs you can write from. This is where creative diversity actually gets generated, so it is a big one. And the thing to hold onto is this: you do not invent in a vacuum. You pull from everywhere, and that pulling is what gives you variety almost for free.

## Level one: the idea

Simple version. You start with your Production Plan, the batch you committed to in Analysis. Sometimes you walk in pointed, going in search of specific new ideas to fill a specific gap. Sometimes it is looser, and you let the ideas you find define what you end up making, because really you are just trying to write a certain number of ads. Either way, you go looking, you collect a pile of seeds, you save every single one, and you turn the best of them into briefs.

Pull wide, save everything, brief the best.

[IMAGE SLOT: the seed bank view, sources streaming into a jar of seeds.]

## Level two: the flow

### Why you pull from sources instead of mapping it all

Remember creative diversity. Your messaging and your creative are how you manufacture it, segments carry the most weight inside messaging, and awareness sits on top. But the concepts and the angles you use are also really, really important, and this is the stage where they come from.

So how do you create variety in your concepts and angles. One tempting answer is to build a big map. People do this with templates. You have a paradoxical question, a problem, a unique mechanism, and you just make sure you hit all of them. We have a lot of these maps and templates and they are genuinely successful.

But here is the catch. It is really hard to map the full possibility of creative space, and trying to map all of it is a bit of a fool's errand, because any map you draw is incomplete and there is always something else out there. So instead of trying to map the whole space, you engineer diversity a different way: you pull from live creative sources. That is what STORMING is for.

### STORMING, and the tool for each source

STORMING is the master concept, and it is an acronym, one letter per source, which makes it easy to hold in your head:

- S, Swipes
- T, Templates
- O, Organic
- R, Research
- M, Matrix
- I, Internal vectors
- N, New styles
- G, Gambits

Eight sources. The trick is that you just pull from all of them, and the diversity comes along for the ride. Here is each one, with what it is and the tool you would reach for.

- **Swipes.** Other people's winning ads that you borrow from. Tool: scrapecreators.com, a service where you paste an ad link and it pulls the ad's text and media back out so you can swipe it, or your own swipe tool, or a swipe library like the Meta Ad Library, Hookd, or Get Hooked. A swipe bot can Mad Lib it for you.
- **Templates.** Your internal map of proven concepts, structures, and beats. This is the internal, template first side. Tool: the template bots we already provide.
- **Organic.** Ideas pulled from organic feeds, where the incentive structures are different, the ideas are new, and things move fast. Tool: your own algorithm (curate who you follow), screenshot and paste posts directly, or scrape the comments through the Meta API.
- **Research.** Anything from your own ads to reviews to comments, everything you can read. Tool: a scraper like Apify (a service that pulls reviews and comments off a page for you), plus your own ad reports.
- **Matrix.** Using your chad logic, the judgment from Strategy, to look at the identified gaps and pour ideas straight into them. Tool: the gap finder and matrix we provide, fed by the Strategy Map.
- **Internal vectors.** Patterns pulled from your own past winners. Tool: a bot that analyzes your past winners. We have some of these built for Genesis already.
- **New styles.** Fresh visual styles you spot and save as you go. Tool: your visual styles database, fed by organic and swipes.
- **Gambits.** Wildcard plays, the longer shots. Tool: fed by your hypotheses database and your comments.

There is a lot of human judgment laced all the way through this. Looking at swipes and organic primes your brain. Analyzing ads and comments primes it more. Thinking through the matrix is really about spotting gaps, which primes your attention to segments. And there are flywheel effects baked in too, because you are constantly updating your library of visual styles based on what you see in organic and swipes.

### The engine and the seeds

Then comes the engine. All of your sources, plus your own instinct, feed an ideation engine that produces raw ideas. In tool terms, the engine is just a Bot, a skill file in your folder that you hand the pulled material plus your Production Plan and let it spit out raw concepts. You run it right inside Claude Code.

Those raw concepts become seeds, and every seed gets saved into a seed bank. The seed bank is a Database, and another piece of your Foundations layer, so you should start it as the simplest thing that works. A single markdown file of seeds is plenty on day one. When that gets clunky, step it up to an Airtable or a spreadsheet so you can actually see them and tag them, and only move to a real backend like Convex or Supabase if a surface ever needs to read them live. The reason you save every seed is so you can reference it later or run operations across all of your past ideas, like asking the bank to resurface every seed you ever had for a given segment.

My own preference, and you can do this differently, is to just generate a big pile of seeds first, because you can always come up with ideas. Get a long list of seeds down into the bank, drop them wherever they fit, and then build briefs from the best of them. From there a human chooses and steers, and turns the chosen seeds into briefs.

### The brief

The brief is worth slowing down on, because how you build it decides everything downstream. You take the raw seed, and then you add four things on top.

- The source. Where the seed came from.
- The reference links. The specific ad or post you are working from.
- The allocation context. The specific persona or awareness level you are aiming at, pulled straight from the Production Plan.
- Your editing notes and steering. How you want it handled. This is the human in the loop, right here.

The source matters most, because it routes the work:

- If the seed is a swipe, you Mad Lib it, keeping it nearly the same and just restructuring it, and you send it to a different bot, the one that just swipes.
- If the seed is organic, you keep the exact hook and edit out from there.
- Everything else just goes through the regular process.

The reference links keep the editor and everyone else oriented. The allocation context steers it to the right persona or awareness level. The editing notes let you guide it however you want.

The thing that assembles all of this is the brief builder, which is another Bot. You hand it the chosen seed and the four add ons, and it formats a clean, consistent brief. Then every brief gets saved into a brief store, which is a second Database, the same simple to fancy ladder as the seed bank, so you can come back to any brief later. The finished briefs are what flow out to Copy.

[IMAGE SLOT: the brief anatomy view, a seed plus source, reference links, allocation context, and steering notes.]

### The tools, end to end

Here is the whole stage as a flow, naming each node, its tool, and the handoff between them.

- Production Plan in. Your plan arrives from Analysis as a markdown file or a row in your store. It tells the engine how many ads and what kind.
- Sources pulled. Each STORMING source fires through its own tool: scrapecreators.com or a swipe library for swipes, your algorithm or the Meta API for organic and comments, Apify for reviews, the template and vector bots for the internal map, the matrix gap finder for gaps. Each one drops raw material into the engine.
- Engine runs. The ideation engine Bot takes the pulled material plus your instinct and produces raw concepts. Runs in Claude Code.
- Seeds saved. Every concept lands in the seed bank Database (markdown, then Airtable, then a backend if needed).
- Human gate. You choose and steer, picking the seeds worth building and tagging each with how to handle it.
- Brief built. The brief builder Bot wraps each chosen seed with its source, reference links, allocation context, and your steering notes, and routes by source: a swipe goes to the swipe bot to be Mad Libbed, an organic seed keeps its hook, everything else runs the regular path.
- Briefs stored and shipped. Each brief saves to the brief store Database and flows out to Copy.

And running alongside all of it, the visual styles Database keeps getting fed by what you see in organic and swipes, and that one feeds forward into Creative rather than into Copy.

### The seven parts in this stage

If you want to see how this maps to the seven kinds of parts from part one: the sources are mostly Connections (scrapecreators, Meta API, Apify) and Knowledge (your template map), the ideation engine and the swipe and vector bots are Bots, the seed bank and the brief store are Databases, the brief builder is a Bot with rules, and you, choosing and steering, are the Human gate.

## Level three: the architecture

[IMAGE SLOT: the Ideation architecture, the clean circuit. Plan and instinct in, internal map and STORMING sources into the engine, seeds, human gate, brief builder, briefs out.]

Here is Ideation as a clean circuit. The Production Plan and your instinct come in from the left. Your internal concept map and your STORMING sources both feed the ideation engine. The engine produces seeds into the seed bank. A human chooses and steers, and a brief builder turns the chosen seeds into briefs. The briefs flow out to Copy, and a copy of each is saved to a brief store. The one extra wire to notice is the visual styles database off to the side, getting fed by organic and swipes, which feeds forward into Creative.

## Level four: the blueprint

[IMAGE SLOT: the full Ideation blueprint, every wire. All eight STORMING sources with their types, the internal map, the flywheel feeds, the engine, seed bank, gate, and brief builder.]

At full depth you can see all eight STORMING sources with their proper types, your internal map, and the flywheel wires that keep them fed. Internal vectors gets fed by your past winners. Gambits gets fed by your hypotheses and comments. Matrix gets fed by your Strategy Map gaps. The visual styles database gets fed by organic and swipes. Everything pours into the engine, out to the seed bank, through the human gate, into the brief builder with all its rules, and out to Copy. This is the version you write out as markdown and hand to Claude.

## Pulling Ideation together

Ideation turns your Production Plan into briefs by pulling ideas from everywhere, internal and external, with STORMING as the master concept and a real tool behind each source. You do not try to map all of creative space, because that is a fool's errand. You pull from live sources instead, you save every seed in a seed bank, you choose and steer, and you build briefs with clear rules. Swipes get Mad Libbed, organic keeps the hook, everything else runs the regular process, and you always add your own steering on top.

# Part five: Copy

Copy is where briefs become actual ads. This is the part most people think is the whole job, and the thing you are about to see is that it is really just one stage powered by a simple, repeatable unit. So hold onto that unit, because everything in this part is built out of it. It is a bot plus a primer.

## Level one: the idea

Simple version. A writing bot plus a primer equals your writing machine. The primer is just your winning ads, nothing fancy. You feed the machine an idea, you push it a few times, and you pick the best output. The magic is that you can take any source of an idea and generate ads from it.

[IMAGE SLOT: the bot plus primer equals ads view.]

## Level two: the flow

### Primers, the secret

Let us talk about primers first, because they are the secret, and they are the heart of your Foundations layer, the thing every winning ad you ever write traces back to. A primer is built from the type of ads you see. It is really just a list of your top performing ads of that type, plus some dos and do nots to steer it. That is it. You might have:

- A primer for long form unaware and problem aware ads, the five hundred to fifteen hundred word story driven ones.
- One for long form solution and product aware, the ones built on comparison and offer.
- A short form one for punchy text ads.
- One for video ad copy meant to be performed on camera.
- One for full video scripts.
- One for AI animation scripts.
- One for story based ads.
- One for most aware ads with direct offers and urgency.
- Plus a hook primer and a headline primer.

You lean heaviest on the two long form ones.

Here is the key intuition about primers, and it goes against what people expect. You do not feed it a buyer document or a pile of product info. You just feed it the winning ads. That is the minimum information you need, because the information already worked. It is even good to write those by hand, because they will be the most human versions. So spend real time on your primers. The structure is something like, use this, do not copy it exactly, just copy the style. And you add your steering, the dos and the do nots, which you keep updating. Every time you get a winner, you update the primer, so it gets tighter and tighter.

In tool terms, a primer is just Knowledge, a markdown file sitting in your folder. Nothing more than that.

### The writing bots

The other tool you need is a writing bot. That can be a named one, something like the Mario bot, or one you build yourself. A bot is just a skill file, another piece of markdown, that you run right inside Claude Code.

Now I will be honest about a little redundancy here. You could skip the primer entirely and feed the brief straight into a writing bot, and it would still write you an ad. But putting the primer in front of it gets noticeably better results, and the primer compounds over time, so it is worth the extra step every time.

### The fixed order: hooks, then body, then headlines

The other big intuition is that there is a fixed order to this, and you always follow it. Hooks first. Then the body from the hook. Then the headlines from the body.

- **Hooks.** You have a dedicated hook bot plus your hook primer, and you feed it your brief. Then you run it two or three times, because no matter how good the first batch looks, you just have to push it to get it really good. Then a human selects. This is the most important gate in the whole stage, because hooks are the most important part of the ad, and if you want you can bypass the rest of the gates after this one and trust the machine.
- **Body.** Now you take your selected hooks plus your brief into a new bot. That bot runs on the body primer, follows directly from those specific hooks, and writes the body. You do this in parallel, typically with two different bots and primers at once, and you run them two to four times, because one output is simply going to come out better than another, the same way it works with image tools. Then a human selects.
- **Headlines.** Last, you take your headline primer and a writing bot or a dedicated headline bot, feed it your finished bodies, your total ads, and generate the headlines from there. Then a human picks the body and headline pair that go together.

Put it all together and you have your doc, the selections document, and that heads to Editing.

### The tools, end to end

Here is the whole stage as a flow, naming each node, its tool, and the handoff.

- Brief in. The brief arrives from Ideation as a markdown file, carrying its Production Plan context. It is the entry point.
- Hook stage. Hook primer (Knowledge) plus hook bot (Bot) plus the brief, run two or three times in Claude Code, then you, the Human, pick the hooks. Most important gate. The rest can be bypassed.
- Body stage. Body primer plus your selected hook plus the brief, fed into two writing bots in parallel, say the Mario bot and one of your own, run two to four times each, then you pick the body.
- Headline stage. Headline primer plus a headline or writing bot, fed your finished bodies, then you pick the body and headline pair.
- Assemble. Everything gets formatted into a selections document, a markdown doc, which flows to Editing.
- Flywheel. Every ad that later wins gets written back into the matching primer as a fresh example plus updated dos and do nots, so the next pass through this stage starts from a better place. That is the system lane compounding, right inside your primers.

### The seven parts in this stage

Mapping it to the seven kinds of parts from part one: the primers are all Knowledge, the hook bot, writing bots, and headline bot are Bots, the selections document is a simple doc you can keep in a Database if you want to search it later, and you, selecting at each gate, are the Human. There is no Connection or Surface strictly required here, which is part of why Copy is one of the easiest stages to stand up.

## Level three: the architecture

[IMAGE SLOT: the Copy architecture, the clean circuit. Brief in, three primer plus bot plus gate stages in a row, selections document out.]

Here is Copy as a clean circuit, and it is the most repetitive shape in the whole system, which is a good thing. The brief comes in. Three primers, hook, body, and headline, sit on top and feed three stages in a row, each one a primer feeding a bot feeding a human gate. The chosen hook wires into the body stage, the body wires into the headline stage, and the output is a selections document that flows to Editing. The one wire to notice is the flywheel underneath, carrying winning ads back up into the primers.

## Level four: the blueprint

[IMAGE SLOT: the full Copy blueprint, every wire. The whole primer library, the named and custom bots, the push and parallel notes, the gates, and the primer flywheel.]

At full depth you see the whole primer library with all its types, the bot options including the named writing bots and your own custom ones, the push two or three times on hooks, the run in parallel on body, and the human gates with the hook gate marked as the most important and the rest bypassable. And the flywheel where every winner updates the primers. This is the version you write out as markdown and hand to Claude.

## Pulling Copy together

Copy is brief to hooks to body to headlines, and the unit underneath all of it is a bot plus a primer. Primers are just your winning ads plus a light steer, they live as markdown in your Foundations, and they get tighter every time you add a winner. You write hooks first and push them two or three times, you write body in parallel with a couple of bots and pick the best, you generate headlines from the finished bodies, and a human selects along the way. Out the other end comes a clean selections document, headed for Editing.

# Part six: Editing

Editing is where you push an ad until it is right, and then finish it into a clean document. And the reason it matters is the loop at the end. The editing you do here does not just improve one ad, it trains your buttons and your primers so the next ad is easier. So pay attention to the part that compounds.

One honest thing up front. A lot of people skip this stage entirely and just one shot the ad straight out of Copy, and that is completely fine. But some people want to edit, want some control over the final thing, and this is the way to make that control actually work with the system instead of against it.

## Level one: the idea

Simple version. You take your ad and you push it until it is right, three ways. By voice, by buttons, or by hand. And every good edit you make becomes reusable. So editing gets easier every single time.

[IMAGE SLOT: the push it until it is right view.]

## Level two: the flow

### The two paths

There are two paths. One is manual, where you just edit it yourself by hand. The other is AI assisted, and the way you do that is with a canvas. The canvas is really just a good writing bot that you do your editing inside, and you keep your primers loaded on this path no matter what.

The canvas can live wherever you like. You can run it inside Poe, which is an app that lets you chat with lots of different bots in one place, inside Claude Code if it calls the bot, or on the Genesis server. I personally like to use the Mario bot, or one of the others, but any solid writing bot works. You drop the ad into it, and then you steer.

### How you steer

Within the AI assisted path there are two ways to steer, and you stack them.

- **Voice.** You talk your edits in using a voice tool. I really like Wispr Flow for this, which is a tool that types out whatever you say, so you can just speak your edits instead of typing them. You say what you want fixed, rapid fire, and then polish.
- **Steer prompts.** These are specific prompts you keep on hand for the common things that get in the way, and you pop them in as needed. Things like: make it more natural, when it is too AI sounding; add more bounce; shorten it, cut the fluff; simplify the reading level; make it better; fix the transitions; fix the logic; fix the call to action; find and replace.

The thing that makes this clean is that every one of those steer prompts is just a little markdown file. You keep them on hand and pop them in whenever you need them, and you can layer voice and manual edits on top. Either way you push until right and you get your final output.

What I have personally found most effective is exactly this stack: take an ad I have written, drop it into the Mario bot with my primer loaded, and then run voice notes through Wispr Flow plus steer prompts to dial it in. That is the fast way to really optimize a piece.

### The two loops that compound

Now the part that matters most, because this is where editing stops being a chore and starts making you faster. There are two loops, and both of them feed your Foundations.

- **Repeated edits become new buttons.** If you find yourself doing the same edit over and over, you turn it into a new steer prompt, save it as a markdown file, and now it is a button you press instead of a thing you retype.
- **Feedback becomes primer rules.** Whatever feedback you gave the AI gets fed back into your primers as dos and do nots.

And here is the trick that ties it together. You set up a skill that runs after each editing session. It takes all the voice notes and feedback you gave during that session, and it writes them back into two places: into your steer prompts as new buttons, and into your primers as new rules. So your editing literally upgrades your own toolkit while you work, and the next pass through is cleaner. That is the system lane compounding again, this time from the words you say while you edit.

### Finishing the ad

After the editing is done, you finish. You make new hooks from the edited ad using your hook primer and a hook bot, you select. You make new headlines using your headline primer, you select. And you format the whole thing into a clean document. Then it goes to Creative.

### The tools, end to end

Here is the whole stage as a flow, naming each node, its tool, and the handoff.

- Ad in. The selections document arrives from Copy.
- Pick a path. Manual editing by hand, or into the canvas.
- The canvas. A writing bot like the Mario bot (Bot), with your primer (Knowledge) loaded, run in Poe, Claude Code, or the Genesis server.
- Steer. Voice through Wispr Flow (Connection), plus steer prompts (markdown buttons) popped in, plus manual edits. Push until right.
- Finish. Make new hooks (hook primer plus hook bot) and select, make new headlines (headline primer) and select, format into a clean document.
- To Creative. The finished document flows out.
- After session skill. A Bot ingests the session's voice notes and feedback and writes it back into your steer prompts and your primers. A saved edit rules Database sits underneath, holding the good edits, with two wires looping back, one to the steer prompts and one to the primers.

### The seven parts in this stage

Mapping to the seven kinds of parts from part one: the canvas and the after session skill are Bots, the primers and the steer prompt files are Knowledge, the saved edit rules store is a Database, Wispr Flow is a Connection, the canvas you edit inside can be a Surface, and you, pushing and selecting, are the Human.

## Pulling Editing together

Editing is pushing the ad until it is right, by voice, by buttons, or by hand, with your primers always loaded on the AI path. The canvas is just a writing bot like the Mario bot, you steer it with Wispr Flow voice notes and steer prompt buttons saved as markdown, and you can skip the whole stage and one shot it if you want. Then you finish by making new hooks, making new headlines, and formatting into a document. And the magic is the two loops: repeated edits become new buttons, and your feedback becomes primer rules, with an after session skill writing both back into your Foundations for you, so the whole thing tightens every time.

# Part seven: Creative

Creative is where the writing becomes statics and video, and then ships. This is where you generate real visual diversity, and there is a powerful idea sitting underneath it about how novelty itself wins. So pay attention to this one. The format itself is the new mechanism.

## Level one: the idea

Simple version. You take one message and you pour it into many shapes. Some are statics, some are videos. And here is the big idea. A format nobody has seen before resets the clock, both for the human brain, which gets grabbed by novelty, and for the platform, which cannot slot your new thing next to your old winners, so it goes and finds a fresh audience for you. The message underneath stays the same. The shape keeps changing. That is how you manufacture novelty faster than the market can copy you.

[IMAGE SLOT: the one message, many shapes view, or the novelty machine.]

## Level two: the flow

There are two tracks: statics and video. Let us take them one at a time, because each has its own framework and its own tools.

### The statics track

Statics come in three kinds, and they line up with awareness, the funnel.

- Native is the unaware, top of funnel style. It looks organic, like it was not an ad at all.
- Templated is the middle. Proven layouts you reuse.
- Branded is the bottom, your hero product shots.

The native ones are the interesting part, because that is where the fresh ideas come from, and they are built from a set of sources we call SCRAWLS. Like STORMING, it is an acronym, one letter per source:

- S, Swipe
- C, Copy derived
- R, Reptile triggers
- A, Audience derived
- W, Wildcard
- L, Loopback
- S, Source

The way you actually make one is you take the ad and you run it through a prompt that converts it into a visual, and each SCRAWLS source is a different way to find or derive that visual. Here is each one, with the tool behind it.

- **Swipe.** First a process to find swipes (Meta Ad Library, scrapecreators.com, Hookd, Get Hooked), then a prompt that reverse engineers the swipe, uses it as a reference, and ships a new version. The ship step runs through an image generator like KIE.ai (which runs the gpt image and nano banana models) or Higgsfield.
- **Copy derived.** A prompt that analyzes the important features of your own ads, the copy blocks and other elements and objects, and turns those into a static.
- **Reptile triggers.** A prompt keyed on the primal, reptile brain triggers, the raw stuff that grabs attention before thinking kicks in, things like faces, food, danger, and novelty. Built to hit those directly.
- **Audience derived.** You take the comments, pulled through the Meta API or Apify, and derive a static straight from real audience language.
- **Wildcard.** You just go in and scrape what is out there in the wild, with something like Apify, and riff on it.
- **Loopback.** You keep a vector of all of your winning ads, a Database, and you run operations on it to spin out new variants.
- **Source.** You start from a raw source asset, a real photo, a product shot, a screenshot, a piece of customer footage, and you build the static around that real material.

The templated and branded statics are simpler. You just have a template bot for each template, and it makes the static for you. Same idea for branded. Those template bots pull from your visual and template database, which itself keeps getting fed by organic and swipes, and which is one more piece of your Foundations.

### The video track, and CAST

The second track is video, and this is where the powerful framework lives. It is called CAST, and like the others it is an acronym, four axes:

- C, Conceit. The social shape the ad mimics. A customer call, a street interview, cue cards, a thumb fight. This is where novelty actually lives.
- A, Actor. Who is on screen, and whose word you are trusting.
- S, Style. The visual substrate, like live action or AI animation.
- T, Terrain. Where it is set.

The old way of cutting video, human first versus voiceover versus animation, was off, because each of those was really keying off a different axis. CAST gives each its own axis, so you can decode any video into four coordinates, and you can generate brand new ones by picking a combination nobody has run. We will show examples of how every type of video ad falls into these four, and how you spin up new ones from there.

Conceit is the engine room, so you point a creativity bot at it. You feed that bot what is working in organic and in ads, and it abstracts the shape and invents new conceits. Each new conceit becomes its own primer, saved into your Foundations alongside the others. In practice the conceit usually shows up as a specific kind of script. You will have Pixar style scripts and other script types that work together, and based on the conceit and the actor you pick the right script.

Then you apply your primers in sequence:

- The payload primer tells you what to say.
- The conceit primer tells you what shape to pour it into.
- Then Style, Actor, and Terrain are your production directions.

And there are two simple moves to remember. To manufacture novelty, you push conceit and style. To scale a winner, you swap actor and terrain. The production itself runs through video and avatar tools, and they split into two jobs. For a real person on screen speaking your script, you use avatar tools like HeyGen or Hedra, which turn a script into a talking person. For the moving footage and animation behind it, you use video generators like Runway, Kling, or Veo, with Higgsfield when you want a more stylized look.

### Iterations, the gate, and shipping

After both tracks, you run iterations on your winners, and this is worth doing properly rather than guessing, because a winner is the cheapest place to get your next winner. The clean way to think about iterations is that they are just systematic moves along the same axes you already know:

- Re hook it. New hooks on the same body, the fastest variation there is.
- Push conceit or style. A new shape or substrate on the same message, which manufactures fresh novelty.
- Swap actor or terrain. The same winning ad with a new face or a new setting, which is how you scale something that already works.
- Re cut for a different awareness level. Take the most aware version and pull it back to problem aware, or the reverse.
- Change the length or the format. A short cut down of a long winner, or a static pulled out of a video.

Then you pass through an optional creative gate, where a human can step in or wave it through. Then you ship to the platform through the Meta API. This is also the natural place to stress test, to sanity check that a combination actually makes sense before you spend on it.

And then the loop closes. The results travel back to Analysis, the winners feed your primers, and the styles feed your visual database. Three wires going home, all feeding your Foundations.

### The tools, end to end

Here is the whole stage as a flow, naming each node, its tool, and the handoff.

- Final ad and copy in. The finished document arrives from Editing.
- Statics track. The visual and template database feeds native, templated, and branded. SCRAWLS conversion prompts (Bots) derive the natives, and image generators — KIE.ai (GPT-Image + Nano Banana Pro), Higgsfield for stylized, Midjourney optional — render them.
- Video track. The conceit bot (Bot) invents conceits into conceit primers (Knowledge). The payload and conceit primers set the script, and the CAST production runs through avatar tools (HeyGen, Hedra) and video tools (Runway, Kling, Veo, Higgsfield).
- Iterations. Both tracks meet at iterations on winners, run along the axes above.
- Gate. An optional human creative gate.
- Ship. Out to the platform through the Meta API (Connection).
- Feedback wires. Results back to Analysis, winners back to your primers, styles back to your visual database.

### The seven parts in this stage

Mapping to the seven kinds of parts from part one: the SCRAWLS prompts, template bots, and conceit bot are Bots, the payload and conceit primers are Knowledge, the visual and template database and the winners vector are Databases, the image, video, and avatar generators plus the Meta ship connection are Connections, the optional creative gate is the Human, and a review screen, if you build one, is a Surface.
