## The vocabulary (reuse these terms when planning — don't reinvent them)

- **Hopper** — a group used to pre-sell an offer over time by giving people small proof tasks instead of pitching cold.
- **Crock pot hopper** — the always-on, slow-simmer group (an existing owned audience). Cold prospects self-warm just by scrolling old posts before you ever talk to them.
- **Microwave hopper** — a tighter, hotter, purpose-built spin-off group for one specific offer, split off once that offer is proven in the crock pot.
- **CDDC (Clearly Different Desirable Concept)** — your positioning must be visibly different from what the prospect is already doing, explainable in seconds, not five minutes.
- **T1 / T2 / T3** — the tap sequence. T1 = public hook/awareness post. T2 = qualifying conversation. T3 = the actual offer/pitch. Write the T3 first — it forces clarity and exposes what proof is missing.
- **CAP (Cause, Action, Payoff)** — the copy structure for individual posts: state the situation/threat or promise, tell them what to do, describe the outcome. Nests recursively.
- **The 5 Ps** — Problem, Personality, Philosophy, Plan, Price. Pre-sell all five before pitching the product and conversion can hit 90%+; skip straight from Problem to Product and you're stuck near 10%.
- **ACO (Access, Control, Ownership)** — the leverage ladder for someone else's audience: guest into it (access) → spin off your own group from it (control) → it's fully yours (ownership).
- **Fence tippers** — late-campaign messages that hit time/money objections and the deadline, not the offer itself — for people already sold, just stuck.
- **"Change the who, not the what"** — the cheapest, highest-leverage lever in any stalled offer is a better-fit audience, not a new product.

## The core method

A hopper works by replacing *telling* people something works with getting them to *prove it to themselves* through one small, assigned action (the "domino"), then converting on the back of that self-proof. Desire is usually already in the market — a post's job is to release it into demand, not manufacture it from nothing, by naming and dismantling one false assumption the prospect is carrying ("popping the cork").

Three principles hold regardless of execution: extend attention on one topic, layer real (ideally demonstrated, not just claimed) proof, and always leave the audience wanting more than you gave them.

Groups go stale when only the same handful of people engage — the fix isn't more content, it's carving a smaller, self-selected slice out with a plain qualifier ("do you already have clients?"), which restores real engagement and gives fresh terrain to test on.

## Positioning the offer

Work the CDDC exercise before writing any copy: what's the prospect's tangible constraint → their assumptions about it → your breakthrough (almost always a direct reversal of that assumption) → the implications, external and internal, of removing it. The implications are where hopper names and hooks come from — not the mechanism itself.

Reduce the offer to a 3-step plan from the customer's point of view, not your feature list. Step 1 has to be counterintuitive — it should contradict something the niche currently believes. Cap it at 3 steps.

Then pre-sell the 5 Ps, in roughly this order of leverage: Plan first (biggest lever, needs real contrast against what they've already tried), Philosophy and Personality as the connective tissue (Philosophy repels/attracts on worldview; Personality is only ~10% of the weight and should come from helping someone over a small real hurdle, never "look how cool I am"), Price last but worth doing (pre-selling it can be the difference between 60% and 90%+ close).

## Running the group

Write the T3 (the actual offer post) before any T1s — it's a forcing function that reveals what proof or benefit you're missing before you post anything live. A strong invite post: identity-based hook → pre-handle the obvious objection → a single reframing insight backed by credible proof → benefit stacking → a low-friction CTA ("type X below").

Cycle post types rather than repeating one: what's-different-now (contrast), proof (before/after, name the assumption that got busted), what's-really-stopping-them (reframe the obstacle as a symptom — throw rocks at generic villains, never the customer), why-now (real cost of waiting, not manufactured urgency), how-it-works (sixth-grade simple).

Don't gate pacing to a calendar — gate it to your own operational capacity and to visible audience engagement (how many actually did the last assignment). Celebrate early micro-actions publicly; never shame non-participants.

Target by provable facts, not vibes — "have you sent 20 cold emails this month with zero replies" beats "ambitious founder" every time.

## Closing and ascension

Never pitch the big offer on a first interaction — sequence through a low-risk "first date" that proves value fast before inviting toward the bigger commitment. When someone buys the entry offer, reach out 1:1 to your best-fit buyers and invite them up, crediting what they've already paid — this ("second helping") beats a generic "cart's open" blast, which trains a list to wait you out.

Late in a launch, stop re-explaining the offer. Fence-tippers only need time/money objections handled and the deadline repeated — multiple times a day, more than most people are comfortable with. Late responders convert at a higher rate than early ones, so a quiet-looking launch isn't dead until the actual deadline.

Hold the investor mindset through the dip: a string of no-replies is expected variance, not a verdict on the offer — don't stop to diagnose on day four, take the next action, and only actually audit the campaign against early directional signals (test-piece response rates), not raw silence.

## Operational notes (context-dependent — check before porting directly)

- Team pay: junior "tapper" roles (canned-response hand-raiser triage) around $20/hr + a small per-sale bonus; senior/copywriter pay scales with the offer's price point and funnel position.
- Licensing: attaching an existing asset as a bonus into someone else's funnel for a revenue share is a legitimate leverage play worth knowing exists, separate from building new product.
- A meaningful amount of this course's operational detail (Facebook Business Pages, tapper-link Messenger CRMs, Facebook's mass-paste restriction risk) is Facebook-specific and doesn't port directly to a Skool group — Skool doesn't have the same personal-profile flagging mechanics. Don't import these as-is; the underlying principle (avoid anything that looks like spam automation, route team replies through something trackable) still applies.

- **Turn the organic "prove it to yourself" pattern into a deliberate mechanic.** Rather than hoping members post their own results, assign a specific small task in a NAP or pinned post ("send one letter this week, report back what happened") and publicly celebrate whoever completes it. This is presently happening by accident (the whiskey-letter post); Travis's method says to engineer it on purpose.

Worth designing the LinkedIn-tier welcome sequence around this instead of a standard drip.

Personality and Philosophy are thin — Travis's rule is these shouldn't be "look how cool Dan is" posts but Dan genuinely helping someone over a specific small hurdle, and a stated stance on B2B outreach/sales generally. Worth a deliberate philosophy post in the group, distinct from tactical content.
- **The Oct fence-tipper cadence has real backing.** The "8 weeks to pre-sell" timeline and the planned Sep/Oct daily posting cadence already point the right direction — Travis's specific addition is: near the actual end-of-Oct deadline, stop re-explaining the mechanism entirely and post/email multiple times in the final day specifically hitting time/money objections and a repeated countdown ("final notice"). ### Conflicts or tensions worth a conscious call, not a silent default
- **Teachshop vs. workshop tension.** The group's described culture is "tactical and show-your-work" — webinar replays, full walkthroughs. Travis's Module 9 argues the opposite: over-teaching in the free tier removes the felt gap that makes someone pay, and the fix is deliberately incomplete content that makes people attempt the task and hit a wall themselves. This is a real decision, not an obvious fix — B2B Leads Lab's free tier may be intentionally generous as a trust-building choice ahead of the Oct paid launch, or it may be quietly working against the ascension. Worth Dan and Joey deciding on purpose which one B2B Leads Lab is doing, rather than it happening by default.
- **Manufactured vs. real urgency.** Travis's fence-tipper method leans hard on repeated deadline messaging — but he's explicit it only works because the scarcity is real (an actual close date, actual other buyers). That's compatible with this workspace's existing "no fake urgency" rule, not in conflict with it — flagged here only so the Oct push doesn't drift into invented pressure under deadline stress.
