## Module 12: Types of Hopper Posts

**Core teaching**
Desire for a solution is (usually) already present in a market — the job of a hopper post isn't to manufacture desire but to convert it into active demand. The blocker isn't usually "reality" but an unexamined false assumption the prospect is carrying; naming and dismantling that assumption ("popping the cork") releases pent-up desire into demand.

**Named framework/terms**
- **Desire vs. demand** — desire is the pre-existing want; demand is that want made active and ready to buy. You can't create desire, only release it.
- **Champagne bottle / cork** — desire is the shaken-up champagne; the false assumption is the cork. Popping the cork doesn't mean the sale is made — the demand still has to be poured into a "glass" (a concrete plan/offer).
- **Bust assumptions, not beliefs** — assumptions are casual, half-examined, easy to replace; beliefs are deep-seated. Use an existing belief as the platform for a new, useful assumption, rather than attacking the belief itself.
- **Breakthrough mechanism** — the specific "how it works" ingredient/method that makes the promise logically believable.
- **BAM (Backwards Ascension Model)** — in-progress idea: sell the top offer first, deliver it, then use real delivery data to reverse-engineer the lower-priced offers, replacing guesswork with tested reality.

**Concrete actionable rules**
- Cycle through post types: (1) "what's different now" using contrast; (2) proof/testimonials structured as before-and-after with the busted assumption named, weighting internal/identity results over product results; (3) "what's really stopping them" — reframe the perceived obstacle as a symptom, only "throw rocks" at generic villains, never the customer or competitor; (4) "why now" — real environmental cost of inaction, not manufactured urgency; (5) "how the mechanism works" — simple, short.
- Present pain converts more than future gain — foreground the most immediate benefit.
- Physically draw out the before/after/in-between before writing contrast copy.
- Explain complex mechanisms at "sixth-grader" simplicity.
- Once demand is created and the plan understood, you've crossed from marketing into sales — treat as a separate stage.
- Let the market set launch pace rather than a fixed calendar; it's fine to extend a stated timeline publicly.
- Late-in-campaign responders reply less often but convert at a much higher rate than early responders.
- Show the next paid step as a visible expected progression, not a bait-and-switch tease.

## Module 13: Access and Control of Assets

**Core teaching**
Don't just work for one-off sale income — work to build ownership/control of a reusable, tested marketing asset that can be redeployed across formats for years. Wealthy operators work for access, control, or ownership of assets, not direct labor-for-money.

**Named framework/terms**
- **ACO — Access, Control, Ownership** — a leverage hierarchy. Access = allowed into someone else's asset. Control = can direct it without owning it. Ownership = fully yours. Progression: guest expert into someone's group → spin off a narrower group of your own.
- **Crock pot hopper vs. microwave hopper** — the crock pot is always-on, slow-simmer; cold prospects self-warm by scrolling old posts. The microwave is the tighter, faster sales-focused group people get moved into to close quickly.
- **The apple analogy** — buying an apple, eating it, tossing the core is one-shot income. The "Mojo way" extracts compounding derivative value from one input (license a photo, core it, photograph again, plant seeds, write a book about the process).
- **CDDC** — strongest CDDCs point at something *external* to the person, not an internal change.
- **"Mental masturbation gallery"** — any sales letter, however well-researched, is still an untested set of guesses — a hopper group replaces guessing with live tested data before the formal asset gets built.

**Concrete actionable rules**
- Test messaging, angle, and mechanism live in the crock pot before building the bigger asset (VSL, sales letter, webinar, T3).
- Once a post/sequence proves out, repurpose it into other formats (report, direct mail, lead magnet, ad, book).
- When guesting in someone else's audience, get introduced around a narrow sub-topic they under-serve; once established, spin off your own dedicated group.
- Watch stick-rate, not just close-rate: "cancel anytime" gets weaker stick-rates than a fixed term — if drop-off clusters at a specific month, consider changing the term length before the price.
- Add unbundled add-ons after the initial close — low risk since a buyer who already said yes rarely cancels over a small extra ask.
- Watch qualitative signal (do people "get it," do they buy) over engagement metrics — excitement doesn't reliably predict conversion.
- Before building around a JV/expert partner's material, get explicit about their non-monetary motivation and any price-ceiling resistance.
- A small in-person/high-ticket intensive can double as front-end monetization and market research.

**Notable example**
Rebecca's group closed 4 recurring $997/month sales from just 5 inbound replies out of ~105 members, with zero direct selling — organic crock-pot conversion.

## Module 15: Fence Tippers and Effectiveness of Multiple Daily Social Posts

**Core teaching**
Late in a launch, prospects on the fence are usually already sold conceptually — the blocker is almost always time or money, not more persuasion about the offer. Messaging should stop explaining the mechanism and instead repeatedly hit time/money objections plus the deadline; doing this multiple times a day converts far better than most people fear.

**Named framework/terms**
- **Fence tippers** — messages purpose-built to push already-convinced prospects over the line — objection-handling and urgency content, not top-of-funnel persuasion.
- **"There's no rush" fence tipper** — reassures buyers they can still capture the result acting later, defusing time-guilt while still moving toward action, backed by a case study.
- **Cart/wagon-without-wheels metaphor** — an overwhelmed prospect is too consumed by effort to stop and learn about the solution — justifies repetition rather than backing off.
- **Pay-as-you-profit program** — small deposit up front, remainder paid from results generated.
- **"Final notice"** — Travis's highest-converting two-word closing phrase, reused every campaign near the true deadline.
- **Price-increase campaign** — framing an upcoming price rise as the campaign engine itself — one of his best-performing campaign types.
- **"Biggest expense is unrealized sales"** — reframes hesitation over a small price bump — the real cost is the sales lost by waiting.

**Concrete actionable rules**
- In the final stretch, multiple posts/emails per day are highly effective — fear of "annoying" people is mostly self-censorship.
- State the one or two hard qualifiers plainly and early; if you already know someone qualifies, just confirm it.
- Late-stage fence-tipper content should focus exclusively on time and money — skip identity/mechanism explanation at this point.
- Deliver objection-handling through third-party case studies, not direct-at-the-prospect framing.
- Repeat the countdown throughout closing day (morning → afternoon → "final notice").
- Expect the highest volume and highest-converting responses in the final hour/day.
- Use a price increase for the next cohort as its own dedicated fence-tipper campaign.
- A scarcity nudge noting others have said yes can pull in fence-sitters — real, not fabricated, urgency.
- Keep firm payment boundaries (one grace period, then removal).

**Notable example**
Elizabeth's client ("channeler of Egyptian deities") sold $111k programs in an unlikely niche; repeating the clock ("ends at noon," "ends in an hour," "final notice") pulled in several last-minute buyers she wouldn't otherwise have gotten.

## Module 16: Ratchet Up the Intimacy

**Core teaching**
Sales approach should scale inversely with audience size — the smaller/more familiar your active group, the more personal and conversational the outreach should be, while large audiences can tolerate more impersonal, automated funnels because volume compensates. External, tool-based offers convert far more reliably than internal/identity-change offers, unless the internal offer commits fully to an extreme, uncompromising promise.

**Named framework/terms**
- **T1 / T2 / T3** — T1 = low-commitment public hook; T2 = qualifying conversation (should feel conversational, not an interrogation); T3 = the actual pitch. Tone shifts by channel even though structure stays the same.
- **External vs. internal offers** — external hands the prospect a tool/script/system that changes outside results without requiring identity change; internal promises identity/mindset transformation. External converts more predictably; internal only converts well pitched at full intensity, no soft middle ground.
- **Tactical front door / strategic living room** — sell the easy, external, visualizable win as the front door; deliver the deeper internal/strategic work once trust and early results exist.
- **Present pain / "start close to the hole"** — get specific about current pain; work with people already close to the outcome so the gap being closed is small.
- **"Straddling the business you're in vs. the business you want to be in"** — position the new approach as a bolt-on, not a replacement, to lower perceived risk.
- **Boat-not-the-leap metaphor** — offer a small reversible first step instead of asking for the full leap.
- **"Walking around the nightclub" metaphor** — repeated, visible presence before a direct approach makes cold outreach land as natural familiarity, not presumption.

**Concrete actionable rules**
- Match format to audience size: small/known audiences get short personal video T3s or direct hand-raise asks; large lists can go straight to a sales page/order form.
- On any order form, place the core ROI proposition near the buy buttons.
- Paint an explicit "day in the life" of using the offer — an unvisualizable process kills conversion more than lack of desire does.
- Diagnose a stalled campaign as one of exactly three causes: (1) offer mismatch, (2) an unaddressed fear (often "what if they say yes and I can't deliver"), or (3) no visible bridge from where they are to the result.
- If prospects lack a sellable offer of their own, build a starter/template offer into your own product.
- After a purchase, test rolling a subset (roughly 5–15%) into a much higher-ticket offer soon after — recent buyers convert to bigger offers at meaningfully higher rates.
- For renewal-stage clients, proactively surface their next challenge and offer a concrete personalized incentive a couple months ahead of the anniversary.
- Use "how come we're not working together yet" as a direct line for warm, visibly-engaged non-customers.

**Notable example**
Elizabeth's $1,000 renewal offer to ~130 existing buyers via a bare-bones order form — coached live to add an ROI callout near the buttons and a "picture painting" video, since the existing copy left the buying process too vague to visualize.
