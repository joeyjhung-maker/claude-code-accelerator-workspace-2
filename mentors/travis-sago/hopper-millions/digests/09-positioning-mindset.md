## Module 9: Workshop Vs. Teachshop

**Core teaching**
Over-teaching kills conversions. When you explain everything so people don't have to struggle, they leave feeling capable and don't buy. The winning move is to teach less and let people *do* the exercise, hit a real obstacle, and feel the gap themselves — that felt gap, not your explanation of it, is what makes them say "I need help." Confrontation with a real limitation is more persuasive (and more loving) than reassurance.

**Named framework/terms**
- **Workshop vs. Teachshop** — a "teachshop" is content-heavy: you explain until people nod along ("mind blown") but never test themselves. A "workshop" makes them attempt the actual task and struggle.
- **Bowl of Fruit Loops** — caution against over-fixating on immediate sales from the hopper; the real prize is market insight — chase sales too hard and you spill the bowl.
- **The Diving Board example** — kids excited to climb up, then freeze at the edge. Hopper members: excited to join, then scared at the actual commitment point — offer help at the fear point, not before.
- **Philosophy > Personality > Plan > Product (posting hierarchy)** — people never tire of philosophy or problem posts; they fatigue on personality (look-at-me) and pure product posts.
- **Identity-based crock pot** — naming/positioning a group around a decision-maker identity rather than a generic niche label, so the offer implicitly carries higher stakes and price tolerance.

**Concrete actionable rules**
- Don't demonstrate the market's gap in the abstract — get members to actually do the exercise so they personally hit the obstacle.
- After a group does an exercise, follow up 1:1 asking about their *next* problem, not just praising the activity.
- In group settings, highlight the small number of people getting results publicly; ask everyone else "what's stopping you" and let them answer.
- Balance ratio: don't cut teaching to zero — the instinct to "save them the struggle" directly hurts conversions.
- Build in a philosophy post and a plan post before any product pitch.
- Set firm boundaries with clients in crisis: no ad hoc emotional coaching over PM.
- Use "next step" framing: acknowledge what they've achieved, then explicitly preview the challenge still ahead.

**Notable example**
Travis's own big-ticket email course: over-teaching to spare students difficulty writing subject lines dropped conversions; switching to a workshop structure (let them struggle live) increased both sales and what students actually got out of it.

## Module 10: 1st Date to Marriage

**Core teaching**
Never try to sell someone your big, committed offer ("marriage") straight out of the gate. Sequence every relationship through an easy, low-risk first step that proves value fast, then use that momentum to invite them deeper. Selling the big thing too early is named as the single biggest reason high-ticket/agency offers convert poorly.

**Named framework/terms**
- **1st Date to Marriage** — never propose "marriage" (the big, expensive, high-commitment program) on a first interaction. Give a low-risk "first date" first, then progressively invite toward bigger commitment.
- **Second Helping Campaign** — reaching out post-purchase to buyers of your smaller offer, congratulating them, and inviting select ones into your next, bigger offer — crediting what they've already paid. Preferred strongly over "reopening cart," which trains the list to wait you out.
- **T-one / T-two / T-three** — sequenced outreach/copy pieces (t-one = opening post/message raising hands; t-three = fuller offer letter/video).
- **Risk reversal / guarantees** — verbalize what you'd already do for the right people rather than avoiding guarantees out of fear. Noah's Ark metaphor: people don't want their money back, they want reassurance they won't "sink."
- **Junior tapper vs. senior tapper** — junior handles scripted/canned replies; senior reads between the lines and handles no-canned-response situations. Different pay scales.
- **McDonaldizing** — breaking a complex skill into separable, teachable component parts so lower-cost people can execute the simple parts.
- **Peter Principle** — promoting good performers repeatedly eventually puts them in a role they're incompetent at.

**Concrete actionable rules**
- After someone buys your entry offer, proactively reach out 1:1 to a shortlist of your best-fit buyers and invite them into the next-level offer, crediting what they already paid.
- Prefer "second helping" over publicly reopening a closed cart.
- Always include a strong risk-reversal/guarantee in premium offers — roughly 3-4x higher conversion with guarantees present, per Travis's own testing.
- Sales during a launch typically dip in the middle (a "lull") — increase touchpoint frequency, don't pull back.
- When people object "I need more leads," reframe: the real problem is usually not knowing what to do with the leads they already have, not lead volume.
- Structure new-hire pay by role: junior tapper ~$20/hr + ~$100/sale bonus; senior pay depends on offer price point and funnel position.
- Post open roles inside your own audience/group first.
- Lead copy with the objection or fear the prospect already has, then resolve it directly.
- Tell client-transformation stories with a clear internal/identity shift, not just the external result.

**Notable example**
A tarot-reading business struggled charging upfront for readings; switched to free taster sessions ("sampling"/"second helping") — attendance jumped to 33 from a couple, about a third converting to paid.

## Module 11: The Investor's Mindset

**Core teaching**
Approach sales/marketing outcomes like a positive-expectancy investor, not a "did I win or lose" gambler. If a single "yes" massively outweighs the cost of many "no"s, keep taking the action regardless of short-run losses — don't personalize a string of no-replies. This is the antidote to the common "day four" collapse in motivation.

**Named framework/terms**
- **The Fourth Day** — the point in any new effort where initial excitement wears off and setbacks start landing; the critical moment to lean in rather than quit.
- **Investor mindset vs. winning/losing mindset** — wealthy/investor thinking asks "what's the expected payoff," accepting many small losses because the occasional big win makes the math overwhelmingly positive.
- **Law of expectancy** — keep taking positive-EV actions and results bear out over volume; individual "tails" shouldn't be over-analyzed emotionally.
- **The BAM moment** — the instant in a pitch where the prospect realizes "this is what's been missing all along." Built from: what's the problem, why haven't they solved it, what don't they like about existing solutions, why is this different.
- **Short putt / long putt / out of range** — categorizing leads by proximity to buying: short putt = close to yes; long putt = trust exists but plan/price unclear; out of range = still has basic credibility doubts, needs top-of-funnel, not a direct pitch.
- **Small but meaningful result / "useful but incomplete"** — the first quick win engineered for a new member that creates momentum toward the real transformation. "Useful but incomplete" gives just enough to want to spend more.
- **Rainmaker vs. T3** — Rainmaker = high-volume, lower-price, automated campaigns to large lists; T3 = personal, conversational sequences for higher-ticket offers.
- **Licensing as a hook** — pairing/borrowing someone else's name, credibility, or IP onto your own offer to multiply perceived value.

**Concrete actionable rules**
- When facing the "fourth day" dip, don't stop to analyze why — take the next action; reserve deep analysis for after validating the campaign isn't fundamentally broken.
- Evaluate whether to keep running a campaign by checking directional signals on smaller test pieces, not just final sales.
- If targeting is off, consider adding a second offer variant for the segment actually showing up, rather than killing the campaign.
- Identify the correct "first domino" small-but-meaningful result by listening to what actually excites new members, not by assuming.
- Use symptom-based outreach when reconnecting with a warm-but-inactive prospect ("are you still experiencing X") rather than jumping straight to the offer.
- Don't box campaigns into arbitrary calendar deadlines; let sales velocity dictate the cadence.
- For high-volume/lower-price offers to large cold lists, use Rainmaker-style order forms; for high-ticket, use personal T3 conversations.
- Skip standalone webinar registration pages when possible — sending traffic straight to an order-form page with the webinar embedded reportedly converts better.
- When paying commission to contractors, prefer percentage-of-revenue over large fixed retainers.

**Notable example**
A hired copywriter/manager quit one day before a real-estate campaign turned a corner — the first sale landed the day after they quit, and the campaign closed roughly $90k off a single email.
