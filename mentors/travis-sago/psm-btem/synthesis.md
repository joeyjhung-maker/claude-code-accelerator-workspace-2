## Circle of Money (Call 1)

- **60% of results = the LIST** (who you're actually talking to). **30% = the OFFER** (what +
  when — the same underlying thing can be repositioned near-infinitely just by slicing the list
  differently). **10% = the COPY.**
- Reinforces an instinct worth stating plainly when a content plan leans too hard on copy polish:
  segmentation and targeting do more work than a better-written sentence.
- The "circle" is a referral flywheel with four legs that all have to hold for it to compound:
  **Bonding** (Rapid Bonding Mojo, below), **Marketing** (Symptomatic Messaging), **Sales** (CAP,
  below), **Delivery**. A gap in any one leg stops the whole thing growing — useful diagnostic
  question when something isn't growing: which leg is actually broken?

## Triangle of Insight — a repeatable email-outlining tool (Call 1)

Three-part skeleton for any nurture email:
1. **Symptom** — something concrete and visible (you could point at it, see it in a movie scene).
   This becomes the subject line.
2. **Wisdom** — a perspective-shift, a small "aha" nugget.
3. **Metaphor / story / analogy** — ties the symptom and wisdom together (a movie, book, or
   personal story).

**Write order:** Symptom → Wisdom → Metaphor first (outline), then when drafting: subject line
restates the symptom → body opens with the metaphor/story → delivers the wisdom → transitions to
the offer.

This is directly usable as a template for B2B Leads Lab's weekly NAP posts — a candidate structure
for whatever primes `/storm-skool` or `/produce-skool` against a `mechanic: NAP` brief, rather than
each NAP being ad hoc.

## NAP vs. Harvest campaigns, and the Garden metaphor (Call 2)

- **NAP (Nurture and Pick) = the daily campaign** — Travis's claim: most money is made here.
  "Pick a different tree" each day (rotate through a handful of angles/offers — don't need more
  than a handful).
- **Harvest campaign = a launch-style push** (a sales event, time-boxed).
- **Garden metaphor:** nurture the ROOT (the relationship), not the fruit directly — a healthy
  root takes care of the fruit (sales) on its own. This is very likely the actual origin of
  [[naps-nurture-and-picks]] already standing in this workspace — worth linking back there.
- **Harvest-specific pricing tip, counter to the usual instinct:** get the price out EARLY, don't
  hide it — then build the benefit case afterward so it tilts back in your favor. Runs opposite to
  "build value, then reveal price at the end."

## 4/6 Human Needs — a content-variety checklist (Call 2)

Only 3 of the 6 (Tony Robbins-style) human needs are covered in these notes — treat as a partial
list, not the full framework:
- **Certainty** — your systems, values, consistent point of view.
- **Uncertainty** — variety, shaking things up.
- **Significance** — rank, status, "inner circle" naming, special tactics/systems that make the
  reader feel chosen.

Useful as a rotation check on a run of NAP posts so they don't all keep hitting the same need.

## Hell>Heaven Hook mechanics (Call 3) — additive to Make 'Em Beg

- Structure: 1) highlight the gap, 2) create tension, 3) release the offer.
- Offer-positioning reframe, distinct from "sell against future pain": find what the reader
  DISLIKES about their current solution/approach, and offer an easier alternative — "you don't
  need to do this because you hate it, try it my way instead."
- **Homework for generating infinite angles (do this as an actual exercise, not just read it):**
  10 symptoms of hell, 10 visions of heaven (must be PICTURABLE — sell "a secure man," not "true
  love"), 10 things they dislike about their current approach (these become hooks — "takes too
  long," "too much effort," "too complex," etc).
- Every email needs one of each: Symptom/Vision/Obstacle + Wisdom/Insight + Story/Metaphor — this
  is the Triangle of Insight again, restated as a per-email checklist.

## Offer mechanics (Call 4, "Catch-up Call")

- **Offer hack:** frame any offer as "Give me ___, and I'll give you ___." Forces clarity and
  often reveals a sharper, sexier version of an existing offer.
- **Don't require an internal/identity change to buy.** Put the obstacle OUTSIDE the person
  (their tools, their list, their channel) rather than framing it as something wrong with them —
  identity-change asks sell poorly.
- **The "S" trick:** pluralize the mechanism noun to shift the emphasis onto the thing doing the
  work instead of the person's effort. "Convert clicks into sales" (implies work) →
  "**Converts** clicks into sales" (implies the thing does the work for you). A genuinely concrete,
  checkable copy-polish rule — worth adding as a line-level check in `copy-rubric.md`.
- **Find the ONE limiting belief, don't try to handle every objection:** "I can't do this because
  ___" — the blank IS the real obstacle. Watch for this exact shape showing up in DMs/comments and
  build the answer to that one blank, not a general objection-handling wall of text.
- **Sell bolt-on, not overhaul.** Frame the offer as adding to what the reader already does, never
  as replacing their model or changing their life. This generalizes
  [[jv-additive-framing]] (currently scoped to JV/partner audiences only) — the same logic applies
  to any B2B offer, not just partner deals.
- **Group framing beats direct-ask framing for limited spots.** "Do you want to join?" raises
  defenses. "I've already set up a group to do X — want in?" lowers them and adds FOMO/belonging.
  Directly applicable to how the B2B Leads Lab beta/guinea-pig spots get framed.
- Don't abandon a new (especially bigger-ticket) offer too fast — bigger decisions take longer to
  convert on, budget more patience before calling an offer dead.

## CAP Sales Mojo — Child / Adult / Parent (Call 5, "Big Ticket Email Mojo")

- CAP describes three buying mindsets the same prospect moves through:
  - **Child** — dopamine and desire. It does not care how the offer works; it sees the BIG outcome
    and reacts, “I want, I want, I want.” Give it a high-outcome, emotionally obvious Heaven
    Island it can want immediately.
  - **Adult** — mechanism and process. It asks HOW the result happens and wants the steps, plan and
    practical route from the current state to the outcome.
  - **Parent** — skepticism. It begins nitpicking, raises objections, asks why the method works and
    demands proof and results from other people before trusting the crossing.
- **Micro application:** one ad, email or post can move through Child → Adult → Parent inside the
  same piece. But a short T1-style email can be deliberately pure Child: high dopamine, almost no
  process or proof, built to maximise replies rather than close the entire argument.
- **Macro application:** structure campaigns in the same sequence. Begin with Child posts that
  dramatise the large desired outcome. Move into Adult content that shows the mechanism, process
  and steps. Finish by hitting the Parent's “sharks”: objections, skepticism, proof and results.
- **Hell Island → Heaven Island → Boat:** the prospect wants to escape Hell Island and reach a
  vivid Heaven Island. The dangerous water between them is full of Parent-level sharks. The
  solution is the boat; the campaign must make the boat and the crossing visual enough that the
  Adult understands the route and the Parent trusts it to arrive safely.
- **Buying sequence:** Child wants the destination → Adult understands the boat and crossing →
  Parent tests whether the boat is safe and proven → only then does the purchase happen.
- **Reconciling Joey's model with Travis's Call 5 satisfiers (2026-09-28).** The states above are
  Joey's working doctrine and stand. Travis's own buying-process description on the same call
  matches them: Child says "oh wow"; Adult asks "how does it work? If no logic, the process
  stops"; Parent asks "will this work? Maybe not for me. Sounds like a scam." Only two of his
  "what satisfies it" tools looked misfiled, and both slot in once you split the question each
  state is asking:

  | State | The question it asks | Tools that answer it (Joey + Travis merged) |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Child** | "Do I want that?" | Overt, blatant, clear benefit. Hit the heart. Never cloudy. Meaningful AND instantly understood. Find the exact wording that triggers the want. |
  | **Adult** | "How does it work? Does it make sense?" | The mechanism and steps (UMP/UMS). **Kitchen-table logic:** "it's like this…" metaphors that make the boat visible. **Proof that shows the process:** a case study walking through how someone actually crossed. |
  | **Parent** | "Will it work *for me*? Is it a scam?" | Two different sharks, two answers. **"Is it proven?"** needs results from people like me, objections and obstacles answered. **"Haven't I seen this before?"** needs dramatic difference: a USP, something clearly new, "I'm the only one doing X without Y." It breaks the pattern-match to the last thing that burned them. |

  - **Testimonials do double duty.** A how-it-happened story feeds the Adult; a it-worked-for-
    someone-like-me result feeds the Parent. Travis filed proof under Adult (proof as logic);
    Joey files it under Parent (proof as trust). Both are right. Decide which job each proof
    piece is doing.
  - **Dramatic difference is Parent work, not Adult work.** The Parent judges "work / won't work"
    by comparing against past experience. More proof won't beat "I've tried this before." Only
    a visibly different boat does.
  - **CAP = CDCDCD, seen from the buyer's side.** Travis's offer tests (Clearly Desirable, Clearly
    Doable, Clearly Different; see `../hopper-millions/synthesis.md`) are the same three states
    from the seller's side. **Clearly Desirable** feeds the Child. **Clearly Doable** feeds the
    Adult: the boat is visible and the crossing is easy ("we handle the packages, just ship us
    the Excel file"). **Clearly Different** answers the Parent's "haven't I seen this before?"
    CDCDCD is how you build the offer. CAP is the order you sell it in. Proof is the one extra
    piece CAP needs: the Parent's "is it proven?" shark.
  - **Travis's worked example**, "sell big ticket $1–5k without the phone": Child = the overt
    benefit; Adult = the UMP/UMS; Parent = "I'm the only person doing/teaching X without Y."
- **Two different "Parents" — don't mix them up.** The reader's Parent *state* (skepticism) is
  separate from the writer's Parent *voice* (tone). All three tapes run in the reader's head
  at once. Nurturing Parent = same side of the table ("I get it, I understand"). Critical
  Parent = judging or scolding.
- **Tonality rule:** write as the Nurturing Parent roughly 70% of the time, Adult roughly 30%.
  Avoid Critical Parent almost entirely.
- **A usable judging checklist for any finished piece of copy** (candidate addition to
  `copy-rubric.md`, distinct from its existing checks): does it hit an overt Child-level benefit?
  Does the Adult understand the mechanism and steps? Does it survive Parent skepticism with proof
  and the relevant objections answered? For a deliberately pure-Child T1, judge it on desire and
  reply-generation rather than penalising it for withholding process and proof.
- **Rainmaker Campaign — a 10-14 day launch structure:** needs a hero (the prospect) with a
  worthwhile goal, a simple, non-personal enemy/obstacle (ideally "no time," "no knowledge" — NOT
  a group of people, avoid blaming gurus/government/other people), and a resolution. One-line
  plot: "Pat's on Hell Island. He wants to get to Heaven Island. What's stopping him?" Plot
  questions: What do they want? What's stopping them (the enemy, simple to see)? What's missing?
  Can it tie into a current event? A strong
  candidate structure for the B2B Leads Lab Sept 30 paid-conversion push
  (`clients/flexxable/b2b-leads-lab/project.md`).

## Ben Settle's views (from the "Effective Sales Emails" section)

- Staying inside your market's world, talking about what genuinely matters to them, means you
  can't bore them — permission to write MORE when you're in the right territory, not a reason to
  hold back.
- Pitch in every email. Readers should always know you're a business that sells things — being
  upfront about that reads as more honest, not less, and performs better than hiding it.
- Be aggressive about letting the wrong people off your list. Lukewarm/cold subscribers dilute
  response rate; you want people actively looking for a solution, no one else.
- Sequential selling: sell ONE flagship product first, expand afterward. Removes decision paralysis
  compared to offering everything at once.

## The general "infinite angles" method (recurring across every call)

Every email = one Symptom/Vision/Obstacle + one Wisdom/Insight + one Story/Metaphor. Rotate just
the symptom/obstacle and you get near-unlimited unique, relevant angles without needing more
offers. An email doesn't need to be relevant to EVERYONE on the list each send — higher relevance
to a smaller slice beats broad mediocre relevance to everyone; the people it doesn't apply to just
skip it, and that's fine.
