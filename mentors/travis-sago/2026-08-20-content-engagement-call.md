## The core principle: run people toward their next challenge, don't just excite them

Excitement is part of the formula, but it isn't the mechanism. Conversion happens when you **run
someone up against their actual next obstacle, let them genuinely experience it, help them over
it, then immediately run them to the next one.** Repeat. Excitement without a real obstacle to
clear in between doesn't move people.

## Map vs. terrain — test the customer journey, don't assume it

A map is not the terrain. Travis's example: running your hand across a raised-relief globe and
feeling the Andes — the flat map's contour lines don't convey how big the mountains actually are.
**You can map out a customer's journey/obstacle sequence, but you won't know if it's right until
you test it against real people** — polls, direct engagement, watching what they actually say and
do.

**Live example — his "AI Royalty Rockstars" community:** the market is saturated with "how to
build AI" content (every YouTube channel teaches this already), so people already have that piece.
What they DON'T have is knowing how to sell what they built. He mapped four major pathways: (1)
need something to sell, (2) need someone to sell it to, (3) need to generate leads, (4) need to
convert those leads — each one deep enough to be "four years of content" on its own. He polled the
group to test the map against the real terrain and found people split in a way the map didn't
originally predict: some wanted to build, but a good chunk had already built something and wanted
to **broker/sell via affiliate instead** — getting a piece without building anything themselves.
Testing the map surfaced a path he hadn't fully accounted for.

## The Evan story — never sell the reward that's further away than their actual next obstacle

Evan is tied up in a straitjacket, in a locked room, in a locked ward, in a locked asylum. He does
not care that you know a great steak place outside — he's consumed entirely by the straitjacket
right in front of him. Sequence:

1. Free him from the straitjacket (solve the obstacle directly in front of him). He's grateful.
2. Give him a few minutes. He peeks through the security window and SEES steak and ping pong
   happening in the ward — but the door's locked.
3. NOW you can sell him the combination for $10 — because that's become his actual next visible
   obstacle, not an abstract future reward.
4. He gets out, plays ping pong, eats steak — and within five minutes he's bored and wanting the
   next thing ("there's no girls in here"). That restlessness is the engine, not a flaw — humans
   always want the next thing, and that's what keeps the whole sequence moving.

**The marketer's mistake:** talking about "Hooters across the street" (a reward several steps
downstream) while the person is still locked in the straitjacket. They won't move, not because the
offer is bad, but because you're too far ahead of where they actually are. **Only ever sell the
next obstacle actually in front of them, never a reward several steps past it.**

## "Diagnose but withhold" — what Travis is actually doing (direct answer to a question from a
prior session)

Joey asked directly whether hiding the answer and asking the audience to comment/guess is a
deliberate mechanic. Travis's answer: **he's trying to find out their CURRENT PLAN** — how
they're already trying to solve the problem, ideally observed outside any sales context where they
know he's trying to sell them (so it's not performance for him). Two reasons this beats just
telling them the answer directly:

1. **If he just tells them, they'll forget it in five minutes.** Telling isn't the same as
   pre-selling.
2. **He'd rather get the AUDIENCE to do the pre-selling and convincing for him** than do it
   himself — see the poll example below for exactly how.

## Audience-driven pre-selling via consensus polls

Concrete example, run in his "Ronin" group: a two-option poll — *"Are you focusing more of your
time on 1) gaining more skills, or 2) gaining more assets?"* Nearly everyone answered "2."

This does several jobs at once:
- **Tests where the audience actually is** (the map-vs-terrain check).
- **The audience pre-sells itself.** Anyone who didn't answer "2" sees a wall of "2, 2, 2, 2" and
  thinks "there must be something to this" — the audience does the convincing, not Travis. Six
  lines of poll copy do the persuasion work a whole sales pitch would otherwise need to do.
- **Generates a segmented lead list for free.** He can identify people who answered "2" (the
  "right" answer) but AREN'T yet in his paid program (Shogun) and reach out to them directly as
  warm leads — they've just told him, publicly, that they already believe the thing he's selling.

**A second-voice observation on the same call, worth keeping:** people gravitate to the answer
that signals they already know the "right" answer — even when there isn't an objectively correct
one. The poll isn't really measuring belief, it's partly measuring identity-signaling. Doesn't
change how to use the tactic, but explains why the consensus effect is so strong and fast.

## Short posts + "chum the water" — the resource-allocation answer for running multiple groups

Travis is explicit that this is how he manages writing content across many groups simultaneously
(directly relevant — this is Joey's exact situation from Sept 1): **he'd rather the audience
generate the content/conviction than write long-form (300-word) posts for every group himself.**

The fishing metaphor: throw several different short "chum" posts (different angles/hooks) into the
water, see which one the audience actually bites on, then **throw more of that specific type** —
and that's when longer-form content is worth writing, once you already know the angle works. Don't
guess which angle deserves a long post — probe short, then invest long only where you've already
seen a bite.
