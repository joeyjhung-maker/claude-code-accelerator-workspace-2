## The vocabulary (reuse these terms — don't reinvent them)

- **Lead vs. engaged lead** — a lead is just a contactable person; an *engaged lead* is one who's shown interest (opted in, replied, followed). The book's whole method is about maximizing engaged leads, not raw contact counts.
- **Core Four** — the only four ways one person can let anyone know about anything: warm outreach (1-to-1, warm audience), posting content (1-to-many, warm audience), cold outreach (1-to-1, cold audience), paid ads (1-to-many, cold audience).
- **Lead magnet** — a narrow, complete solution to one problem, valuable enough to charge for, that reveals a second problem your core offer solves. Three types: reveal-a-problem (diagnosis), sample/trial, one-step-of-a-multi-step-process.
- **Give:ask ratio** — how much value you give before you make an offer. "Give until they ask" is Hormozi's own escalation of Gary Vee's "jab jab jab right hook."
- **More / Better / New** — the escalation ladder for any Core Four channel that's plateaued: do more of it, then improve the biggest drop-off point (the constraint), then only once both are exhausted, expand to a new placement/platform/channel.
- **LTGP:CAC** — lifetime gross profit vs. cost to acquire a customer. 3:1 is the floor; below that is a business-model problem (raise LTGP), a CAC more than 3x industry average is an advertising problem (fix targeting/offer), otherwise it's a sales problem, not an advertising one.
- **Client-financed acquisition (CFA)** — structure the offer (with an immediate upsell if needed) so the customer pays back CAC within the first 30 days. This is what removes cash as the constraint on scaling ad spend.
- **Rule of 100 / Open to Goal** — commit to 100 primary actions/day for 100 days as the floor; "open to goal" is the harder version — you work until you hit the *outcome* number, not a fixed time block.
- **Lead getters** — the four ways other people advertise on your behalf instead of you: customer referrals, employees, agencies, affiliates. Each is a way to trade one-time setup cost for compounding leverage.
- **Size-of-the-pie fallacy** — assuming your niche is "saturated" because the 3–4 competitors you can see must be splitting the whole market. Almost always false; the addressable audience on any platform dwarfs what a small business has touched.

## Get Understanding (offers & lead magnets)

The whole book rests on a definition fight Hormozi has with himself for six months: a lead is just "someone you can contact" — worthless on its own. The real output of advertising is the *engaged* lead. Everything downstream — Core Four, lead getters, scaling — is really "how do I get more engaged leads, cheaper, more reliably."

Seven-step lead magnet build: pick the narrow problem → pick how to solve it (reveal/sample/step) → pick delivery format (software/info/service/physical) → test the name (headline > image > subheadline, in that order) → make it easy to consume (every format at once) → make it genuinely good ("give away the secrets, sell the implementation") → give a clear CTA with a real reason to act now (scarcity, urgency, or just a stated reason — any reason beats no reason).

**Where this already lives in the workspace:** this is functionally what a NAP is — [[naps-nurture-and-picks]] — a free, narrow-problem-solving asset (poll, handraiser) that earns the right to a later ask. The book's insistence that a lead magnet should be good enough to charge for is a useful gut-check the next time a NAP feels thin.

## Get Leads (the Core Four)

### Warm outreach
1-to-1 to people who already gave you permission to contact them. Ten-step process: build the list (phone/email/social contacts — everyone has more than they think) → pick one platform → personalize the opener → 100 reach-outs/day, 3 attempts each → reply using **A-C-A** (Acknowledge, Compliment, Ask a question toward your offer) → make the easiest possible offer (free, for the first five, in exchange for a testimonial) → work through hidden-cost objections by asking "what would make this worth it?" → cycle to the next platform → raise price every 5 sign-ups once referrals start (80% off → 60% off → 40% off...) → re-warm the list with Dean Jackson's 9-word email: *"Are you still looking to [4-word desire]?"*

**Direct hit for this workspace:** the 9-word email *is* the IAA mechanism — [[iaa-mechanism-old-leads]] — reactivating old/dead leads with a single low-friction question rather than a pitch. Hormozi's benchmark math (1-in-5 reply, 1-in-5 of those take the free offer, 1-in-4 of those convert) is a useful sanity-check ratio for B2B Leads Lab warm-list plays.

### Post free content
Content isn't the asset — the *audience* is. A content unit = Hook (topic + headline + format matched to platform) → Retain (lists, steps, or stories to keep curiosity open) → Reward (actually satisfy what the hook promised — audience growth is the only honest scorecard). Headline components worth stacking: recency, relevancy, celebrity, proximity, conflict, unusual, ongoing.

Seven lessons worth keeping loose in the back pocket: talk "how I" not "how to" (experience is unfalsifiable); repeat yourself constantly (most of the audience only sees a fraction of what you post); narrow to a puddle before claiming a pond (local/niche credibility before general); your best free content quietly becomes your sales team's tool library; free content retains *paying* customers too, not just prospects; there's no such thing as too long, only too boring; post live rather than pre-scheduling — the immediate feedback loop sharpens the work.

### Cold outreach
1-to-1 to strangers. Three new problems strangers create, solved in order: get a way to contact them (scrape → broker → manual, in that order of effort) → personalize + big-fast-value (the lead magnet has to be blow-their-mind good, not just decent — Hormozi's own 3x take-rate jump came from upgrading the offer, not the script) → volume (automate delivery and distribution where ethical, follow up more times in more ways, and cycle back through the whole list every 3–6 months because circumstances change even when the message didn't).

His personal cold-outreach build took nearly a year to become profitable despite an aggressive plan — worth remembering as a expectation-setting anchor, not just a success story.

**This is the direct model for B2B Leads Lab's lumpy-mail cold outreach** — [[b2b-leads-lab-project]]. The "personalize, then big fast value" sequencing and the "expect a long ramp, don't judge on week one" lesson both map onto Dan's cold-mail funnel more literally than any other chapter in the book.

### Paid ads
1-to-many to strangers, paid reach instead of earned reach. Four problems in order: platform choice → targeting (lookalike from your best list, layered with interest/demo filters) → the ad itself → capturing contact info (a landing page that visually and verbally matches the ad — no "click to close" break in the promise).

Ad anatomy, three parts: **Call-out** (label / yes-question / if-then / ridiculous-result — verbal or visual, aimed at the cocktail-party-effect of a name being heard across noise) → **Value** via the **What-Who-When** framework (What = the 4 value-equation drivers and their opposites; Who = whose *status* changes when they buy — spouse, kids, rivals, not just the buyer; When = show consequences across their own past/present/future timeline) → **CTA** (spell out the literal next action, short URL, stack urgency/scarcity/a-stated-reason).

Money mechanics: three phases (track → lose money on purpose while testing, budgeted at 2x first-30-day cash per test → print money once profitable, then reverse-budget from "how many customers do I want," padding 20% for scaling inefficiency). LTGP:CAC ≥ 3:1 is the health floor. Client-financed acquisition (recoup CAC within 30 days via an immediate upsell) is what unlocks aggressive scaling without needing outside cash.

**Direct application:** this is the Meta-ads chapter the workspace's `/produce` and `/brief` pipeline already half-implements (value equation, awareness routing) — the What-Who-When "Who" axis (status effect on people around the buyer, not just the buyer) is the one angle least represented in the current brief/hook toolkit and worth testing on a Flexxable client brief.

### More Better New
Once a channel plateaus: **More** first (double the volume — Rule of 100 applied per-channel) → **Better** second (find the biggest drop-off step — the *constraint* — and test only that, one variable per platform per week, log every result so you're never starting from zero) → **New** only once more/better have flat returns (new placement on a known platform → new platform → new Core Four channel entirely, in that order).

The "constraint" logic is the same 80/20 idea already active in this workspace's account-read skill (bracket by KPI, find the real bottleneck) — this just gives it Hormozi's specific ordering discipline: don't jump to "new" until "more" and "better" are actually exhausted, not just uncomfortable.

## Get Lead Getters

Leverage framing: doing the Core Four yourself is linear (more input = more leads); getting other people to do it is where growth stops being linear. Four flavors, roughly in the order most businesses naturally reach them:

- **Customer referrals** — the highest-leverage, lowest-cost lead source because it's the only one that can be *exponential* (referrals > churn = compounding growth with zero extra spend). Two reasons most businesses get too few: the product isn't remarkable, and nobody asks. Six referral-generating levers map directly onto ad anatomy (sell better customers → set better expectations → get people better results → make wins land faster → keep improving the product → always have a next thing to sell them). Seven concrete ways to *ask*: one-sided or two-sided cash/credit incentives, asking at the moment of purchase, using a referral as a negotiation chip, timed referral events, always-on referral messaging, and unlockable bonuses.
- **Employees** — the "internal Core Four": every method for getting customers has a direct equivalent for getting hires (warm outreach → ask your network, cold outreach → recruiting, content → job postings, paid ads → promoted postings). Training model: **Document → Demonstrate → Duplicate** — write the checklist, walk them through it, then watch them run it and fix the checklist, not the person, when it breaks. Return math: `payroll ÷ engaged leads = cost per lead`, run into the same LTGP:CAC test as any other channel before deciding a hire is or isn't working.
- **Agencies** — written from the *client's* seat (how to use an agency well: reframe from "outsource this forever" to "pay to learn this fast, then bring it in-house," with an explicit deadline and a training relationship, not just execution — downgrade to consulting-only once your team matches their results). But his 10-point "how to pick a good agency" checklist reads just as well flipped around as a positioning audit for an agency being hired: word-of-mouth reputation over paid-ad reputation, a waiting list, clear expectation-setting, long-term-strategy framing over short-term hacks, a genuinely good offer (dream outcome / proof / timeline / effort), and being expensive on purpose ("all good agencies are expensive, but not all expensive agencies are good").
- **Affiliates** — an independent business advertising your stuff to their audience in exchange for money or product. Six-step build: find affiliates who already reach your customer (categorize by what your best customers buy/where they go/what they do) → make them a money-making offer, not a product pitch → qualify them by getting them invested (make-them-a-customer or make-them-an-expert, both work by getting skin in the game) → decide what you pay for and use a 3-tier payout (partial for signing up, more for activating, full for sustained performance — this alone can materially improve blended LTGP:CAC) → launch with **whisper → tease → shout** (build curiosity months out, reveal specifics weeks out, hard CTA days out) → integrate long-term (give away your lead magnet inside their offer, sell your lead magnet separately, or let them sell your core offer directly — increasing depth of integration in that order).

**Direct application for this workspace:** the affiliate chapter is the clearest external validation of how [[JV Partnerships Map]] already operates — the "make them a customer/expert first," tiered payout, and whisper-tease-shout launch sequence are all things worth checking new JV partner onboarding against. See [[jv-webinar-frontend-ladder]] for how this workspace already prices JV front-ends; Hormozi's CAC-based payout math is a useful cross-check on whether a partner's commission structure is actually sustainable.

## Get Started (implementation)

**Rule of 100**: 100 primary actions/day for 100 days, per channel, as the volume floor — most people who say a channel "doesn't work" have done a fraction of the required volume (Hormozi's own flyer story: 300 flyers when the effective test size was thousands).

**Open to Goal**: work until the *outcome* number is hit, not until a time block ends — borrowed from gym sales floors ("hit 5 sign-ups, then you're done, however long it takes").

**One-page advertising plan**: pick the lead type (customer/affiliate/employee/agency) → pick Rule-of-100 or Open-to-Goal → fill in the daily action → run it until you can afford to hand it to someone else → repeat with employees as the new target. Deliberately not a long strategy document — a single page, filled out in five minutes, that leaves no room to fudge whether the work actually happened.

**Seven-level roadmap** (paraphrased, useful as a "where are we on this ladder" gut-check for a client or for Flexxable itself): (1) warm outreach only → (2) warm outreach + content, maxing personal capacity → (3) first hires take over a channel → (4) product good enough for real referral volume (~25%+ of customers) → (5) multiple channels, multiple platforms, multiple placements at once → (6) experienced department-head hires own whole channels without founder involvement → (7) unwritten — Hormozi hadn't crossed it at time of writing.
