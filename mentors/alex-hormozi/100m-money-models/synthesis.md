## The core idea

A **Money Model** is not one offer — it's a deliberate *sequence* of offers, ordered so each one gets paid for by the one before it. The target Hormozi sets: make enough profit from a customer in the **first 30 days** to cover the cost of getting *multiple more* customers — at that point cash stops being the constraint on how fast you can scale ad spend, because every dollar you spend comes back inside a month (any business can float 30 days on a credit card, so a 30-day payback effectively makes growth capital-free).

Four offer types, built **in this order, one stage at a time — never all four at once**:

1. **Attraction Offers** — turn strangers into customers (get cash)
2. **Upsell Offers** — get them to spend more, immediately (get more cash)
3. **Downsell Offers** — turn a "no" into a "yes" (get even more cash from the same leads)
4. **Continuity Offers** — keep them paying (get the most cash, over time)

Building all four before the first one is reliable is the failure mode he warns against explicitly: "perfect one offer at a time." Each stage should pay for the next before you build the next.

## Stage 1 — Attraction Offers (turn strangers into customers)

Discounts and free stuff exist on a continuum — "free," "$1," and "50% off" are the same lever at different strengths. Five plays:

- **Win Your Money Back** — customer pays up front, gets it back (cash or credit) if they hit a goal, take required actions, or both. Works because: it lowers perceived risk to zero, the *actions* required double as your best customers' habits (so baking them into the refund criteria improves outcomes for everyone), and — this is the counterintuitive part — **the money is made on the people who succeed, not the ones who fail**, because a successful customer is primed for the next offer. Rule of thumb: only run this if refund rate stays under ~5%; give store credit toward something *more expensive* (spread over time, not lump-sum) rather than cash back, since that keeps them a customer.
- **Giveaways** — advertise a big prize (with a stated dollar value to anchor it), pick one winner publicly, then privately offer everyone else who qualified a steep discount on the same thing ("consolation prize" framed as a gift, not a sales pitch). The whole entry list is pre-qualified because they self-selected into wanting the prize.
- **Decoy Offer** — advertise a free/cheap "decoy," but when a lead engages, present a stripped-down decoy *next to* a much better premium option. Most people take premium once the contrast is visible. Sell benefits, not features, in the ad; save feature detail for the sales conversation. Key discipline: strip the decoy down as far as reasonable and make the premium option genuinely something you want them to buy — a decoy that's obviously fake kills trust in everything else you sell.
- **Buy X Get Y Free** — reframe a discount as free stuff (buy 1 get 2 free costs the same as 33% off 3, but converts better because "free" beats "discount" psychologically). Always give more free than paid, and raise base prices *before* running this so margin survives.
- **Pay Less Now or Pay More Later** — give a choice: pay full price later (with a card on file and a conditional satisfaction guarantee), or pay a steep discount now. Removes all risk for the buyer, gets a card on file either way, and sets up an immediate upsell conversation once they've committed to "later."

## Stage 2 — Upsell Offers (whatever you offer next)

Core insight: **the first thing you sell is rarely where the profit is** — it's the second, third, fourth offer (his burger-shop math: $0.25 profit on the burger alone, $3.00 once fries/drink/supersize stack on). Four plays:

- **The Classic Upsell** — your core offer reveals the next problem; solve it immediately, while they're still there ("you can't have X without Y" — car without insurance, bike without a helmet). "Do you want anything else?" gets a defensive "no" that's actually psychologically primed as agreement to what was just offered — a genuinely clever framing worth testing in sales scripts.
- **Menu Upsell** — four-part sequence: *unsell* what they don't need (builds trust, clears the runway), *prescribe* exactly what they do need (removes the buy/don't-buy decision by presenting it as already decided), offer an *A/B choice* between two things that both convert (never yes/no), then *card-on-file* to remove friction from paying. Almost anything can become an A/B choice (flavor, timing, quantity, delivery speed).
- **Anchor Upsell** — present a premium option (5–10x price) first. Expect "the gasp." Come to the rescue with the real (still-solid) offer, which now looks cheap by comparison. Must be a genuine offer you'd be happy to sell — a fake anchor reads as fake and burns trust the same way a fake decoy does. Some people do buy the anchor; that's pure upside.
- **Rollover Upsell** — credit some or all of a past purchase toward a bigger next purchase. Used for: re-engaging lapsed customers (a "winback campaign"), rescuing an upset customer as an alternative to a straight refund, or even poaching a *competitor's* upset customers by rolling over what they still owe elsewhere. Price the next offer at least 4x the rolled-over credit so a full-value rollover is still only a ~25% discount.

## Stage 3 — Downsell Offers (what to offer when they say no)

Ground rule that governs everything in this section: **never discount the same thing to close a sale** — that trades short-term revenue for long-term trust, and customers compare notes. Change *how* they pay or *what* they get instead. Three plays, escalating in that order when someone hesitates:

- **Payment Plan Downsells** — reframe interest as a "pay in full and save" discount rather than a surcharge for spreading payments (same math, better framing). Step down through: 3rd-party financing / credit card / layaway → half now half later (aligned to their payday) → check they actually still want it (only proceed if they rate desire 8+/10) → three payments → evenly spread payments → free trial as the last resort. Align billing to paycheck dates to cut declined-payment rates.
- **Trial With Penalty** — free trial, but a fee kicks in if they skip the activities that make them a good long-term customer (same criteria you'd use for a Win-Your-Money-Back offer, run in reverse). Always take a card on file; explain the fee *after* getting the card, not before — resistance is much lower that way. This converts more people into real product usage than an ordinary free trial does, because the penalty creates actual engagement.
- **Feature Downsells** — lower price by removing a feature, not by discounting the same thing. Remove highest-value features first (which paradoxically often re-sells them on the *original* full offer once they see what they'd be giving up) — this is the direct mechanism behind the friend's story of tripling close rate by offering to drop the money-back guarantee for a lower price, which mostly made people realize they wanted the guarantee.

## Stage 4 — Continuity Offers (keep them buying)

Continuity alone is a weak *attraction* offer (low cash today) but becomes the biggest total-revenue lever once it's stacked on top of stages 1–3 rather than used as the front door. Three plays:

- **Continuity Bonus Offers** — give something valuable (with a real dollar anchor) *for joining*, not for the membership itself — advertise the bonus, not the membership. Bonus should already exist / already be part of what you deliver anyway (cheapest kind to give). Pricing lever: to shift X% of people toward choosing continuity over a one-time payment, make the one-time price roughly `1 + X/30` times the monthly rate (e.g. ~1.33x gets 50% to pick continuity, ~2x gets 70%).
- **Continuity Discount Offers** — free time now or later in exchange for a term commitment. Four ways to apply the discount (up front, at the end/"earned," spread evenly, or after the first payment or two) — spreading it keeps cash flowing while still delivering the full value. One high-leverage, easy-to-miss detail: **bill in 4-week cycles, not calendar months** — 13 four-week cycles fit in a year vs. 12 months, an 8.3% revenue lift for changing nothing but the billing word.
- **Waived Fee Offers** — a real setup fee for month-to-month flexibility, waived if they commit to a term (typically a year+); if they cancel early, they owe the fee. Works because the cost to leave now roughly matches the cost to stay, so inertia favors staying. Best fit for longer commitments and slow-to-show-value services (SEO, weight loss, investing) where early emotional cancellation is the main churn risk.

Cancellation policy across all continuity types: state it clearly and up front. His own preferred default — cancellation fee equals whatever discount they were given, so canceling just reverts them to full price rather than penalizing them further. Offering an exit interview (with the fee waived for doing it) reportedly saves roughly a third of would-be cancellations and surfaces real product feedback.

## Putting it together

Build one stage at a time, and only add the next once the current one is reliable and (ideally) paying for the next stage's testing budget. His own Gym Launch build order: Decoy Offer (attraction) → Classic Upsell + Continuity Bonus (a $42k/yr annual program) → Payment Plan Downsell for the ones who said no to that → Menu Upsell + Feature Downsell once even the downsell wasn't converting everyone. Each addition compounded revenue, not replaced the prior stage.

Two structural notes worth keeping:
- **Affiliate products can fill Money Model gaps** — if you don't have a next thing to sell, sell someone else's thing for a commission rather than building a whole new product line. Ties directly to the affiliate mechanics in [[hormozi-100m-leads-synthesis]].
- **Simple scales, fancy fails** — the win usually isn't more products, it's more *ways to offer the same product* (quantity, frequency, bundling, payment terms).

## Where this matches, extends, or conflicts with what the workspace already does

- **Matches:** the discipline of building one offer stage at a time before adding the next mirrors [[The Lock]]'s "don't move to the next thing until this one rhymes" logic — a useful vocabulary bridge. The value-equation framing under Decoy/Anchor upsells (dream outcome, perceived likelihood, time delay, effort) is the same one already load-bearing in `/brief` and `/produce`.
- **Extends [[flexxable-offer-doc]] (ROYA):** if ROYA's offer doesn't yet have an explicit upsell/downsell/continuity sequence mapped out beyond the front-end, this book's four-stage structure is a ready-made checklist to audit it against — specifically whether there's a Classic Upsell wired in at the moment the front-end offer's "next problem" becomes obvious, and whether a Feature Downsell exists for prospects who balk at ROYA's stated price before assuming they need a cheaper offer.
- **Extends [[jv-webinar-frontend-ladder]]:** the $500–1K reversible front-end pricing this workspace already runs is functionally a Pay-Less-Now-or-Pay-More-Later / Win-Your-Money-Back hybrid (low-risk entry, real refund mechanic). The Anchor Upsell and Rollover Upsell plays are the two most directly transferable additions if the ladder ever needs a second rung — anchor a bigger commitment first, use rollover credit to make the front-end price count toward it.
- **Mostly new territory:** the specific downsell scripting (Payment Plan step-downs, Trial With Penalty, the "seesaw" close) isn't represented anywhere in this workspace's current sales/close material — worth flagging if Joey or Dan ever need to write a sales script rather than ad copy, since this workspace is ad-copy-focused and doesn't currently own the close.
