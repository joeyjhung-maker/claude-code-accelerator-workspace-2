# CORE PROBLEM

**The Leadbase Newbie has bought into the pay-per-lead model intellectually — but they cannot yet make it work in practice, and they are running out of time, money, and confidence before they find out if they ever will.**

They came in believing PPL was the smarter, cleaner alternative to the grind they were already in — or the business model that would finally give them independence. They've watched the videos. They've joined the community. They've picked a niche. Some have even spent real money on ads. But the gap between "understanding the model" and "making consistent money from the model" is proving much wider and lonelier than the content suggested. They are generating leads that don't qualify, pitching clients who won't pay upfront, and wondering — privately, never in the group — whether the problem is the model or whether the problem is them.

The ROYA pitch lands into this exact gap: they need a win. Not a theory. Not another framework. A real, provable, client-in-hand result — fast enough to restore their belief before it collapses entirely.

# TOP 5 BIGGEST FEARS

*These are the 3AM fears — the ones they do not post in the Skool group.*

---

**Fear 1: That they've already proven they can't do this — and they just haven't admitted it yet.**

This isn't fear of failure in the abstract. It's the specific, creeping suspicion that the evidence is already in. They burned the budget. The leads didn't convert. The client didn't pay. And instead of being a normal part of the learning curve, what if it was a verdict? What if every "mistake I made with my first client" post they read in the community is someone else's honest reckoning — and they're one more failed campaign away from writing their own?

*"I keep telling myself it's just a learning curve. But what if I'm just not built for this? What if I'm the guy who understands it perfectly and still can't make it work?"*

---

**Fear 2: That the people in their life who silently doubted them are going to be right.**

This is not about the antagonists — it's about the people who were careful and kind about it. The partner who said "I support you, I just want to make sure you have a backup plan." The parent who asked "so how does the money actually come in?" The friend who said nothing but whose face said everything. The newbie has been quietly, privately promising these people — through results, not words — that it was going to work. Every month it doesn't is a month closer to having to admit it out loud.

*"My girlfriend hasn't said anything in months. That's almost worse than if she did."*

---

**Fear 3: That the market is already too crowded and they got in too late.**

Ryan addresses this directly in his content, but the fear doesn't die with reassurance — it just goes underground. The newbie has seen the Skool group. 12,000 members in the free community. 350 in Pro. Every one of them is potentially targeting the same niches, the same clients, the same buyers. And when Joey's own strategy notes reference "Indian agencies selling crap leads" flooding the market and driving down perceived value, the newbie hears: *the floor is already falling out and I haven't even got my first real client yet.*

*"By the time I figure this out, is there even going to be room for me?"*

---

**Fear 4: That they will run out of money before they run out of excuses.**

This is the fear with a clock on it. Ad spend is real money. Software subscriptions are real money. The $99/mo for Leadbase Pro is real money. And the newbie is doing the math privately: how many more months can I fund this before I have to stop and call it? The fear isn't just of losing money — it's of reaching the moment where they have to make a choice between their dignity and their bank account, and choosing their bank account.

*"I've got maybe three more months before my wife starts asking questions I don't have good answers to."*

---

**Fear 5: That the problem isn't the model — it's their own sales ability — and that's not fixable.**

This is the deepest one, and the one they are least likely to ever say out loud. The model makes sense. The training is solid. The community is helpful. So if it's not working, the variable left is them. And specifically: their ability to get on a call with a stranger, present an offer, handle pushback, and close. The "how do we ask for upfront payment?" objection from the Sharks tab isn't just a tactical question — it's a proxy for a much darker private belief: *I am not a salesperson. I never have been. And this whole thing might require me to be one.*

*"I can build the funnel. I can run the ads. But when I'm actually on the phone with someone who's pushing back... I fold. And I don't know how to fix that."*

# PRIMARY TRANSFORMATION — THE MAGIC GENIE SOLUTION

**If a genie snapped their fingers, here is what the Leadbase Newbie's life looks like:**

---

**Outcome 1: They have a client — a real one, paying consistently, without begging for it.**

Not a trial. Not a "let's see how the first batch goes." A proper, ongoing relationship where money arrives on a schedule and they don't have to chase it. They know their CPL. They know their margin. They know what next month looks like. The uncertainty is gone — replaced by a system they can actually see working.

*"I want to wake up on the first of the month and know, roughly, what's coming in. I don't need to be rich. I need to not be anxious."*

---

**Outcome 2: They have proof — something they can point to when anyone asks.**

A case study. A screenshot. A number. Something concrete that ends the vague, uncomfortable "how's it going?" conversations with their partner, their parents, their mates. Not bragging — just evidence. The ability to say "here's what I did, here's what it produced" without flinching.

*"I just want to be able to answer the question honestly. Right now I can't do that."*

---

**Outcome 3: They feel like they belong in the room.**

The Skool wins channel stops feeling like a place that proves they're behind and starts feeling like a place that confirms they're on the same path. They post a win — even a small one — and it's real. Not performed. They stop reading other people's results with that complicated mix of inspiration and envy and start reading them with recognition.

*"I want to post something in that wins channel and mean it."*

---

**Outcome 4: The sales conversation stops being the thing they dread.**

They can get on a call with a prospect and not fold when there's pushback. They have an answer for "why should I pay upfront?" that doesn't make them feel like they're begging. The close doesn't feel like a confrontation — it feels like a natural end to a conversation they know how to have.

*"I want to stop dreading the phone call. I want to feel like I know what I'm doing when I'm on it."*

---

**Outcome 5: The people who doubted them — quietly or loudly — have to update their opinion.**

Not revenge. Just resolution. The partner who's been patiently waiting. The parent who's been carefully not asking. The friend who said "fair play for trying." They all get to find out they were wrong to worry — and the newbie gets to enjoy that quietly, without making a thing of it.

*"I don't need anyone to make a big deal of it. I just want them to stop worrying. And maybe stop treating it like a phase."*

# MARKET SPECIFICS

## What Does This Market Hinge Their Success On?

The Leadbase Newbie believes their success hinges on **landing the first real, paying, repeat client** — and specifically, on doing it in a way they can replicate. Not a one-off. Not a favour. A proper, arm's-length business transaction where a stranger paid them money for leads, the leads converted, and the client came back.

Beneath that, they hinge success on **getting a result they can prove** — because without proof, they cannot pitch the next client, they cannot post in the wins channel, they cannot answer the people in their life who are quietly waiting to see what happens. The proof is not just commercial — it is psychological. It is the thing that converts "I'm trying this" into "I do this."

There is also a secondary hinge that most of them haven't consciously named: **they need to believe the sales conversation is survivable.** The upfront-payment objection is the most-repeated pain point in the entire research dataset. It is not a pricing problem. It is a confidence problem. They need a version of the offer where the close doesn't require them to be someone they don't yet feel like.

---

## What Does This Market Have to Give Up By Giving Up Their Problem?

This is the piece most people miss about the Leadbase Newbie, and it matters enormously for how a ROYA pitch lands.

**Their problem gives them cover.**

As long as they haven't cracked it yet, they are still in the legitimate "learning phase." They can stay in the community, keep watching content, keep tweaking the funnel, keep saying "I'm working on it" — and none of that is dishonest, because they genuinely are. The problem protects them from the moment of reckoning: *I tried this properly, with real effort and real money, and it didn't work.*

Giving up the problem means giving up the excuse. And giving up the excuse means accepting either that they succeeded — or that they failed. The limbo is uncomfortable, but it is safer than the verdict.

There is also a smaller but real secondary comfort: **the community itself.** The Skool group is warm, active, and full of people in the same position. The struggle is, in a strange way, the membership fee. If they solve the problem, they graduate out of the newbie identity — and that identity, however frustrating, is at least known. Who they are on the other side of success is less certain.

---

## Who Does This Market Blame for Their Problem?

**Primary blame — external:**

- **The platforms.** Meta's Instant Forms are a recurring villain in the community — blamed for low-quality, low-intent leads that make the newbie look bad to their clients. The algorithm is blamed for targeting the wrong geography, the wrong demographic, the wrong intent level. The platform feels like it's working against them.

- **The buyers / clients.** "They won't pay upfront." "They blame the leads when their own follow-up is broken." "They ghost you when it's time to pay commission." The client is frequently cast as the unreliable, ungrateful, or dishonest party — even when (as Ryan and Tahir both gently imply) the real issue is the newbie's own qualification process or offer structure.

- **The market / competition.** "Indian agencies selling crap leads" driving down perceived value. Niches getting crowded. Buyers who've been burned before and now won't trust anyone. The market is framed as hostile territory that makes legitimate operators look bad by association.

**Secondary blame — internal (private, rarely voiced):**

- **Their own sales ability.** They suspect — but will not say — that the real bottleneck is them on the phone. The close. The upfront-payment conversation. The moment where they need to hold their ground and they don't.

- **Their own execution speed.** They know they over-research, over-build, and under-execute. The community explicitly calls this out ("a lot of beginners get stuck trying to build the perfect stack instead of actually getting offers out there"). They know it's true of them. They blame themselves for it quietly.

- **Their own niche choice.** When things aren't working, the niche is an easy internal target — even when (as Tahir repeatedly tells members) the problem is almost never the niche. The niche-switch impulse is partly a displacement of self-blame.

## Top 5 Biggest Objections to a ROYA Pitch Specifically

*These are not generic objections. These are the specific resistance points this segment would have to being pitched ROYA as a foot-in-the-door offer, grounded in the research.*

---

**Objection 1: "I'm already in Leadbase. Why do I need another program?"**

This is the first and most immediate wall. They've already committed — financially and psychologically — to Ryan's system. A second program feels like an admission that the first one wasn't enough, or like they're being distracted from the thing they're supposed to be focused on. The deeper fear underneath: *am I the kind of person who just keeps buying courses instead of doing the work?*

**What makes them respond:** ROYA has to be framed not as an alternative to Leadbase, but as the thing that makes Leadbase actually work — the missing piece between "understanding the model" and "getting the first client." It's not another course. It's the bridge.

---

**Objection 2: "I don't have a result yet — I'm not ready to pitch anything to anyone."**

The newbie is waiting until they feel ready before they go to market. They believe readiness is a prerequisite for action, not a product of it. ROYA, positioned as a way to get a client first and build from there, will feel backwards to them — they want to perfect the system before they sell it.

**What makes them respond:** The research shows this belief is explicitly named and corrected inside the community itself (Anna Ozon's "build before you validate" mistake, the "perfect stack" trap). ROYA needs to speak directly to this — the first win doesn't require perfection, it requires a different offer shape. One that's easier to close.

---

**Objection 3: "I can't afford to spend more money on something that might not work either."**

Budget is real and finite. The $750 burned by Jack Hodges, the $350/day Grace Weaver refunded, the $99/mo already going to Leadbase Pro — this market has already made financial commitments they can't fully justify yet. A new paid offer, however low-ticket, lands against that backdrop.

**What makes them respond:** Speed and specificity of the win. If ROYA can credibly promise a result that pays for itself — and fast enough to matter before the next billing cycle — the ROI framing overrides the cost objection. Hatim S. getting a first client in two weeks is the kind of proof point that works here. The offer needs its own version of that.

---

**Objection 4: "This sounds like it's pulling me away from lead gen — I want to do lead gen."**

The newbie came to Leadbase because they want to build a PPL business. ROYA, if pitched as a standalone offer or a detour, will feel like a distraction from the goal. "Get clients with ROYA first" can easily sound like "forget lead gen for now" — which they will resist.

**What makes them respond:** The foot-in-the-door framing has to be explicit and honest. ROYA is not instead of lead gen — it's the fastest way to get a client, a case study, and cash flow, so that when they do go to market with lead gen, they have something to stand on. The sequence has to be made clear: ROYA → win → credibility → lead gen offer. Not ROYA instead of lead gen.

---

**Objection 5: "I've already tried things that were supposed to work and they didn't. Why is this different?"**

This is the deepest objection and the hardest to shift. It is not really about ROYA — it is about accumulated disappointment. They've watched the YouTube videos. They've joined the community. They've run the ads. And the gap between what was promised and what happened is still wide. Another offer, however well-positioned, has to clear the bar of "this isn't just another thing I'm going to try and fail at."

**What makes them respond:** Specificity of mechanism + community proof that looks like them. Not Jacob Watson doing $160k in April — that's too far away. The Hatim S. "first client in 2 weeks" proof point is closer. What works best is proof from someone who was exactly where they are — isolated, uncertain, not yet winning — and got a specific, named result in a specific, believable timeframe. The community's own culture of honest "what went wrong" posts means they are unusually good at detecting performed success. The ROYA pitch has to feel like the Kyle Johannessen post, not the wins channel.

# SUMMARY: THE PSYCHOLOGICAL THROUGH-LINE FOR THE ROYA PITCH

The Leadbase Newbie is not primarily a tactics problem waiting to be solved. They are a **confidence and credibility crisis** looking for a way out.

They understand the model. They have the community. They have the content. What they do not have is the one thing that changes everything: **a result they can point to.** Not a promise of a result. Not a framework for getting a result. A result.

ROYA's foot-in-the-door positioning works with this segment because it offers exactly that — a faster, lower-friction path to the first win — without asking them to abandon what they're already building toward. The pitch that lands is not "try something new." It is: **"Here is how you finally get the thing you've been trying to get since you joined Leadbase."**

The pitch that fails is anything that sounds like another course, another system, another reason to keep learning instead of earning.

The emotional unlock is not excitement. It is **relief.** The feeling that someone has finally handed them the specific thing that was missing — not more information, but a way through.

People like seeing PROOF"*) adds a specific, sharp new layer to Fear 5 — it's not just "can I sell," it's now also "can I even produce creative that doesn't look fake at the volume the algorithm demands."

**New objection this adds to a ROYA pitch:** a variant of Objection 5 ("why is this different") — even if ROYA gets them a client, this segment may privately worry that keeping the ads working long enough to keep that client is its own unsolved problem. This reinforces rather than undercuts the foot-in-the-door logic: ROYA sidesteps needing to solve the ads-production problem before getting a first win, which is precisely the point.
