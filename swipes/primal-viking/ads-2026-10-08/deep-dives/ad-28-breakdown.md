## 1. Scroll-Stopper
- Eye path: (1) the beer glass, warped into a pot-belly bulge, with the headline printed on the bulge. (2) The headline itself, bold white caps on amber beer. (3) The supplement bottle at right, the same height and glow as the glass.
- Hook type: visual metaphor / curiosity-meets-call-out. The glass IS the beer belly. The drinker sees his own gut as a pint glass.
- The image makes the claim and the headline names the dilemma. The primary text then carries the story.

## 2. Copy (all text, by role)
Headline (on glass bulge, white caps): "CAN'T QUIT BEER BUT DON'T WANT BEER BELLY?"
Bottle label (product): "PRIMAL & VIKING" / "ULTRA-POTENT ARCTIC HERBS" / "REINDEER ORGAN SUPERFOOD MIX" / "NATURAL TESTOSTERONE BOOSTER" / "DIETARY SUPPLEMENT 60 CAPSULES" (approx, label partly small). Viking head with antlers logo.
CTA button/badge: none in the image.
Primary text (as provided, begins): "I'm 48, and according to my friends... my beer belly is undefeated. 'You can't see your feet anymore.' 'Brewing something?' 'Looks like your six-pack retired and turned into a family pack.' Comedy gold. Until I get home and try to tie my shoes..."
(Rest of the primary text was not supplied. Transcription above is the full opener given.)

## 3. Target Avatar and Awareness
- Avatar: men in their 40s (named age 48) who drink beer regularly, have a gut, and joke about it. Call-out is by behaviour ("can't quit beer"), not just age.
- Awareness: problem-aware leaning solution-aware. He knows the belly is a problem and has not found a way that lets him keep beer. The image promises a "have it both ways" resolution. The quiz CTA suggests the funnel handles the product-education step.

## 4. Angle and Mechanism
- Angle: permission angle. Keep the beer, lose the belly. It removes the sacrifice (quit drinking) that every other belly-fat solution demands.
- Secondary angle in copy: humour/friends banter that flips to private shame (shoelaces).
- Mechanism: only implied. Testosterone booster plus "reindeer organ superfood" / "arctic herbs" on the label. No named mechanism in the image.

## 5. Proof and Objection Handling
- No numbers, testimonials, or badges in the image.
- Pre-empted objection: "I'd have to give up beer." Answered by the headline's first clause.
- Proof in copy is first-person confession (age 48, friends' jokes), not results.
- Inference: market is skeptical that anything works without cutting alcohol, and sophisticated about diet/exercise advice.

## 6. Emotional Angle
- Primary: embarrassment masked by humour (the joke stings), then private frustration (can't tie shoes).
- Secondary: relief / permission.
- Imagery carries the identity (beer is lifestyle, warm pub tone). Copy carries the shame.

## 7. Visual Approach
- Photorealistic, appears AI-generated or heavily composited (the warped glass, perfect foam, glowing bottle).
- Warm amber bar setting, bokeh liquor bottles, wooden bar top. Beer and bottle share the same lighting so the product reads as belonging at the bar.
- Produced rather than native, but the pub setting feels familiar and non-clinical. Text sits inside the glass, so it reads as part of the scene, not an overlay.
- Bottle label is legible at small size; brand colours (orange/cream) pop against the amber.

## 8. CTA
- Implied only. Click-through to an emoji quiz page (low-friction quiz, per URL "emoji-quiz-reindeer-short"). No urgency or offer in the image.

## 9. Format
- 4:5 feed static. Text density very low (one 8-word headline). Reads with sound off by definition. Works in feed; the bottle label is small for stories.

## Hook Effectiveness Score: 8/10
- Visual metaphor lands instantly with zero reading.
- Headline is a question that names both the desire (beer) and the fear (belly) in 8 words.
- Loses points: the bottle placement reads as ad-obvious, no proof, and no mechanism visible. The question format is common in the supplement category.

## What Makes It Work
1. The glass-as-belly metaphor does the call-out visually. A man sees his own gut before he reads a word.
2. "Can't quit... but don't want..." is a permission frame. It validates his habit instead of shaming it, so he does not bounce.
3. Primary text opens with a joke (friends' banter) that earns attention, then pivots to the private moment (shoelaces). Image sets the topic, copy supplies the story.
4. Product sits next to the glass at equal weight, so beer and the fix are one scene. The solution is "alongside" beer, not "instead of" it.
5. 16 weeks of run time suggests the angle is converting.

## What Could Be Improved
1. No proof element. A single stat, badge, or review star would give the claim weight.
2. The "reindeer organ" mechanism is buried on the label. Surface it as a callout to separate from generic T-boosters.
3. No on-image CTA or offer. A small "Take the 60-second quiz" tag would pre-frame the click.
4. Bottle is small and the label hard to read at feed size. Scale it up or crop in.
5. Headline is a stock question. A sharper line (a specific scenario, a number) would build on the metaphor.
6. The image does not connect to the shoe-tying payoff in the copy. A visual tie would tighten the story.
