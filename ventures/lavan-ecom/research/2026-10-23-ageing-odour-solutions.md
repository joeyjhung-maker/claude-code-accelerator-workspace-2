# Ageing odour: what actually fixes it (skin, scalp, bedding, clothes)

Date: 2026-10-23
Related: [[2026-10-23-50plus-skin-mining]] [[research-playbook]] [[2026-10-23-supplier-trace]]

Scope: US 50+ market. "Older person smell" (greasy, musty, stale, "old man/old lady"), including the smell that stays in pillowcases, sheets and clothes. This file does not repeat the persimmon/nonenal verdict in the earlier file (unproven; see that file section 1).

- **Evidence scale used:**
  - Strong = several independent controlled studies or a clinical reference text agrees.
  - Moderate = at least one peer-reviewed study that tests the thing, or consistent expert guidance with a plausible mechanism.
  - Weak = mechanism or expert opinion only, tiny or uncontrolled studies, patents, or company-affiliated work.
  - Anecdotal = forum reports and brand claims only.
- **The honest headline:** nobody has run a good trial of "what removes older-person smell." The best evidence is on adjacent questions (fabric chemistry, wash temperature, armpit bacteria, medical causes). Most of the "fixes" are weak to anecdotal. That is itself a finding.

## 1. BOTTOM LINE: what works, ranked

Ranked by (evidence + how often people independently report it + how much it moves the smell). Effort is for the person (or the spouse/carer doing the laundry).

| # | Fix | Evidence | How it works (as best supported) | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Rule out a medical or medication cause first** (new or sudden change, ammonia/fruity/fishy/musty-sweet, or with weight loss, fever, tiredness, night sweats) | Strong that it is the correct first step (Mayo, Merck via forum quote, multiple dermatologists) | Diabetes, kidney, liver, thyroid, infection, Parkinson's, medications, TMAU and incontinence all change odour. Cosmetic fixes do nothing for these | One GP visit, blood and urine tests |
| 2 | **Stop wearing and sleeping in polyester / microfibre; use cotton (or similar natural fibre) next to the skin and as pillowcases** | Moderate (Alberta Textile Research Journal 2020, Ghent 2014 SEC) | Polyester is oil-loving, binds sweat/skin-oil odour compounds and releases them poorly in wash; cotton releases them far better. Micrococcus favoured polyester in the Ghent test | Low: swap pillowcases/sleepwear (cost only) |
| 3 | **Wash worn fabric hotter and more often: 60C where the care label allows, with a full-strength detergent, not cold/eco-only; change pillowcases every 2 to 3 days (weekly sheets)** | Moderate for temperature on odour in polyester (Auckland, SEC abstract), moderate for hygiene effect (Riley 2017 SEC), moderate-weak for the change schedule (dermatologists quoted in the press) | 60C removed the most odour compounds from polyester and fully reduced microbes in lab tests; 30C was better for cotton in the same study. Cold washes with liquid detergent leave more bacteria (press report SEC). Frequency matters because sebum re-oxidises on fabric | Medium: more laundry, hotter cycles |
| 4 | **Treat the trunk, not just the armpits: scalp, hairline, nape, behind ears, upper back, chest. Wash these on purpose, with a cleanser, and rinse well** | Weak to moderate (dermatologist opinion; no trial) | Dermatologists say the odour is more likely on flat areas: trunk, hairline, scalp. Sebum there oxidises. Forum user LSLGuy: hard to reach the middle of the back | Low to medium (tools help: long-handled brush or washcloth strap) |
| 5 | **Antibacterial wash on armpits, chest, back (benzoyl peroxide 5 to 10% wash, or 4% chlorhexidine)** | Weak. Mechanism plausible; **no peer-reviewed trial on ageing odour or armpit odour found.** One chlorhexidine-vs-BPO RCT (surgical prep, 50 men) found neither lowered Cutibacterium load. Baylor dermatology Q&A says BPO can reduce body odour over time (expert opinion SEC) | Kills skin bacteria that metabolise sweat and sebum. Ageing greasy smell may be less bacterial than armpit odour, so this may only help partly | Low: 1 to 2 min lather and rinse. Risks: dryness, irritation, and benzoyl peroxide can bleach towels/sheets (I did not find a source stating this, so treat as a to-check) |
| 6 | **Enzyme + oxygen-bleach laundry routine (presoak in a lipase/protease product, then wash)** | Weak. Lipase breaking down sebum is supported by detergent-industry research (Novozymes-linked microscopy study, patents SEC). **No independent study that tests it against ageing body odour.** One user (sonja92b) says only a presoak helped | Lipase turns oily sebum into water-soluble parts; protease handles protein; oxygen bleach lifts stains. Hot water can deactivate enzymes (forum claim) | Medium: hours of soak per load |
| 7 | **Deodorant/antiperspirant on the armpits** | Moderate for armpit sweat and odour (Mayo Clinic sweating and body odour page, SEC mirror). Not a fix for greasy back/head odour. One 9-person study suggests stopping antiperspirants changes the bacterial mix; effects on odour unclear | Antiperspirant blocks sweat (aluminium salts). Deodorant masks/changes bacteria | Very low |
| 8 | **Vinegar (1:1 with water) or isopropyl alcohol on armpits** | Anecdotal | Acidic or antiseptic effect on bacteria. A Mayo poster says a "Mayo doctor" suggested vinegar for deodorant allergy (second-hand) | Low; may irritate |
| 9 | **Oral and denture hygiene, hydration, incontinence care, medication review (for carers)** | Moderate that they matter for the specific odours they cause (dentures: stomatitis rates 7% with clean vs 54% with poor hygiene in a Danish nursing-home study SEC). Weak for "old person smell" itself | Mouth odour, urine, dry mouth and dehydration produce smells that people attribute to the body | Medium for carers |
| 10 | **Persimmon / green tea / polyphenol soaps** | Weak (one 20-man uncontrolled trial from the maker's institute; plausible mechanism; no independent trial). Full detail in earlier file | See earlier file | Low |
| 11 | **Diet changes (less red meat, more garlic, antioxidants), hydration** | Weak (small studies: 17 men, 42 men, women rating pad odour; antioxidant advice is expert opinion) | Diet changes sweat/skin volatiles slightly | Low |
| 12 | **Sun-drying, baking soda, vinegar in rinse, "laundry stripping," charcoal bags, air purifiers, mattress sprays** | Anecdotal (mostly blog and retailer content; users in the Mayo thread say several of these did not work) | Mixed | Low to medium |
| 13 | **Copper / silk / silver "antibacterial" pillowcases** | Anecdotal. Lab bacteria-kill numbers exist on brand pages; no independent study shows less smell in people | Treated fabric kills bacteria in a lab test | Cost only |

**Plain reading:** the best-supported moves are (1) see a doctor if it is new or sudden, (2) get away from polyester, (3) wash fabrics hotter and more often, (4) actually clean the trunk and scalp, (5) use an antiseptic wash on the trunk, in roughly that order. Almost nothing in the "miracle product" category has independent proof.

**Where users and evidence disagree:** users say the smell survives vinegar, baking soda, OxiClean and sunshine (sonja92b, trishbergmann). That fits the Alberta finding that polyester holds odour compounds in the fibre and the repeat-wash plateau. It does not prove any particular booster works.

## 2. EVIDENCE DETAIL, WITH SOURCES

### 2.1 Body washing and skin

**What the smell is (context)**
- Choudhary (University of Pittsburgh dermatologist): 2-nonenal forms when skin surface fats oxidise; may rise from middle age and plateau or vary a lot between people. Other factors: medications, chronic illness, reduced mobility, hygiene changes, living environment, diet, clothing and laundry. Her advice: "cleanse gently, wash clothes often, and moisturize." She says research does not confirm antioxidant skincare reduces 2-nonenal and no accepted treatment specifically reduces it. [Popular Science summary, FETCH, SEC](https://www.popsci.com/science/old-person-smell-science/) and [Yahoo Health, FETCH, SEC](https://health.yahoo.com/your-body/dermatology/articles/old-person-smell-real-heres-111603282.html)
- Danielle Reed (Monell sensory scientist, same Yahoo piece): the compound "loves to stick to your skin and it loves to stick to fabrics"; washing removes some but does not stop production. [FETCH, SEC]
- Boakye (Bergen Dermatology, same piece): antioxidant cleansers and tannin products may help some people; persimmon has a plausible mechanism but large trials are lacking. Rating: weak.
- Rating on "the smell is generated continuously from oxidising skin oil": moderate (Haze 2001, see earlier file). Rating on "ordinary soap cannot remove it": no independent evidence.

**Antibacterial / antiseptic washes**
- Baylor College of Medicine dermatology blog says benzoyl peroxide is bactericidal and consistent use may reduce body odour over time; warns of dryness/irritation at higher strengths. Expert explainer, not a trial. [Baylor blog](https://blogs.bcm.edu/2023/08/30/pee-ew-getting-rid-of-body-odor-with-benzoyl-peroxide/) (page would not open for me; content from search summary, SEC). Rating: weak.
- Mona Gohara (Yale dermatologist) in a consumer article: use a 10% BPO wash in small amounts on the underarm area, small amount to avoid irritation; the article calls it "extremely irritating" for new users. [AOL, FETCH, SEC](https://www.aol.com/lifestyle/dermatologists-acne-wash-stops-underarm-154300469.html). Rating: weak.
- No peer-reviewed axillary odour trial of BPO wash found. A search for chlorhexidine odour trials found only: a 1968 four-subject hexachlorophene soap study (reduced bacteria and odour, but odour did not track bacterial reduction closely), an 87-person hidradenitis survey (64.4% of chlorhexidine users rated it helpful for body odour, self-reported), and patents. [1968 J Soc Cosmetic Chem](https://library.scconline.org/v019n09/61), [PMC9833445](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9833445/). Rating: weak.
- RCT of 4% chlorhexidine vs 10% BPO soap for home surgical prep (50 men, shoulder surgery, International Orthopaedics 2020): neither reduced Cutibacterium (skin surface 100% positive in both). Not about odour; a caution that washes may not clear bacteria as hoped. Abstract only. [Springer Medicine](https://www.springermedicine.com/incision/acne/randomized-controlled-trial-of-chlorhexidine-wash-versus-benzoyl/20757506)
- A 2014 nine-person study (Callewaert et al., Archives of Dermatological Research 306:701) found antiperspirant use shifted armpit bacteria toward Actinobacteria, called "unfavourable" for odour by the authors; each person's pattern was individual. [PubMed 25077920](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25077920/). Tiny study. Rating: weak.
- Clinical trial registered for a bacteriotherapy spray for underarm odour (67 people, crossover). No results found. [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03967470](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03967470)

**Acidic / low-pH cleansers**
- One dermatologist in a press snippet said odours are more likely on flat body areas (trunk, hairline, scalp) and "can be treated with an acidic body wash." I could not open the source page to confirm who said it or the wording. **Unverified.** Rating: anecdotal / unverified.
- Mirai's pH test strips and acidic claims are marketing.

**Exfoliation:** I found no source tying exfoliation to ageing-odour reduction. Rating: no evidence found. One Mayo poster (a young woman) scrubbed and exfoliated her whole body daily and still felt she smelled. [RAW]

**Shower frequency**
- Press and caregiver sources suggest 2 to 3 full showers a week for many older adults, with daily washing of armpits, groin, feet and skin folds. These are lifestyle/news pieces (Futura Sciences, DailyCaring); I could not verify the study they cite. [SEC](https://dailycaring.com/senior-bathing-whats-really-necessary), [SEC](https://www.futura-sciences.com/en/how-many-showers-a-week-do-you-really-need-to-stay-healthy-as-you-age_22380/). Rating: weak.
- Note the conflict: the smell people complain about happens despite daily showering (many quotes below). Fewer showers does not look like the cause in these accounts.

### 2.2 Scalp and hair

- Scalp odour comes from sweat, sebum and microbes; dandruff, seborrhoeic dermatitis, product build-up and infrequent washing are common non-serious causes. [Acibadem health library, SEC](https://acibademinternational.com/health-library/why-does-my-scalp-smell-here-is-what-the-evidence-says/). Rating: weak (hospital-network explainer).
- Wash frequency set by genes and hormones: most adults 2 to 3 washes a week; very oily scalps daily. "Scalp training" lacks evidence. [Acibadem, SEC](https://acibademinternational.com/blog/how-often-should-you-wash-your-hair-dermatologys-honest-answer/). Rating: weak.
- A Karger study abstract on shampoo wash frequency and scalp/hair exists ([Skin Appendage Disorders 7(3)](https://karger.com/sad/article-abstract/7/3/183/295193/The-Impact-of-Shampoo-Wash-Frequency-on-Scalp-and)); I did not open it.
- **I found no source that links scalp squalene oxidation to pillowcase smell directly.** The sebum-oxidises-to-yellow-stain mechanism appears only on mattress/pillow retailer blogs. Rating: anecdotal. Clarifying/anti-dandruff shampoo for this purpose: no evidence found. One Mayo poster suggested Listerine on the scalp; anecdotal and unverified.
- Seborrhoeic dermatitis / yeast (Malassezia): a dermatologist in the press snippet said a yeast infection is a reason to "certainly see a dermatologist." I could not confirm Malassezia as a pillowcase-smell cause from any source. Treat as a hypothesis.

### 2.3 Laundry and fabric

- **Polyester holds odour (Alberta).** Abdul-Bari and McQueen, Textile Research Journal 2020. Polyester and cotton knit were soiled with three odorants and laundered with several detergents. Odour compounds bound more strongly to polyester; washing removed them better from cotton; polyester "isn't easily releasing those sweaty-smelling compounds"; between 5 and 10 wash cycles there was no significant change in odorant extracted. The article gives no detergent advice; its only practical tip is to wear cotton. [University of Alberta Folio](https://www.ualberta.ca/en/folio/2020/07/research-reveals-why-its-hard-to-get-the-smell-out-of-polyester.html) [FETCH]. Rating: moderate (one peer-reviewed lab study).
- **Bacteria and polyester (Ghent, 2014).** News report: 26 volunteers wore cotton, polyester or blended shirts for a spinning class; a trained panel found polyester smelt worse after 28 hours; Micrococcus favoured polyester, Corynebacterium (the main armpit odour genus) was absent on clothes; Staphylococcus on both. Authors suspect surface properties or "funky juice" pooling between fibres. Small study. I could not open the NPR page; this is from a search summary. [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2014/09/05/346055067/stinky-t-shirt-bacteria-love-polyester-in-a-special-way) (SEC). Rating: moderate-weak.
- **Wash temperature (Auckland).** A University of Auckland thesis/record (title not retrieved) tested 20/30/40/60C on removal of odour compounds and microbes from polyester and cotton: odour (VOC) removal was better at 30C for cotton and at 60C for polyester; full microbial reduction at 60C; current wash standards consider only visible soil, not odour. Abstract via search summary only. [Auckland ResearchSpace](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/59047) (SEC). Rating: moderate-weak.
- **Hygiene wash (Riley et al., 2017, healthcare uniforms):** 40C gave a 3.5 log reduction of a 7.7 log inoculum; 60C removed it completely. Search summary only, SEC. A news item (BHG, citing an Antibiotics journal paper) says cold liquid-detergent washes encourage odour bacteria; I did not read the paper. [BHG](https://www.bhg.com.au/lifestyle/to-stop-musty-smelling-clothes-science-says-avoid-this-wash-cycle/) (SEC).
- **Washing machines transfer odour bacteria.** Callewaert et al., Frontiers in Microbiology 2015: in five household machines, living bacteria in the effluent were not lower than the influent; malodour-associated species were enriched on cotton after washing and moved between clothes and the machine's biofilm. [PMC4672060](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4672060/). Rating: moderate (peer-reviewed; small, cotton only). It explains why a "clean" wash can smell and why cold cycles may not reset it.
- **Enzymes.** Lipases break down triglycerides, the main part of sebum; newer lipases act in the wash, older ones mainly in drying. Patent literature notes lipase can cause residual malodour from released short-chain fatty acids. Sebum is "the major soil present on laundry" and unremoved fat holds particulate soil (patent text). A Novozymes-linked fluorescence microscopy study showed lipase converting lipid soil into more water-soluble parts. These are industry sources. [USPTO 5707950](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5707950), [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/understanding-how-enzymes-help-keep-clothes-clean). Rating: weak for body odour specifically. A forum poster warns hot water inactivates enzymes (anecdotal).
- **Oxygen bleach / sodium percarbonate:** no source tested it on sebum or ageing body odour. A pillowcase guide (housewifehowtos) says bleach does not cut grease. Rating: no evidence; anecdotal against, from sonja92b who tried OxiClean with no success.
- **Vinegar, ammonia, baking soda, borax:** no controlled evidence. Caregiver posts report mixed results (see section 3). Ammonia: not found in any source; skip.
- **Dryer heat:** a caregivers.com poster says if soiled clothes go into the dryer with a bad odour, "it gets baked in" (anecdotal, FETCH). Sun-drying is repeated on blogs; no study found.
- **Fabric softener** may coat fibres and trap odour (forum opinion); one source says it may mask rather than remove. Anecdotal.
- **How often to change bedding:** Dr Marisa Garshick (board-certified dermatologist, quoted by AOL) suggests pillowcases every 2 to 3 days for acne-prone skin; other experts say 2 to 3 times a week, once a week if no skin concerns; sheets weekly (Cleveland Clinic dermatologist quoted) or at least every 2 weeks (Alok Vij, Upworthy). Media quotes, not guidelines. [AOL](https://www.aol.com/youre-probably-not-changing-pillowcase-100000168.html), [Upworthy](https://www.upworthy.com/?p=2750), [SheKnows](https://www.sheknows.com/health-and-wellness/articles/2045929/how-often-should-you-change-your-sheets/). Rating: weak-moderate.
- **Fabric choice:** cotton favoured over polyester (Alberta). Linen: no data found. Silk: one ClinicalTrials.gov pilot (NCT00767104) on silk-like pillowcases and acne; no results found; nothing on smell. Copper: brand lab data only (99.9% bacteria reduction on treated fabric under AATCC 100 for one brand; same brand says copper offers no skincare benefit to users). [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00767104), [Coop copper page](https://coopsleepgoods.com/collections/copper-collection). Rating: anecdotal.
- **Antimicrobial textiles only solve part:** Alberta authors say odour comes from compounds fibres absorb, not just bacteria.

### 2.4 Mattress, pillow, room

- All sources I found are retailer blogs (Puffy, Wakefit, Tom's Guide): baking soda overnight then vacuum, 1:1 vinegar mist with full drying, sunlight, ventilation, and mattress/pillow protectors. A musty smell may mean mould, a different problem. No independent odour study. [Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/wellness/mattresses/5-reasons-why-your-mattress-and-pillows-smell-plus-how-to-deodorize-them), [Puffy](https://puffy.com/blogs/best-sleep/why-does-my-pillow-smell). Rating: anecdotal.
- One caregiver (zumapal) burned old mattresses and replaced carpet and "still have smells." [FETCH]. Humidity above 70% encouraging mould is from one India-based retailer. Rating: anecdotal.
- Mattress/pillow protectors are recommended by retailers; sebum soaking into a non-washable pillow is the logical reason. No data.

### 2.5 Diet, hydration, smoking, alcohol

- Red meat: Havlicek 2006, 17 men on meat or non-meat diet for 2 weeks, women rated odour; non-meat donors rated more attractive, more pleasant and less intense. A later study (Zuniga) found the reverse; a review suggests quantity matters. Rating: weak. (SEC summary of the studies, [Stirling repository](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/23059))
- Garlic: Fialova, Roberts and Havlicek 2016, 42 men, garlic-eating donors' odour rated more favourably. Rating: weak.
- Antioxidant foods and hydration: advised by Wesson (University of Florida, Fox News) and Choudhary; Choudhary says the effect of antioxidants on 2-nonenal is unconfirmed. Rating: weak/opinion. [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/health/old-people-smell-real-experts-say-heres-how-avoid-you-age.amp)
- Dehydration: one carer reports a sulfur breath smell that stopped after rehydration and mouth care (Elderlymomcare, caregivers.com, FETCH). Anecdotal.
- Smoking and alcohol: **no study on body odour found.** Merck Manual text quoted by a Mayo poster lists curry, garlic, onion, alcohol and drugs such as penicillin as causes of eccrine bromhidrosis (forum quote of the manual, not verified at source). Rating: anecdotal pending a Merck check.
- Medications: Wesson lists drugs for cholesterol, ADHD or depression as possible influences on metabolism and sweating (Fox, SEC). Rating: weak.
- Menopause/oestrogen: Wesson says falling oestrogen changes skin and scent and that HRT can help some odour changes with risks (Fox, SEC); Mayo poster gardeningjunkie credits an oestrogen vaginal ring for vaginal odour (anecdotal, personal).

### 2.6 Medical causes and when to see a doctor

- **Mayo Clinic sweating and body odour page** (SEC mirror): see a doctor if you suddenly sweat much more or less, sweating disrupts daily life, night sweats with no reason, or you notice a change in your body odour; doctor may order blood or urine tests for infection, diabetes, overactive thyroid. [Koc University mirror of Mayo content](https://www.kuh.ku.edu.tr/mayo-clinic-care-network/mayo-clinic-health-information-library/diseases-conditions/sweating-and-body-odor). Rating: strong (clinical reference; confirm on mayoclinic.org).
- Odour clues (health-news sources, SEC): fruity/sweet = poorly controlled diabetes (ketones, urgent if with confusion, vomiting, chest pain, trouble breathing); ammonia/urine = kidney disease; musty = liver disease; fishy = trimethylaminuria (rare inherited; diagnosed by urine test; managed with diet, mildly acidic soaps, sometimes antibiotics, charcoal). [Medical News Today on TMAU](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/trimethylaminuria), [Ubie health explainer](https://ubiehealth.com/doctors-note/persistent-body-odor-causes-remedies-medical-42-bo23e4). Rating: moderate. These pages are explainers, not primary clinical sources.
- **Parkinson's:** greasy/musky/sour odour reported (Mayo poster writerslife: husband diagnosed after two years of specialists; linked page from the Davis Phinney Foundation not opened). Anecdotal here; the sebum change in Parkinson's is documented in the literature but I did not pull a paper.
- Other flagged causes: hidradenitis suppurativa, fungal skin or nail infections, yeast, incontinence and urinary infection, dementia-related hygiene refusal, dental and denture problems, EPI/pancreatic problems and colitis (forum posters), dehydration, medication effects.
- **Dentures:** a Danish nursing-home study (1987) found denture stomatitis in 54% of residents with poor denture hygiene versus 7% with clean dentures; Turkish studies found 44% to 62% prevalence and low overnight removal (19.4% removed overnight in one). Odour was not measured directly. (SEC via search summary; abstracts.)
- Wesson also warns a sudden change in odour can signal a serious illness such as cancer or dementia (Fox, SEC). Wesson and Choudhary both say check whether the smell is real: perception varies.

### 3.4 What this tells us

- **The washing methods people try first (vinegar, baking soda, OxiClean, sunlight, Dawn) mostly fail on bedding.** Three separate spouses say so on one Mayo thread.

- **Several "fixes" are really stopping a different cause:** mildew, dementia-related hygiene refusal, dehydration, incontinence.
- I did not find any "this worked" quote about BPO wash from a 50+ person. I did not find any quote about silk or copper pillowcases working for smell.

## 5. GAPS AND PRODUCT OPPORTUNITIES (factual, one line each)

1. No one sells a **bedding-focused odour reset** (enzyme + oxygen booster + instructions for 60C wash + pillowcase rotation) aimed at 50+ spouses; the products that exist are sports detergent (Hex, WIN) or pet-stain enzymes (Nok-Out, Nature's Miracle).
2. Users report presoak wins are partial and take hours; a product that **shortens or standardises the presoak** (measured scoop, time, temperature) is not on the market that I found.
3. **Pillow-specific**: sebum sinks into pillow cores that cannot be hot washed; users throw pillows away ("I have to throw away sheets and pillows"). A washable or protector-based pillow system is mentioned only on retailer blogs.
4. **Cotton-only bedding sold on the odour problem**: the best-supported fabric finding (polyester holds odour) is not used in any ageing-odour product I found.
5. **Trunk and scalp wash**: dermatologists say odour sits on the trunk, hairline and scalp, yet products are sold as armpit deodorants or whole-body "deodorant" (Lume); a wash for scalp, nape, upper back, chest with a tool to reach the back (LSLGuy: "darn hard to wash the middle of your back") has no clear leader.
6. **Antiseptic wash for ordinary 50+ skin**: BPO and chlorhexidine are found by hack, with irritation warnings; a gentle low-strength antiseptic body wash for older skin, with directions, was not found.
7. **A doctor-triage step**: spouses write "doctors don't address it" and "I need to know how to tell him to go to a doctor." No brand pairs the product with a plain red-flag checklist (see section 6).
8. **The spouse/carer as buyer**: wives and daughters write most threads; the "how do I tell him" problem is open.
9. **Perfumed products dominate** (Febreze, Lume scents); a fragrance-free option is requested by users with allergies (gardeningjunkie, sweetpink recommends fragrance-free detergent).
10. **Incontinence-linked bedding and clothing odour** is a separate caregiver market with its own products, not examined here.
11. **No independent test exists.** An A/B sniff test (plain soap vs antiseptic vs persimmon; same laundry) has not been done (carried over from the earlier file). Anyone who ran it honestly would own the evidence gap.

## 6. WHEN IT IS MEDICAL: red flags to see a doctor

Sources: Mayo Clinic sweating and body odour page (mirror); Choudhary and Wesson (press); health explainers listed in 2.6. Confirm wording on mayoclinic.org before any consumer use.

See a doctor, and do not rely on washing or laundry, if the odour:
- **Is new or changes suddenly** (Mayo: "You notice a change in your body odor").
- **Comes with weight loss, fever, persistent tiredness, or night sweats** with no reason.
- **Smells fruity or sweet** (possible high ketones in diabetes; urgent care if there is confusion, vomiting, chest pain or trouble breathing).
- **Smells like ammonia or urine** (kidney) or **musty/sulfur** (liver; or dehydration, per one carer).
- **Smells fishy** (trimethylaminuria, rare; urine test).
- **Smells sour, musky or greasy together with tremor, stiffness, or movement or sleep changes** (Parkinson's reported by one Mayo poster; ask a doctor).
- **Is in skin folds with painful lumps or draining** (hidradenitis suppurativa) or **comes with red, scaly, itchy, sore or oozing skin** (infection, yeast, seborrhoeic dermatitis).
- **Follows a new medication** (cholesterol, depression, ADHD drugs named by Wesson; fish oil plus antidepressant mentioned by forum posters in the earlier file).
- **Is linked to incontinence, urinary symptoms, or dementia-related refusal to wash.**
- **Is in the mouth or on dentures, or the person has dry mouth or sore gums** (dental check; denture stomatitis is common in older adults).
- **Persists after 2 weeks of hot-washed cotton bedding, daily problem-area washing and an antiseptic wash** (this threshold is my practical suggestion, not from a source).

Also mentioned by sources: people with a spouse who notices a smell they cannot detect themselves; Wesson and Choudhary both say check whether the smell is real and not assume it is the person. Ask a second person, not only the spouse.
