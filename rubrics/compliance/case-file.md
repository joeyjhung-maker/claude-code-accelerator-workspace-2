# Compliance case file — what a violation looks like in real ads

*Examples the judge can point to. Short on purpose. Add a case only when it teaches a pattern

## Enforcement actions

### TruHeight (Vanilla Chip LLC) — FTC final order, 15 Jul 2026
- Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-approves-final-order-against-truheight-deceptive-unsubstantiated-advertising-supplements-kids
- What they did: supplement ads claimed height growth in kids and teens without adequate
  scientific evidence. Social profiles that looked like real users were bots. Reviews were
  written by staff and vendors. Free product or discounts were traded for 5-star reviews.
- Cost: $4 million judgment, $750,000 paid, plus a standing order on all future health claims
  and reviews.
- Pattern for us: a physical-change claim on a supplement, propped up by social proof. The
  social proof was treated as its own violation, not as support.

### FTC penalty-offense notices on substantiation — Apr 2023
- Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/04/ftc-warns-almost-700-marketing-companies-they-could-face-civil-penalties-if-they-cant-back-their
- About 670 companies, supplement marketers among them, were put on notice that unsupported
  health claims can now carry civil penalties.
- Pattern for us: "everyone in this niche says it" is not cover. Not checked: whether any
  particular brand received a notice.

### Health Research Laboratories / Whole Body Supplements — FTC order, 2022
- Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/06/ftc-finalizes-order-banning-deceptive-marketing-supplement-seller
- Supplements sold with heart-disease and diabetic-neuropathy claims. Order finalised in 2022.
  Details beyond that were not read on 2026-10-09.

## Worked examples from the FTC's own guidance (S-F1)

These aren't cases, but the FTC wrote them as "this is deceptive" illustrations, so they carry
weight.

| Pattern | FTC's example | What it means for a supplement ad |
|---|---|---|
| Dramatic testimonial plus small-print disclaimer | A "lost 16 pounds in eight weeks" before/after with fine print, where the real trial average was about four pounds | A testimonial number is an efficacy claim. "Results not typical" doesn't fix it. |
| Ingredient science sold as product science | Studies on single ingredients used to back the combined product | "Carnitine does X" doesn't prove the capsule does X. |
| "Clinically tested ingredient" | Reads to consumers as "this product is proven" | Same for "backed by science", lab imagery, bloodwork screenshots. |
| Traditional use | Herbal tea "used for centuries", acceptable only with a clear no-scientific-evidence line, and undone by a testimonial about fast relief | A history hook becomes an efficacy claim the moment the ad says it worked. |
| Traditional use for serious conditions | A folk remedy for tumours, deceptive even with a disclaimer | Never use tradition to imply treating a medical condition. |
| DSHEA disclaimer | An unsupported diabetes claim with a prominent disclaimer, still deceptive | The disclaimer is not a shield. |
| Implied disease claim | Product name plus pictures of sneezing people implies cold prevention | Symptom imagery or a symptom checklist implies the disease claim without naming it. |
| Before/after imagery | Implies the product improves the condition shown | The picture is a claim and needs the same proof. |
