## META — Advertising Standards

### S-M1 Health and Wellness
- URL: https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/
- Read: 2026-10-09. Page change log shows: 22 Jul 2026, 27 Dec 2024.
- Targeting: ads for dietary, health, weight loss or weight gain products must target 18+.
  Cosmetic products and procedures also 18+. General well-being (fitness, gyms), general food
  and protein products, non-permanent cosmetics, and dental products are exempt from the age rule.
- For weight loss / weight gain products and cosmetic products, ads can't contain statements of
  inferiority about physical appearance: negative terms, descriptions or questions that attack a
  person's appearance, specific body parts or hygiene.
- Weight loss / gain ads can't show a close-up of a body area with fat being pinched, or claim
  results come solely from a wearable product.
- No claims (even from health professionals) to cure, heal or eliminate these conditions. Meta
  calls the list exhaustive: **Diabetes, Herpes, Thyroid, Psoriasis, Ebola, Cancer, Autism,
  Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, Motor Neurone Disease, HIV.** Claims about treating or managing
  symptoms of these are outside this specific rule.
- No clickbait in a health or weight context: sensational language with exaggerated or extreme
  claims, or promises of specific outcomes within a set time frame without disclaimers or
  qualifiers.
- Allowed at 18+: dietary weight loss / gain supplements. The ad may show people using the
  product and its impact, and should clearly indicate the time taken to get noticeable results.
- Allowed at 18+: general cosmetic products and procedures showing before-and-after.
- Sexual and reproductive health: allowed at 18+ only when the focus is health and medical
  efficacy, not sexual pleasure or enhancement. Non-surgical genital enhancement products are
  not allowed.

### S-M2 Privacy violations and personal attributes
- URL: https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/
- Read: 2026-10-09. Change log shows: 26 Jun 2024.
- Ads can't assert or imply a personal attribute of the viewer, directly or indirectly. The list
  includes age, physical or mental health (including medical conditions), disability and
  vulnerable financial status.
- Ads can't imply the advertiser knows medical information about the viewer or their family.
- Allowed: "you / your" with no personal attribute attached. Passing reference to gender, age
  groups or age ranges. Describing the product or service. Public-service style information
  that doesn't assert the viewer has a condition.
- Meta's own pattern, health examples: naming the service or treatment is fine ("X counselling
  available", "New Y treatment available"). Asking or telling the viewer they have the condition
  is not ("Do you have X?", "X getting you down?").
- Meta's own pattern, age examples: "fight wrinkles like never before" passes. "Ready to upgrade
  your skin to look younger?" fails. The difference is whether the line is about the product or
  about the viewer's body.

### S-M3 Prohibited commercial practices
- URLs: https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/deceptive-content/prohibited-commercial-practices/
  and https://transparency.meta.com/policies/community-standards/prohibited-commercial-practices/
- Read: 2026-10-09.
- Bans deceptive tactics for commercial gain: deceptive pricing, unauthorised endorsements,
  celebrity or brand bait.
- Meta also acts on user-feedback signals: misleading or unreasonable pricing, product that
  differs from what was advertised, unauthorised charges, refund or return policies not honoured,
  subscriptions without clear disclosure. Persistent negative feedback can restrict the account.

### S-M4 Misinformation
- URL: https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/unacceptable-content/misinformation/
- Read: 2026-10-09. Change log shows: 18 Jul 2024.
- Ads can't contain content debunked by third-party fact-checkers. Repeat offenders can lose the
  ability to advertise.

### S-M5 Drugs and pharmaceuticals
- URL: https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drugs-pharmaceuticals/
- Read: 2026-10-09. Change log shows: 11 Jun 2024, 31 Oct 2024, 26 Feb 2025.
- No ads for unsafe substances, products or supplements, "as determined by Meta at its sole
  discretion". Prescription drugs need LegitScript certification and Meta authorisation.
  Supplements don't need LegitScript.
- Practical read: an ad that positions a supplement as a replacement for a prescription drug
  invites review under this policy.

### Unverified — third-party reports only (do NOT grade as fact)
These came from agency blogs on 2026-10-09 and conflict with each other. Treat as CHECK FIRST.
- That the 22 Jul 2026 update moved Meta to claim-by-claim review and stopped auto-rejecting
  before/after imagery for weight loss. Meta's own page text supports before/after for general

- That supplement ads must carry the "not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent" line in
  the ad copy. One blog says so. Meta's page doesn't.
- That an AI system flags before/after-style imagery paired with results claims.

## FTC — advertising law

### S-F1 Health Products Compliance Guidance (Dec 2022)
- URL: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance
- Read: 2026-10-09 (first ~100k characters of the page).
- **Net impression.** An ad is judged on what consumers take from it, not what the marketer
  meant. Words, product name, images and context all count. If an ad has more than one reasonable
  reading, every reading needs proof.
- **Proof comes first.** Health claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence before
  the ad runs. Randomised controlled human trials are the most reliable form. Animal and
  test-tube studies are supporting only. Anecdotes and testimonials are never enough.
- **The evidence must match the product.** Same ingredient, dose, form, population and outcome.
  Studies on individual ingredients don't automatically support a claim about the finished
  product. "Clinically tested ingredient" implies the product itself is proven.
- **No cherry-picking.** The whole body of evidence counts.
- **Establishment claims.** "Clinically proven", "scientists agree", lab coats and charts all
  imply a level of proof that must actually exist.
- **Testimonials.** A real, honest testimonial still needs backing: the advertiser must have
  evidence the product works for buyers the way it did for the endorser. A dramatic result with
  a "results not typical" line stays deceptive. Typical results must be stated prominently next
  to the quote.
- **Experts.** Must be real, qualified, and have actually evaluated the product. Paid or
  company-linked experts must disclose that.
- **Traditional use.** Needs the same product, dose and form as the tradition, plus a clear
  statement that there's no scientific evidence. Never for serious medical conditions.
- **Disclosures.** Must be hard to miss and can't contradict the claim. Fine print, links and
  vague qualifiers don't work.
- **The DSHEA disclaimer does not rescue a deceptive ad.**
- **"FDA approved" style claims** are deceptive unless literally true for that claim.

### S-F2 Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR 255, and FAQ
- URL: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking
- Read: 2026-10-09.
- An endorsement can't be used to make a claim the marketer couldn't legally make itself.
- If an endorser's results are better than buyers will generally get, the ad must make the
  generally expected results clear.
- Endorsers must have actually used the product. Relatives, employees and anyone paid or given
  product must disclose that.

### S-F3 Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule, 16 CFR 465
- URL: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers
- Read: 2026-10-09. In effect since 21 Oct 2024. Civil penalties for knowing violations.
- Bans fake or false consumer testimonials: a person who doesn't exist, didn't use the product,
  or whose experience is misrepresented.
- A review used in an ad becomes a testimonial and is covered.
- AI avatars aren't banned outright. An avatar delivering a testimonial is a violation when the
  underlying testimonial is fake or false, and can be deceptive under the FTC Act regardless.

### S-F4 Other FTC rules that show up in supplement ads (from general knowledge — verify before relying)
*Not re-read on 2026-10-09. Flag findings under these as CHECK FIRST and name the rule.*
- Made in USA Labeling Rule, 16 CFR 323: an unqualified "Made in USA" needs all or virtually
  all of the product, including ingredients, to be US-made.
- Pricing guides, 16 CFR 233: a crossed-out or "X% off" price must be measured against a real
  price the product was actually sold at.
- Guarantee guides, 16 CFR 239: "money-back guarantee" and "risk-free" must be honoured as the
  ad states them, with material conditions disclosed.
- Negative option / subscription rules: recurring billing must be clearly disclosed with
  consent before charging.

## FDA — supplement claims

### S-D1 Structure/function vs disease claims, 21 CFR 101.93
- URLs: https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/structurefunction-claims
  (content current as of 28 Mar 2024) and https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/101.93
- Read: 2026-10-09.
- A supplement may describe how a nutrient affects the normal structure or function of the body.
  Only a drug may claim to diagnose, treat, cure, mitigate or prevent a disease.
- "Disease" means damage to an organ, part, structure or system so it doesn't function properly,
  or a state of health leading to that.
- A statement is a disease claim if, explicitly or implicitly, it says the product:
  - affects a specific disease or class of diseases
  - affects the characteristic signs or symptoms of a disease, in medical or everyday words
  - affects an abnormal condition tied to a natural process (ageing included) when that
    condition is uncommon or can cause significant harm
  - is a substitute for a therapy for a disease
  - augments a drug or therapy
  - implies disease treatment through its name, pictures or citations
- Required label disclaimer for structure/function claims: the two-sentence FDA statement
  ("This statement has not been evaluated…" / "This product is not intended to diagnose, treat,
  cure, or prevent any disease."). FDA must be notified within 30 days of marketing the claim.
- FDA's reach is labeling, which includes the website and material that accompanies the
  product. The FTC covers the ads. Both apply the same disease/structure line in practice.

### S-D2 Testosterone context
- URL: https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-takes-step-forward-testosterone-therapy-men
- Seen in search results 2026-10-09, not read in full.
- Prescription testosterone is approved for specific forms of hypogonadism. Low testosterone,
  hypogonadism and gynecomastia are medical conditions. A supplement ad that says it fixes them
  is making a drug claim.
