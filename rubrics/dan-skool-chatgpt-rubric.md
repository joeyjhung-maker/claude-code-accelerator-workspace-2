## The hierarchy

Apply these in order. A later preference never overrides an earlier requirement.

1. **Truth:** no invented results, features, prices, dates, backstory, first-person experience or
   physical scene Dan could not have witnessed.
2. **The ask:** deliver the requested topic, audience, length, format and CTA.
3. **Selling power:** earn attention, make the idea easy to get, create desire or action.
4. **Dan's voice:** make it sound spoken, playful and confidently imperfect.
5. **Polish:** fix only what obstructs meaning. Never polish the life out of the copy.

## Standing structural rule: one sentence per line

Every finished post gets reformatted so each sentence sits on its own line (blank line between).
This is a mechanical reformat, not a content edit — it does not count against the 1-3 high-impact
edit budget and it never touches wording, jokes, fragments, or rough edges. It just breaks lines.

- A list of parallel items (five signals, three checks) stacks as ONE tight block with no blank
  lines between the items. Blank lines go before and after the block. (Joey, 2026-10-09.)
- An ellipsis-joined run of beats ("Pulled the address off Companies House… sent it out… and then
  nothing.") stays ONE line — the ellipsis marks a continuing breath, not a sentence break.
- A genuine full stop starts a new line, even for a short fragment ("Crickets.", "Never opened
  it.").
- A single sentence joined by an em dash or "and" stays one line if it reads as one breath — don't
  split a clause mid-thought just to shorten a line.
- Do this reformat every time, regardless of whether Mariobot's raw draft already did it —
  Mariobot does not reliably apply this on its own, especially when run with
  `--no-style-contract`.
- After reformatting, run a rhythm scan. Three or more consecutive sentences under roughly six
  words is a warning that “one sentence per line” has become machine-gun copy. Combine related
  thoughts into a fuller spoken sentence unless the short run is an intentional closing crescendo.

## Standing structural rule: no sign-off on Skool posts

Skool community posts do NOT end with a `Dan` sign-off (Joey, 2026-10-09). The post ends on its
last real line: the lesson, the question, the homework, or the shout out. Strip any "Dan",
"Rooting for ya, Dan" or similar that Mariobot adds. A P.S. can still follow the last line with no
name above it.

EMAILS keep the sign-off. When a Skool post is duplicated to email, the email version gets `Dan`
added back at the end.

Older specimens in this file's source list still show a `Dan` sign-off. That is history, not the
pattern to copy.

## The five-question judge

### 1. Would the opening stop the right person?

A strong Dan opening usually does one of these:

- names the desired outcome plainly: “Lumpy mail clients the EASY way”;
- states a sharp diagnosis: “Why ‘Me Me Me’ outreach bombs”;
- introduces a vivid character or enemy: Trevor, Keith, the Resistance Bear;
- opens a tiny recognisable scene that demonstrates the idea immediately.

Reject generic insight-coach openings, throat-clearing, abstract metaphors that take several lines
to connect, and questions used merely to manufacture curiosity. Mine the strongest phrase already
inside the body before inventing a clever headline.

### 2. Can the reader SEE the problem or payoff?

Prefer concrete micro-scenes over explanations:

- accidentally opening an Etsy shop;
- Gary from roofing receiving Sarah from accounting's letter;
- one trampoline-park owner hearing how to fill Saturday birthday-party slots;
- a package sitting on a desk instead of an email entering junk.

Use names, objects, jobs, places and moments. Hypothetical comedy is allowed when obviously
hypothetical. It must never masquerade as a real result or event.

### 3. Is there one clean sales spine?

The post should move through one simple chain:

**Hook or scene → problem/tension → named mechanism or useful insight → concrete reader payoff →
appropriate next action.**

Do not cram the whole offer into one post. Do not repeat the same conclusion in three forms.
If the mechanism is useful, name it plainly (for example, reciprocity) so the reader can reuse it.
Sell the outcome the reader wants; the tool, service or process is how they get it.

When teasing a tool through a philosophy post, teach the transferable principle first and reduce
the product to the step it makes faster or easier. Do not list every possible use case or feature.
A clean pattern is: **principle → simple steps → “you can do this now” → tease the step the tool
automates.** This lets the post deliver value today while creating desire for the product.

For a **gated lead-magnet teaser**, protect the information gap. Explain why the problem matters,
make the cost of getting it wrong concrete, name the asset, and tease the depth or number of parts.
Do not reveal the checklist labels, framework steps, answers, or mechanism the reader is meant to
request. If the teaser gives them the usable payload, the comment gate has lost its purpose.

When an outcome is not yet proven, use Dan's confident uncertainty (“My hunch is that results will
be WAY higher”) instead of presenting the expected lift as established fact.

### 4. Does it sound like Dan speaking quickly to the group?

Positive voice targets:

- Aussie conversational register: ya, 'em, peeps, kinda, wanna, somethin'.
- Cap “ya” at two uses per post. More than that makes Dan's voice feel forced; use normal “you” and “your” everywhere else.
- Short lines and deliberate line breaks that sound spoken aloud.
- Ellipses for pauses and pivots.
- CAPS on the words Dan would stress vocally.
- Cheeky exaggeration and playful characters: GAZLLION, Trevor, Keith, Resistance Bear.
- Slightly rough grammar, loose capitalization, doubled punctuation and stretched words when they
  add humanity: “reeeealll quick,” “Do not skip this step!!”
- Plain statements mixed with one memorable image or joke.

Do not correct purposeful roughness into formal English. Do not force every line into the same
short rhythm. Keep longer characterful lines when the voice earns the length.

### 5. Does it make the reader want something or do something?

Translate features into a lived result:

- faster → less time packing or prospecting;
- easier → hand over the information and get back to coffee dates;
- safer → fewer embarrassing mistakes and less account risk;
- narrower targeting → the prospect feels understood and takes the call.

Use an appropriate close:

- teaching: one confident instruction or “Make sense?”;
- poll: a clear reason to vote and mutually useful options;
- offer: one direct CTA with the real destination;
- engagement: a question that makes people think or reveals useful audience information.
- homework reply (Joey, 2026-10-09): give the reader a small task that APPLIES what the post just
  taught, so they understand it and start using it, then ask them to report below with their
  answers. Size it like a journal exercise: about 10 minutes, done where they are sitting, answered
  in the same sitting. Shape: "So take some time and think about [the thing]. / Then, answer
  below… / 👉🏻 [ONE pointed question whose answer is their homework]" (specimen: "What's your niche
  and "signal(s)" that will show if your prospects are in motion?"). The reader does the work and
  Dan promises nothing back.
  - It is NOT field work. "Go reach out to 10 people and then report back" is too much work and the
    turnaround is too long. If the task needs another person, a send, a call or a wait, it is the
    wrong size for this close.
  - This is different from a domino assignment, which sends them off to do something in the real
    world and report back days later. Homework is thinking and writing; a domino is doing.
  - Travis does this a lot. ONE tactic for communities, not a default: do not run it on
    back-to-back posts in the same room. Untested as of 2026-10-09, so it is an option, not a
    winner.
- identity close (Joey, 2026-10-09, A-pile email): turn the lesson into a name the reader can BE,
  set against the one they do not want to be, then tell them to be it ("A-pilers win the prize /
  B-pilers get the bin / Be an A-piler using what we're teaching in here :-)"). The nod to "what
  we're teaching in here" is as far as the selling goes. One specimen so far; an option, not a
  default.

- temperature poll (Joey, 2026-10-14, "Offer Goggles" audit-call post): tests interest before
  anything opens. Shape: "Now… / Before we block out the calendar for these… / May I get a temp
  check?" → ONE 👉🏻 question in plain, friendly words that the poll options answer ("Would you be
  down to hang for an hour and make some $$ together?") → real stakes as a light joke about going
  back to Dan's CURRENT work ("sending locked gifts", not the older whiskey bottles) → the poll
  widget, with a funny hot option and a funny opt-out. No 1-to-10 number when the poll widget is
  the temperature check. One specimen so far; an option, not a default.

Do not bolt on a question just because it is a Skool post.

**Standing CTA (Joey, 2026-09-28): every post ends with** "If you're ready to [outcome matching the
post's theme], comment "[KEYWORD]" and we'll get the deets to ya." The keyword is one themed word in
caps, personalised to the post. The line before it must flow into it. No video/link pushes unless
Joey asks for a click-driver. A post without this close FAILS the ask.

Scope note (2026-10-09): Joey ran two posts without this close on the day the Topa campaign
finished: the Jack Brandt proof post ("give value and goodwill now") and the signals post ("no CTA,
pure value post"). When Joey briefs a post as pure value or no CTA, that brief wins and the missing
keyword close is NOT a fail. Whether the keyword close is still the default outside a live campaign
has not been confirmed by Joey. Ask if unsure.

Third case (2026-10-16, LinkedIn-service NAP): briefed WITH the keyword close (AUTOPILOT); Joey cut it and
ended on "Just an idea for anyone who wants to try it out." That makes three seed/value posts in a row
without the keyword. For a NAP that seeds an idea, default to a soft close unless Joey asks for the keyword.

For practical advice, do not stop at the instruction. Name the bit the reader is reluctant to do,
then disarm it with the mechanism that makes it easier. Example: calling takes “some cojones,” BUT
gift recipients are among the easiest cold leads to call because the gift has already opened the
door. This emotional bridge is often what turns correct advice into advice somebody will follow.

A short FYI post may still use a functional headline. “Informal” means getting
to the point quickly; it does not automatically mean stripping away the post furniture Dan uses.

## How ChatGPT edits Mariobot

1. Read the raw draft once for momentum and personality before marking anything.
2. Check truth and explicit constraints first. These are hard gates.
3. Choose the **one to three changes** that would most improve attention, clarity, desire or voice.
4. Preserve strong jokes, strange phrases and human roughness—even when they break an old stylistic
   preference.
5. Rewrite locally where possible. Do a full rewrite only when the hook or sales spine is wrong.
6. Do not run the old `copy-rubric.md` or mechanical lint as an automatic style gate for Mariobot.
7. Reformat to one sentence per line (see the standing structural rule above) — always, as a
   final mechanical pass, separate from the 1-3 content edits above.
8. Do not narrate minor flaws to Joey. Show the strongest finished version. Mention only a hard
   factual uncertainty or a decision that genuinely needs him.

## Recurring failure modes to catch

- A safe but generic opener that could belong to any sales coach.
- Starting from the mechanism when the reader cares about the outcome.
- Explaining an abstract benefit without a concrete scene.
- Inventing specificity to make a line punchier.
- Overloading the brief until Mariobot has no room to be funny or surprising.
- Replacing lively copy with technically cleaner but flatter copy.
- Treating every fragment, list, ellipsis or grammatical rough edge as an error.
- Ending with a generic engagement question after the point has already landed.
- Nitpicking small wording while missing whether the copy actually sells.
- Copying a swipe's visible labels while missing each section's persuasive job. Map the argument,
  not just the nouns: if a swipe section pain-digs alternatives, discredits them, and contrasts a
  better path, the adaptation must do that same work in the new market.
- Giving away a gated asset's checklist or framework inside the teaser instead of preserving the
  information gap that makes the reader comment for access.
- Letting a clever self-referential callback become more memorable than the lesson. When Dan says
  readers do not need to copy his full-volume personality, a plain spoken line can land better than
  another Trevor/YUGE joke. Use a P.S. to broaden a narrow example across adjacent channels when
  that extension is genuinely useful rather than cramming it into the main spine.
- Ending a domino assignment without asking members to report back with the work. The report-back
  creates accountability and seeds future proof. Dan may add a strong qualified prediction in the
  P.S.; preserve it as personal conviction, but never harden it into a guarantee or invented stat.
- For serious mindset posts, forcing a playful voice tell into every paragraph can weaken the
  conviction. Use Dan’s roughness selectively, and prefer an aspirational identity/outcome headline
  over a merely descriptive threat headline when the post is meant to rally the room.
- Do not let a compressed, clever phrase make the final instruction less immediate. Prefer plain
  spoken actions with causal connective tissue: “So don’t blindly accept someone else’s diagnosis”
  is clearer and more natural than “Don’t inherit someone else’s diagnosis.”

- Missing the reluctance-disarm on a feared step. If a route asks the reader to CALL someone (or any
  step they dread), the next line must name the fear and flip it ("It takes balls but if you're
  hungry, this is the FASTEST way to book calls"). Joey added this by hand on 2026-09-28 even though
  the rule above already existed. Check for it explicitly.
- Dismissing step one to set up the "real magic". Credit it first ("That by itself is a huge
  gamechanger"), then escalate.

- Stopping at a benefit without cashing it out to the booked call. For LinkedIn/Topa posts, check
  that the payoff runs through to coffee dates, ideally via a "which means…" ladder that threads
  lumpy mail back in and names the follow-up asset (network PLUS enriched database). Joey has added
  this by hand twice (2026-09-28, 2026-09-29).

- BANNED phrases (hard fail, Joey 2026-09-29): "Fair question…" and "So yeah…". Cut or replace on
  sight, including in Mariobot drafts. Added 2026-10-14: "Fair enough" (Joey: "never say this
  again"). Just go to the next beat.

- A moral-of-the-story recap before the sign-off ("The point is simple. / Stop doing X. / Start
  doing Y. / One X beats ten Y every day of the week."). Joey cut the whole block on 2026-10-09 and
  ended on the action. If that closing line is the strongest plain statement of the idea, it is
  probably the HEADLINE: he moved it up top and replaced his own angle line with it. An angle line
  in a brief is the argument, not automatically the headline.

- Clean contrast pairs: "Not because X. / Because Y." and "That's not X. / That's Y." Joey rewrote
  both in one post (2026-10-09). His versions join the lines the way Dan talks ("And it's not
  usually… / But because…"), add an honest aside in brackets ("altho sometimes it can be"), and put
  the READER in the sentence ("You're not comin' in cold anymore").

- Slogan lines with no join. Lead a consequence with "Which means…" so it follows from the line
  before ("Which means the prospect feels seen instead of stalked").

- Filler intensifiers. Cut "absolutely", "genuinely", "literally", "honestly" on sight. Joey has
  now removed these from two pieces in one day (2026-10-09). The line is always stronger without.

- Stock copywriter phrases: "But here's the kicker…", "Think about that for a sec.", "changed the
  game". Joey swapped "here's the kicker" for the next thing that actually happens ("And before
  they can get their hands on it…") and cut the rest (2026-10-09). Use the next concrete beat as
  the transition.

- Introducing a borrowed idea (a mentor, a classic, a study) without saying what it gets the
  reader. Straight after the intro line, add the payoff ("And it's fantastic at helping us
  understand how to get our outreach OPENED"), THEN explain the idea.

- One-sided payoff on an outreach tactic. Joey showed both sides: "For THEM…" (what the prospect
  gets) then "And for US…" (what we get). Use it when the tactic could otherwise read as a trick.

- Vague source when the real one is known. Mariobot wrote "Something came up on a call recently";
  Joey named the real event ("On Tuesday Johnny and Joey held a bonus Q&A call for all the new TOPA
  users") and used it to plug the replay (2026-10-16). If the brief gives a real who/when, use it.

- Hiding the product on a post about USING the product. The brief said "Topa optional, one light
  mention"; Joey named TOPA as the vehicle in every step and pointed to the house asset (the 1-2-3
  handraiser) instead of "write the messages" (2026-10-16). For B2B Leads Lab posts about a Topa use
  case, the product is the how, not a pitch to hide.

- Killing a fear without saying WHY it feels true. Joey added "It may FEEL like everyone's doing the
  same thing just because you're in here. But our group is a TINY slice of the overall b2b pie"
  (2026-10-16). Name why the worry seems real, then shrink it. He also swapped a dry overlap stat for
  a scale image ("thousands of different niches… all over the world… millions of cities").

- A value post with no bridge. On the saturation post Joey kept the soft close, then used the P.S.
  to name the real hurdle ("isn't so much competing… but getting the ATTENTION of the biz owners you
  want") and invite people into B2B Leads Lab. Pattern for cross-posted NAPs: kill the false
  problem in the body, name the true problem in the P.S., point at the group that solves it.

- Narrating proof instead of showing it ("X said it best…", "His opinion:", "who has… said she…",
  "Then she saw… and said…"). Dan makes the claim; the quote stands bare with a short tag. Hard fail
  (Joey, 2026-10-08).

## Improvement loop

Whenever Joey supplies a final edit:

1. Save his version as the source of truth.
2. Diff it against the version he received.
3. Identify what changed in hook, scene, mechanism, payoff, humour and CTA—not merely grammar.
4. Add a rule here only when it repeats or clearly corrects a consequential miss.
5. Add the final piece to the specimen list when it introduces a new repeatable pattern.

The objective is fewer corrections from Joey over time, not a larger rubric.
