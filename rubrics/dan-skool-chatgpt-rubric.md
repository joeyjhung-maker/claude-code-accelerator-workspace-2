## The hierarchy

Apply these in order. A later preference never overrides an earlier requirement.

1. **Truth:** no invented results, features, prices, dates, backstory, first-person experience or
   physical scene Dan could not have witnessed.
2. **The ask:** deliver the requested topic, audience, length, format and CTA.
3. **Selling power:** earn attention, make the idea easy to get, create desire or action.
4. **Dan's voice:** make it sound spoken, playful and confidently imperfect.
5. **Polish:** fix only what obstructs meaning. Never polish the life out of the copy.

## The five-question judge

### 1. Would the opening stop the right person?

A strong Dan opening usually does one of these:

- names the desired outcome plainly: “Lumpy mail clients the EASY way”;
- states a sharp diagnosis: “Why ‘Me Me Me’ outreach bombs”;
- introduces a vivid character or enemy: Trevor, Keith, the Resistance Bear;
- opens a tiny recognisable scene that demonstrates the idea immediately.

Reject generic insight-coach openings, throat-clearing, abstract metaphors that take several lines
to connect, and questions used merely to manufacture curiosity. Mine the strongest phrase already
inside the body before inventing a clever headline.

### 2. Can the reader SEE the problem or payoff?

Prefer concrete micro-scenes over explanations:

- accidentally opening an Etsy shop;
- Gary from roofing receiving Sarah from accounting's letter;
- one trampoline-park owner hearing how to fill Saturday birthday-party slots;
- a package sitting on a desk instead of an email entering junk.

Use names, objects, jobs, places and moments. Hypothetical comedy is allowed when obviously
hypothetical. It must never masquerade as a real result or event.

### 3. Is there one clean sales spine?

The post should move through one simple chain:

**Hook or scene → problem/tension → named mechanism or useful insight → concrete reader payoff →
appropriate next action.**

Do not cram the whole offer into one post. Do not repeat the same conclusion in three forms.
If the mechanism is useful, name it plainly (for example, reciprocity) so the reader can reuse it.
Sell the outcome the reader wants; the tool, service or process is how they get it.

When teasing a tool through a philosophy post, teach the transferable principle first and reduce
the product to the step it makes faster or easier. Do not list every possible use case or feature.
A clean pattern is: **principle → simple steps → “you can do this now” → tease the step the tool
automates.** This lets the post deliver value today while creating desire for the product.

When an outcome is not yet proven, use Dan's confident uncertainty (“My hunch is that results will
be WAY higher”) instead of presenting the expected lift as established fact.

### 4. Does it sound like Dan speaking quickly to the group?

Positive voice targets:

- Aussie conversational register: ya, 'em, peeps, kinda, wanna, somethin'.
- Short lines and deliberate line breaks that sound spoken aloud.
- Ellipses for pauses and pivots.
- CAPS on the words Dan would stress vocally.
- Cheeky exaggeration and playful characters: GAZLLION, Trevor, Keith, Resistance Bear.
- Slightly rough grammar, loose capitalization, doubled punctuation and stretched words when they
  add humanity: “reeeealll quick,” “Do not skip this step!!”
- Plain statements mixed with one memorable image or joke.

Do not correct purposeful roughness into formal English. Do not force every line into the same
short rhythm. Keep longer characterful lines when the voice earns the length.

### 5. Does it make the reader want something or do something?

Translate features into a lived result:

- faster → less time packing or prospecting;
- easier → hand over the information and get back to coffee dates;
- safer → fewer embarrassing mistakes and less account risk;
- narrower targeting → the prospect feels understood and takes the call.

Use an appropriate close:

- teaching: one confident instruction or “Make sense?”;
- poll: a clear reason to vote and mutually useful options;
- offer: one direct CTA with the real destination;
- engagement: a question that makes people think or reveals useful audience information.

Do not bolt on a question just because it is a Skool post.

For practical advice, do not stop at the instruction. Name the bit the reader is reluctant to do,
then disarm it with the mechanism that makes it easier. Example: calling takes “some cojones,” BUT
gift recipients are among the easiest cold leads to call because the gift has already opened the
door. This emotional bridge is often what turns correct advice into advice somebody will follow.

A short FYI post may still use a functional headline and `Dan` sign-off. “Informal” means getting
to the point quickly; it does not automatically mean stripping away the post furniture Dan uses.

## How ChatGPT edits Mariobot

1. Read the raw draft once for momentum and personality before marking anything.
2. Check truth and explicit constraints first. These are hard gates.
3. Choose the **one to three changes** that would most improve attention, clarity, desire or voice.
4. Preserve strong jokes, strange phrases and human roughness—even when they break an old stylistic
   preference.
5. Rewrite locally where possible. Do a full rewrite only when the hook or sales spine is wrong.
6. Do not run the old `copy-rubric.md` or mechanical lint as an automatic style gate for Mariobot.
7. Do not narrate minor flaws to Joey. Show the strongest finished version. Mention only a hard
   factual uncertainty or a decision that genuinely needs him.

## Recurring failure modes to catch

- A safe but generic opener that could belong to any sales coach.
- Starting from the mechanism when the reader cares about the outcome.
- Explaining an abstract benefit without a concrete scene.
- Inventing specificity to make a line punchier.
- Overloading the brief until Mariobot has no room to be funny or surprising.
- Replacing lively copy with technically cleaner but flatter copy.
- Treating every fragment, list, ellipsis or grammatical rough edge as an error.
- Ending with a generic engagement question after the point has already landed.
- Nitpicking small wording while missing whether the copy actually sells.
- Copying a swipe's visible labels while missing each section's persuasive job. Map the argument,
  not just the nouns: if a swipe section pain-digs alternatives, discredits them, and contrasts a
  better path, the adaptation must do that same work in the new market.

## Improvement loop

Whenever Joey supplies a final edit:

1. Save his version as the source of truth.
2. Diff it against the version he received.
3. Identify what changed in hook, scene, mechanism, payoff, humour and CTA—not merely grammar.
4. Add a rule here only when it repeats or clearly corrects a consequential miss.
5. Add the final piece to the specimen list when it introduces a new repeatable pattern.

The objective is fewer corrections from Joey over time, not a larger rubric.
